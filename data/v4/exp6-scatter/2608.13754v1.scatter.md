# Explanation Multiplicity: Circuit-Level Interpretability

Evidence Does Not Survive Defensible Analytic Variation

Ajay Pravin Mahale

12 August 2026

###### Abstract

The EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk systems to file technical documentation describing how the system reaches its decisions. Mechanistic interpretability is the obvious source of such evidence, and circuit discovery is its most developed instrument. We ask whether that evidence survives the condition under which it would be relied upon: competent analysts use the same system and tool but different defensible settings.

We pre-registered a crossed grid of analytic axes, with every level taken from a published implementation, and mapped each discovered circuit through a deterministic claim map to a structured Annex IV statement. Across the specifications that produced claims, the derived statement changes across most specification pairs, and the modal claim commands less than half the space. The evidence fails a filability criterion at every tolerance a conformity assessment body would plausibly accept.

Standardising the most influential choice, the evaluation metric, does not rescue the filing. Removing circuit size from the claim entirely and holding it fixed still leaves instability above the pre-registered threshold. The underlying circuits are structurally near-disjoint and functionally uncorrelated, so the instability is not one mechanism described in different words.

We give a standalone filability protocol and report that a documented discovery objective does not execute on the library’s own canonical task. Whether the conclusion holds at scale is untested.

## Terminology and formalism

A specification is a tuple of analytic choices,
$s=(o,a,d,m,\tau,P,r,g)$, with $o$ the discovery objective, $a$ the ablation operator, $d$ the corruption distribution, $m$ the evaluation metric, $\tau$ the size threshold, $P$ the prompt variant, $r$ the seed and $g$ the granularity.

Circuit discovery returns $C(s)\subseteq E$, a subset of the model’s edge set. Circuits touch attention heads and MLPs. The graph used here is factorised, with separate query, key and value inputs.

For specifications $s_i$ and $s_j$, circuit instability is

$$
D(s_i,s_j)=1-\frac{|C_i\cap C_j|}{|C_i\cup C_j|}.
$$

$\bar J$ is the mean Jaccard similarity over unordered pairs.

Let $\varphi$ map a circuit to a structured Annex IV statement. Claim instability, or the flip rate, is the probability that distinct specifications yield different statements:

$$
F=1-\frac{\sum_c n_c(n_c-1)}{N(N-1)}.
$$

Here $n_c$ counts specifications yielding claim class $c$, and $N$ is the number of specifications. This is the unbiased Gini–Simpson form: the probability that draws without replacement differ. It is unit-tested against the brute-force pairwise definition over randomised cases. The modal share is $\pi^*=\max_c n_c/N$.

For conditioning axes $G$, the within-group form is

$$
F_{\mathrm{within}}(G)
=1-\frac{\sum_g\sum_c n_{g,c}(n_{g,c}-1)}
{\sum_g n_g(n_g-1)}.
$$

This weights every pair equally rather than every group equally. With no conditioning axes, it reduces exactly to the unconditioned flip rate.

Evidence is filable at tolerance $\alpha$ if and only if $\pi^*\geq1-\alpha$. The ceiling on the flip rate is $F\leq1-1/k$ for $k$ claim classes. A flip rate must therefore be read against how many classes its map can produce; it is not near one by construction.

## Introduction

An audit means that auditors reach the same conclusion. That is not a definition borrowed from machine learning; it is what makes an audit worth commissioning.

The EU AI Act requires technical documentation about the general logic of high-risk AI systems and the measures needed to interpret their outputs. It also gives people subject to certain automated decisions a right to clear and meaningful explanations. Mechanistic interpretability offers to supply the technical content. Circuit discovery produces a subgraph said to explain a behaviour, and it is the part of the field with mature tooling.

If a provider files a circuit-level account of why its system behaved as it did, the filing is worth something only if another analyst, given the same system and tool, would file something compatible. We measure how far a regulatory claim moves across defensible analytic specifications and attach a decision rule a standards body could use.

We do not propose a better circuit discovery method or argue that interpretability is worthless. We argue that the evidence, as currently produced, does not meet the standard that filing it presumes.

Our contributions are a pre-registered multiverse of circuit discovery, a deterministic map to regulatory statements, and a filability criterion with a measured value on a canonical task. We test the instability under the most aggressive available controls: standardising any single axis, removing circuit size from the claim, and holding size fixed. We also show a general methodological hazard: when specifications select objects of different sizes, pooled statistics can invert. Several of our own statistics do.

## Related work

#### Non-identifiability

Méloux et al. [6] ask whether a unique explanation exists for a given behaviour under mechanistic interpretability’s own criteria, and answer that it need not. Our premise is theirs. Our contribution is downstream: we measure what non-identifiability costs when an explanation is filed rather than published.

#### Faithfulness metric sensitivity

Miller et al. [8] survey design choices in ablation-based faithfulness measurement. They find existing methods highly sensitive to seemingly insignificant changes and conclude that faithfulness scores reflect researchers’ methodological choices as well as circuits. They establish that an axis matters. We cross analytic axes and propagate the result to a regulatory statement. Their released library is our instrument, so this is a stress test of the tooling by its own definitions.

#### Structure versus function

Bayat Makou et al. [1] vary input statistics while holding the task fixed. They find structurally distinct circuits implementing the same computation, which they call phantom specialisation. This is the strongest objection to our design. If discovery merely samples an equivalence class of behaviourally identical subgraphs, a claim flip rate measures which member was sampled. We address the objection with a functional measurement rather than an argument.

#### Variance as estimation

Méloux et al. [7] treat mechanistic interpretability as statistical estimation and analyse estimator variance. Seed variance is an axis of our space. Their framing is the natural statistical companion to our treatment of the whole specification.

#### Auditability

Lan et al. [4] observe papers reaching conflicting conclusions about the same behaviour, followed by another finding both partly correct and incomparable. They call for auditing standards that allow certification in safety-critical settings. We answer with a measurement and criterion rather than a proposal. Sharkey et al. [10] list open problems, including the socio-technical ones touched here. Mueller et al. [9] argue that comparing methods requires standardised evaluation, adjacent to our argument that certification requires standardised specification.

#### Multiverse method

Our design follows Steegen et al. [13], who propose reporting across defensible data-processing choices, and Simonsohn et al. [12], whose specification curve we adapt. Simmons et al. [11] named the underlying problem: undisclosed analytic flexibility lets an analyst present almost anything as significant. Applying this psychology methods literature to machine learning evidence is not decorative. Explanation stability is exactly the kind of claim flexibility can manufacture.

#### Explanation metrics under optimisation pressure

Hsia et al. [3] show that sufficiency and comprehensiveness can be inflated without changing predictions or explanations. Both occur among our evaluation metrics. This is a reason to report discards by metric rather than silently pool them.

## Setup and analytic commitments

The model is GPT-2 small and the task is indirect object identification [15]. This choice is not an apology. The non-identifiability and faithfulness literature we extend works at this scale; comparability is the point. The edge count makes a size-matched random baseline meaningful.

Every level of every analytic axis comes from a published implementation. None was invented for this study. Discovery uses the edge attribution patching family [14] and integrated edge gradients, implemented in the library released with Miller et al. [8]. The automated circuit discovery lineage is Conmy et al. [2]. Corruption levels come from Wang et al. [15].

Corruption is nested within the operators that read it rather than crossed with every operator. Some operators never read the corrupt distribution, and crossing those would emit duplicate specifications. The seed selects which prompts are sampled: it measures evaluation-set variability, not nondeterminism.

The analysis plan was committed and tagged before the confirmatory sweep. Both abstracts were drafted in advance, so neither direction could be written up as a surprise. Deviations are recorded in an append-only file, with dates and whether each decision preceded or followed seeing the affected result. Outcome rules and thresholds were fixed and tagged before the sweep and never revisited.

The claim map is the paper’s most attackable choice. It was fixed in the pre-registration, implemented as deterministic code and published. It is not generated by a language model, which would add model variance to circuit variance without a way to separate them.

The map has addressees following the regulation rather than convenience. $\varphi_{\mathrm{overseer}}$ is keyed on dominant layer band, then size class, then ranked input segment. $\varphi_{\mathrm{affected}}$ leads with input segment because the right is owed to the person the decision concerns. Granularities are nested by construction: a finer claim refines rather than contradicts its coarser version.

The size bins were returned by a committed calibration rule from a measured node-count curve, not chosen to produce the reported flip rate. We evaluate alternative binnings as well as removing the size term.

No $p$-value is computed across specifications. They form a designed grid, not an independent sample [13, 12]. Uncertainty uses a nonparametric bootstrap resampling specifications, never pairs. $F$ and $\bar J$ are U-statistics over pairs, and pairs sharing a specification are dependent.

Functional agreement uses per-example correct or incorrect verdicts, with the measures of Bayat Makou et al. [1] reproduced verbatim. The random baseline draws size-matched circuits from the model’s full edge population.

## Results

### Circuits are almost disjoint

Mean overlap was computed exactly over all specification pairs rather than sampled. Distinct specifications sometimes hold the same circuit, making the exact computation tractable.

The distribution says more than the mean. The median pair shares very few edges; only a small fraction of pairs has high Jaccard similarity. Observed overlap is well above the analytic random line, $k/(2|E|-k)$, at the median circuit size. Beating chance at this scale is not evidence of much: expected random overlap is itself very small.

### Claims disagree across most pairs

The flip rate exceeds the pre-registered threshold by more than three times, with its interval lower bound also clearing the threshold. The modal share fails every pre-registered filability requirement.

The most common claims attribute the same model’s behaviour on the same task to early layers and to late layers. They are not different emphases. They contradict.

Every addressee–granularity combination rejects filability. Even the coarsest statement available to an affected person changes across a substantial fraction of pairs. The result is not an artefact of fine-grained claim language.

The committed size bins do not maximise instability. Several alternatives give higher flip rates, including halving every threshold. The result persists across substantially different binnings and when size is removed from the claim altogether.

### No analytic axis rescues the filing

The evaluation metric is the largest single lever, and it is not enough. Completely standardising it, which is more than a conformity procedure could demand, leaves the flip rate far above threshold, including its interval lower bound.

Structure and claims are governed by different axes. A REML variance decomposition on the logarithm of selected circuit size attributes most structural variance to the ablation–corruption interaction. The metric accounts for little structural variance even though it is the largest driver of claim variance. A standards body reading the structural decomposition would standardise the ablation operator and fix almost none of the filing instability.

There is no single knob. A procedure that standardises an axis and declares the problem solved would be mistaken by a wide margin.

### The result under maximum pressure

Remove every trace of circuit size from the claim, leaving only the dominant layer band, and hold circuit size fixed. The interval lower bound still exceeds the pre-registered threshold. Where in the model the behaviour is attributed continues to change across pairs when nothing about size can contribute.

### The random baseline

Pooled, the discovered multiverse is less stable than the size-matched random null, with disjoint intervals. The pre-registered hypothesis that the distributions would not be separated is therefore rejected.

We do not report that direction as a finding. It is an artefact of the size distribution. At large sizes, a uniform random draw touches every component and can produce only a single claim. Holding size fixed reverses the ordering at medium and coarse granularity. Discovered circuits are more stable than random ones once the comparison is fair. They carry information, but are still not stable enough to file.

The discovered multiverse also collapses to a single claim at its largest sizes. A substantial set of specifications therefore contributes no flips at all. The instability lives entirely below the stated size boundary.

### Filings and mechanisms

The pooled gap between claim instability and functional instability exceeds the pre-registered threshold. On that pre-registered quantity, the hypothesis is supported.

Raw functional agreement is almost entirely explained by circuits being right about the same fraction of examples, not the same examples. Cohen’s kappa remains low at every circuit size while raw agreement climbs with accuracy. This answers the phantom specialisation objection: discovery is not sampling behaviourally interchangeable subgraphs.

The gap does not survive holding size fixed. It fails the threshold at most sizes and is negative at half of them. At both ends of the range, mechanisms differ more than filings do. The pooled figure is carried by the intermediate size band alone. We report the pooled result because it was pre-registered, and the decomposition because the pooled result alone would mislead.

## The filability protocol

For a filed claim derived from a circuit, declare the specification space $\mathcal S$: the analytic axes and every level a competent analyst could defend, each supported by a citation.

Declare the claim map as deterministic, published code, not a language model. Compute the modal share over the declared space and the flip rate. Apply the filability criterion at the chosen tolerance.

Report the modal share, flip rate, discard rate per axis level and within-group decomposition of any pooled statistic.

A filing that cannot state its own modal share has not been audited. It has been asserted. The protocol does not require the regulator to adjudicate which analytic choice is correct. That makes it implementable: it requires only that the filer declare the space and report how much of it agrees.

## Caveats and limitations

### Scope

The study covers a single model and task, and says nothing at all about scale. Larger models have more components, allowing more room in both layer band and size class. The direction of the effect on the flip rate is not obvious. This is the limitation most likely to change the number.

A replication on Pythia-160m is pre-registered but not run. It belongs to a different architecture family and performs the same task. Its grid is identical on every axis except the seed axis, which has fewer levels. The plan, reduced grid and criteria distinguishing replication from refutation were committed and timestamped before any cell ran. It will be reported in a revision whichever direction it falls. Another task remains designed and not run.

### Exclusions and composition

Exclusion criteria were fixed before any circuit was seen. A discovery objective fails to execute: every cell in that arm raises a runtime error from mean squared error computed against an integral target inside the library’s own code. A digest over sorted failing cell identifiers verified that the failure set is exactly that arm and nothing else.

We did not repair it. The library is the instrument under test and is reproduced verbatim. This documented, publicly exported objective fails on its own canonical task. We report its discard rate and regard this as an observation about the maturity of tooling expected to support regulatory evidence. It is not the headline, or a criticism of a library whose authors are among those calling for the field to address exactly this [10].

The metric-relative threshold rule admits no circuit for many specifications. Discarding is high and uneven across metrics, making the surviving pool metric imbalanced. This was disclosed before the lock, using a validation slice. The rule was fixed in advance and no failing item repaired. We state the resulting composition rather than smooth or adjust it.

### Circuit size

Size is an outcome, not an axis: the threshold rule chooses it. It distorts the pooled flip rate, direction of the null comparison and sign of the functional gap. Fixing size reduces instability more than standardising any pre-registered axis. This is a general hazard for multiverse designs selecting objects of different sizes. We report every pooled statistic with its within-size decomposition.

These results do not say discovered circuits are no better than random, that filings differ where mechanisms do not, or that discovery dresses a single mechanism differently. Two of the unsupported readings were ones we held ourselves before the decompositions were run. Mechanisms differ substantially, and circuits are functionally uncorrelated.

### Width of the multiverse

The multiverse is as wide as the library, not the literature. Optimal ablation [5] is a defensible operator the instrument does not implement, so it never entered the space. The measured flip rate is therefore a lower bound with respect to the field’s full set of defensible choices.

### Uncertainty

Intervals quantify uncertainty given the grid and license nothing beyond it.

The bootstrap is invalid for a reported family with very small conditioning groups. Retained self-pairs dominate and make the intervals unusable. Those quantities are point estimates with the diagnostic attached. We did not construct a bias-corrected estimator after seeing the naive intervals disagree: choosing an estimator in response to a result is the behaviour this paper documents.

## Discussion and reproducibility

The regulatory lesson is not that interpretability evidence is worthless. Its evidentiary weight is measurable rather than assumed, and measuring it is cheap relative to the sweep that produced the circuit.

The pre-registration is a timestamped public-repository commit tagged `prereg-p1-confirmatory`, made before the confirmatory sweep. Every reported number traces to a config, seed and environment fingerprint. The sweep used a single environment, verified from the results archive rather than asserted. The code commit was recovered by content-addressed comparison of the uploaded archive against the git tree. The analysis layer runs without a GPU.

A verification script recomputes headline quantities from raw results and asserts against the reported values, deriving the flip rate by independent routes including brute-force pairwise computation. Another script checks that every numeric manuscript token matches a value derived from stored results. Every table is emitted directly from those results rather than transcribed.
