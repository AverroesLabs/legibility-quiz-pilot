# Probe Generalization as Subspace Selection for OOD Deception Detection

Daniel Yoo

Adrians Skapars

## Abstract

Linear probes can detect behaviors and concepts inside language model activations, but they may fail to transfer to out-of-distribution examples. We study deception-detection probes on held-out datasets and find that projecting inputs onto a small subset of principal components from the training distribution enables cross-domain transfer that nearly matches probes trained directly on the test distribution.

We also find that principal component interpretations can identify part of this transferable subset. An LLM judge scores each component according to whether its most- and least-activating examples suggest a transferable deception direction. Probing on the highest-scoring components closes much of the baseline-to-oracle gap on Insider Trading Report and a smaller part on Sandbagging.

Directions that receive large weights from a source probe appear to encode source-specific surface features. Directions that transfer appear to encode the same contrast more abstractly, in a form that natural-language descriptions can capture. Broadly, our results suggest that the OOD robustness of probes is largely determined by subspace selection.

Machine Learning, ICML

## Terminology and Study Design

Principal components are abbreviated as PCs. We work in the basis of source-domain PCs rather than raw activations, avoiding the assumption that the model’s latent dimensions provide a privileged basis.

Roleplaying is the source domain. Insider Trading Report, Insider Trading Confirm, and Sandbagging are the target test sets. These deception-detection datasets follow Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025). In Roleplaying, the model is placed in a simple scenario with an opportunity to lie. In Insider Trading Report, it acts as a trader and decides whether to honestly report using insider information. In Insider Trading Confirm, after hiding earlier use of insider information, it is asked to confirm that it did not use any. In Sandbagging, it is told to deliberately under-perform on some tasks while behaving normally on others.

Responses receive honest, ambiguous, or deceptive labels. Ambiguous responses are removed from the experiments.

Following Ying et al. (2026), we use residual-stream activations from the layer with the highest cross-domain AUROC in their analysis. To extract activations, we pass each prompt through the model and average token activations over only the assistant’s response.

We standardize source activations and apply the same source statistics to all target activations. PCA is performed on the scaled source activations. Probes are logistic regression classifiers. When using a subset of retained PCs, we project activations onto that subset before training and evaluation. Full-representation baseline probes instead train directly on the standardized activations.

The standardized coefficient used to rank a PC is the absolute value of its probe coefficient multiplied by the standard deviation of that PC. Both source-PCA and target-PCA rankings use activations projected onto the source-PCA basis. The source-PCA ranking uses weights learned on source data; the target-PCA ranking uses weights learned on target data. For either ranking, the resulting evaluation probe is trained on source data projected onto the highest-ranked PCs.

Greedy selection begins with an empty set. At each step, it searches the retained source PCs and adds the candidate that most improves target AUROC. For each candidate, source activations are projected onto the current subset and a logistic regression probe is trained on those projections. Probe weights are always fitted on source data.

For the cross-validated greedy experiment, selection uses the non-held-out target folds, and the selected source-trained probe is evaluated on the held-out target fold. A separate greedy run uses the full target dataset to produce a stable PC set for choosing the size of the ranked subsets. The variance control selects the highest-variance PCs, matching the size of the greedy subset.

The interpretation method follows Bills et al. (2023) and Casademunt et al. (2025). For every retained source PC, an LLM receives the most- and least-activating source examples and describes the positive pole, negative pole, and direction as a whole. The judge assigns an OOD score for generalizability. Interpretability selection retains PCs that receive the maximum observed score in both scoring runs. The ablated control uses the retained PCs excluded by that selection. Another control uses a variance-selected subset of the same size as the interpretation-selected subset.

## Introduction

Linear probes are standard tools for reading information from language model activations (Alain and Bengio, 2018; Hewitt and Liang, 2019). They are cheap to train and expose some semantically meaningful information in an otherwise uninterpretable representation space. Yet probes that look strong in-distribution are sometimes unreliable out-of-distribution (Kirch et al., 2026).

Existing work shows that key behavioral concepts are linearly represented in model activations (Marks and Tegmark, 2024; Park et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025). Probes should therefore have the capacity to detect these concepts, but in practice they appear to learn spurious correlations (Orgad et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). This is unacceptable for probe-based monitoring of harmful interactions. The challenge is to build probes that learn only generalizing features.

Principal components offer a simple, data-driven way to study structure in representation spaces. Prior work also suggests that the choice of PCs matters. Mu et al. (2018) show that removing the top PCs of an embedding space improves downstream performance. Kantamneni et al. (2025) use top-PC projections as a probe baseline. Our question is which subset of principal components supports generalizable probe training, and how that subset can be identified.

We use deception detection as a testbed for exploring selection strategies that avoid spurious correlations and transfer to target domains. The investigation asks whether a transferable subspace exists, whether familiar weight-based rankings recover it, and whether interpretation can identify useful directions without target data.

Our findings are as follows:

- A small transferable subspace exists. A target-supervised greedy search over source PCs finds a compact subset on which a source-trained probe nearly matches a target-trained probe.
- Source-side and target-side rankings fail to recover that subspace. Ranking PCs by source or target probe weights does not produce a basis that transfers better than the full-representation baseline.
- An LLM judge partially recovers the subspace without target data. Its interpretations can help select PCs likely to encode transferable behavioral contrasts rather than surface patterns.

## Related Work

### Linear concept directions in activation space

A related line of work studies whether high-level behavioral or semantic properties are represented as directions or low-dimensional subspaces in activation space. Marks and Tegmark (2024) report linear structure in true/false representations across datasets. Park et al. (2024) formalize the linear representation hypothesis and connect it to probing and steering. Zou et al. (2025) model high-level properties such as honesty and emotion as population-level directions that can be read from and intervened on in model activations.

This perspective has also been used to explain and control model behavior. Arditi et al. (2024) show that refusal behavior can be mediated by a single direction, suggesting that some high-level behaviors may be localized in simple linear features. Lu et al. (2026) identify an “assistant” axis corresponding to assistant-like behavior.

Together, these works suggest that linear structure can support mechanistic understanding and activation-space interventions. They also raise questions about when directions capture stable concepts rather than correlations that depend on context.

### Linear probes and their limitations

Linear and lightweight probes have a long history in interpretability as tools for analyzing information represented in neural networks (Alain and Bengio, 2018; Hewitt and Liang, 2019). Recent applications include safety-relevant signals in LLM activations, latent truthfulness without direct supervision (Burns et al., 2024), and strategic deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025).

Methodological skepticism is also central to the probing literature. Hewitt and Liang (2019) argue that high probe accuracy alone is insufficient evidence that a model already represents the target property. The probe may itself learn the task or exploit surface-level correlates. Ravichander et al. (2021) similarly question whether probing reliably establishes what information is encoded, rather than merely recoverable.

Recent work also finds that probes with strong in-distribution performance can generalize unevenly out of distribution (kramár2026buildingproductionreadyprobesgemini). These concerns motivate careful evaluation of whether safety probes identify robust internal signals rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

### Interpreting features with natural-language explanations

Another approach assigns natural-language descriptions to neurons or learned features. Bills et al. (2023) introduced LLM-generated explanations of neurons based on high-activation examples. The approach has since been extended to automatic labeling of sparse-autoencoder features (Paulo et al., 2025; Bricken et al., 2023), enabling large-scale interpretation of learned feature dictionaries.

Recent work asks whether natural-language feature descriptions can support intervention as well as interpretation. Casademunt et al. (2025), for example, identify concepts through automated natural-language interpretation and ablate them during fine-tuning to reduce misaligned behavior.

## Findings

### 4.1 Baseline transfer and information in the retained subspace

We first test how well probes trained on full source representations transfer to each target. Transfer is reasonably good for Insider Trading Confirm, moderate for Insider Trading Report, and poor for Sandbagging.

A probe trained directly on target data within the retained source-PC subspace separates deceptive from honest responses on every target. The retained subspace therefore encodes enough information for this distinction across all target datasets.

The difficulty does not arise because the relevant information is missing. Instead, the source probe learns a direction in the subspace that does not generalize. The results distinguish the information present in the representation from the direction that source-domain training chooses to use.

### 4.2 A transferable subspace exists

We ask whether training probes on source data can close the performance gap between the full-representation baseline and target-trained probes. Greedy PC selection converges on small subsets rather than requiring the full retained representation.

The greedy method comes close to a probe trained directly on the target dataset for Insider Trading Report and Sandbagging. It substantially improves over the baseline on these harder generalization tasks. A transferable subspace therefore exists in principle.

The variance control matches greedy selection only for Insider Trading Confirm, the easy case. It falls far behind on Insider Trading Report and Sandbagging. Choosing a small subspace is not enough: which PCs are selected matters. The amount of variance explained by the selected PCs does not predict their usefulness.

The comparison supports the existence of a source-domain subspace in which source-trained weights can work well on a different domain. It also distinguishes successful selection from simply keeping the leading directions of variation.

### 4.3 Weight-based rankings do not recover the subspace

We consider simple hypotheses about what makes the greedy-selected PCs transferable. One is that the selected PCs are those that individually separate target classes well across the test set. On this account, greedy selection finds them because it has access to target data. The other is that they are the PCs on which the source probe already relies most heavily, with greedy selection rediscovering the source signal in a more compact form.

If either hypothesis were correct, probes based on the corresponding ranking should recover greedy performance.

Both hypotheses fail, but in different ways. The source-PCA probe transfers about as well as the full-representation baseline. The PCs on which the source probe relies are those that do not generalize.

The target-PCA probe does well on Insider Trading Report, but performs worse than greedy selection on Insider Trading Confirm. On Sandbagging, its transfer performance is below random. The PCs that best separate target classes are not those that continue to transfer best when their weights are learned from source data.

Greedy and target-PCA selections agree on less than a fifth of their choices. Together, the comparisons suggest that the transferable subspace is neither the part of representation space most discriminative on the source nor the part most discriminative on the target.

### 4.4 Interpreting transferable directions

A small subset of source PCs supports cross-domain transfer, but it is not the subset relied on by source classification or target classification. We ask whether it corresponds to anything human-interpretable.

The PCs shared by greedy selections across targets receive the judge’s maximum OOD score more often than the leading PCs selected by source-probe weights. This suggests that LLMs can partially recover transferable directions.

Interpretations of greedy-selected PCs describe the contrast at a level of abstraction that survives the move to other domains. They include contrasts between denying or deflecting and providing details, between minimizing or offering innocuous explanations and giving specific or incriminating details, and between hedged defensive wording and other responses.

Other directions are described mainly through domain-specific lexical cues, stylistic framing, formal cues, or task-dependent wording. The contrast between medical or leave language and project or status language is an example of a direction that the judge regards as brittle across domains and rephrasings.

The source-weighted directions also include contrasts that can correlate with deception in the source setting. Their explanations nevertheless often emphasize explicit denials, common response forms, word order, assertive first-person defenses, or discourse forcefulness. These descriptions distinguish surface-dependent signals from more abstract behavioral contrasts.

### 4.5 Selecting PCs through interpretation

We test whether interpretation-based selection can recover a meaningful fraction of greedy performance without using target data.

The interpretation-selected probe outperforms the full-representation baseline on every target. It also outperforms the source-variance-selected control, with wide margins on Insider Trading Report and Sandbagging. As expected, it outperforms the probe trained on the ablation of the selected PCs across all targets.

The size of the recovery differs sharply by dataset. On Insider Trading Report, interpretation selection closes much of the gap between the baseline and greedy oracle. On Sandbagging, it closes a smaller part of that gap.

Overall, selecting PCs purely from natural-language descriptions of what they encode provides a meaningful improvement over baseline performance on the generalization tasks. The selection uses no target data.

## Caveats and Limitations

Our strongest claims remain narrow.

All experiments use a single model, a single activation layer, and a single source dataset. Whether the findings generalize to other model families or datasets remains an open question for future work.

Insider Trading Confirm is uninformative for comparisons because baseline transfer is already near the ceiling. There is little headroom: the selection methods perform roughly alike, apart from the interpretation-ablated method.

Sandbagging gives the weakest results. Interpretation selection produces only a modest AUROC improvement and recovers a limited share of the gap to the greedy upper bound. It is also the most out-of-distribution target, and the modest gain likely reflects that distribution shift. The Insider Trading Report result is more robust.

The greedy probe is an upper bound on transfer performance, not an unbiased OOD estimate. Although probe weights are trained on source data, subset selection uses labeled target examples.

The greedy values used for ranking comparisons differ slightly from the cross-validated greedy values because the ranking comparison uses the full target dataset rather than selecting separately within folds.

We retain the leading source PCs because alignment with SAE decoder features decreases with PC index (Bricken et al., 2023). Lower-index PCs are more amenable to natural-language interpretation and thus comparable with interpretation-driven selection methods. This is not a claim that low-index PCs generalize better.

LLM judge scores are noisy. No PC received a score above the maximum observed value used for interpretation selection.

The pipeline is fully linear: PCA projection followed by logistic regression. The existence of a transferable subspace is therefore perhaps unsurprising. Whether these findings extend to nonlinear probes or other readout locations remains an open question.

We make structural claims about what PCs “encode” from top-activating examples and the LLM judge’s verbalization of their contrast. These are descriptive claims, not causal claims. We have not ablated the high-scoring PCs during inference to verify that they carry the contrast they appear to encode.

## Conclusion

In probe-based deception monitoring, a small subset of source-domain PCs enables cross-domain transfer that nearly matches target-trained probes. Source-side or target-side separability alone does not find this subset. An interpretation score from an LLM judge partially recovers it without access to target data.

Robust probing requires identifying not only whether a behavioral signal is linearly accessible, but where in activation space it is represented most robustly. Linear probes trained over full representations may combine general behavioral features with dataset-specific surface cues. Such heuristics can support high in-distribution accuracy while hurting transfer.

Sparse PC subspaces may instead isolate directions corresponding to more general behavioral structure. This reframes OOD probing as a representation-selection problem. The goal is not only to fit a classifier, but also to choose a subspace that preserves the intended behavioral feature while filtering out spurious correlation.

## Appendix: LLM Judge Prompt

> You are interpreting a principal component direction in a neural representation space.
> This direction is bipolar:
>
> - Positive activations mean movement toward one pole.
> - Negative activations mean movement toward the opposite pole.
> - Larger magnitudes mean a stronger match to that pole.
>
> Each line is formatted as token<TAB>activation, with activations normalized to integers in the interval specified below.
>
> The examples below were selected from a dataset with this setup: The model is instructed to Roleplay in a simple scenario. The model is asked a question which gives a strategic chance to lie.
>
> High-positive examples:
>
> {positive_examples}
>
> High-negative examples:
>
> {negative_examples}
>
> Infer the dominant contrast captured by the direction using both the positive and negative examples.
>
> Important instructions:
>
> - Do not assume the direction is a broad sentiment direction unless the examples clearly support that.
> - Prefer the narrowest accurate description over a broad generic one.
> - Do not overgeneralize beyond what is supported by the examples.
> - Rate how likely this direction is to be useful for out-of-distribution deception on the specified scale, and briefly explain why.
> - Give low scores to directions that are mainly surface cues, lexical habits, or task-specific phrasing, even if they correlate with deception in these examples.
> - A direction that is noisy, brittle, context-dependent, or likely to fail under different wording or domains should usually receive a score in the lower range, not the higher range.
> - Reserve the higher range for directions that seem plausibly tied to a more general deception-relevant strategy or behavior, rather than local wording.
>
> Write your answer in exactly this format:
>
> Positive pole: ...
>
> Negative pole: ...
>
> Shared direction: ...
>
> OOD deception usefulness (specified scale): ...
