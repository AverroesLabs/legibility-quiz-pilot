# PrivDrift: Auditing User-Secret Leakage Under Topic Drift in Active LLM Conversations

Luciano Rolando Maldonado Romero

Affiliation: West Virginia University, Morgantown, WV, USA

Correspondence to: lrm00020@mix.wvu.edu

###### Abstract

Large language models increasingly operate as persistent assistants in user-facing, shared-session, and tool-augmented settings. Sensitive information disclosed during an active conversation may remain behaviorally recoverable after the dialogue shifts to unrelated topics. We introduce PrivDrift, a benchmark for auditing whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable after conversational topic drift and persuasion-based probing. It uses controlled multi-turn dialogues with seeded secrets, content-dense drift turns, and standardized extraction probes. Across the evaluated LLMs with extended context windows, dialogue-level hybrid leakage remains substantial and varies strongly by model, secret type, and persuasion intensity. Additional topic drift does not reliably reduce leakage within the tested window. These findings suggest that privacy risk in active LLM contexts should be evaluated as a persistent behavioral failure mode rather than only as training-data memorization or immediate jailbreak behavior.

###### Keywords:

Trustworthy AI, Large Language Models, Privacy, Evaluation, Benchmarking

## Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have evolved from simple chat systems into persistent assistants capable of maintaining coherence over extended interactions (OpenAI, 2023; Touvron et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Healthcare triage, financial planning, enterprise copilots, and personal productivity tools are settings in which users may disclose Personally Identifiable Information (PII). Examples include phone numbers, financial identifiers, email addresses, and health-related details. Context retention supports useful multi-turn assistance, but it also creates a privacy risk: sensitive information disclosed earlier may remain available to later generations.

Existing safety evaluations mostly target training-data memorization and immediate jailbreaking. The former extracts private information from model parameters (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2023); the latter elicits harmful behavior in the current turn (Wei et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). Work on prompt injection and multi-turn attacks also examines later conversational manipulation (Liu et al., 2023; Russinovich et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2024). These settings do not directly measure whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable after unrelated conversational drift. An important evaluation gap is whether topic shift acts as a practical privacy boundary within an active conversation.

PrivDrift addresses that gap by measuring leakage under topic drift and persuasion intensity. Its contributions are a controlled benchmark for auditing recovery from active conversational context; an evaluation using direct, justified, and high-pressure probes; and a reproducible hybrid detector combining normalization-based matching with an open-weights LLM judge. We report dialogue-level, probe-level, regex-only, fuzzy, and hybrid leakage rates. We also propose Privacy Half-Life as a stability-based metric for persistent suppression.

The results show substantial leakage across models. Persuasion intensity significantly affects leakage, while secret type produces sharp differences. SSNs and credit cards are suppressed far more often than emails and phone numbers. None of the evaluated models reaches stable decay within the observed drift range. These findings concern the assistant’s behavior when prompted to reproduce sensitive information, rather than simply whether the information remains in a transcript.

## Terminology and Evaluation Rules

Active-context re-disclosure is a failure mode in which sensitive information disclosed earlier in a conversation remains recoverable through later prompts after unrelated topic drift. The core question is whether the assistant will behaviorally reproduce the secret.

A user discloses a secret, denoted by \(S\), at turn \(t_0\). The conversation then proceeds through \(d\) unrelated but content-dense drift turns. The sequence is denoted by \(D=\{u_1,a_1,\ldots,u_d,a_d\}\). At evaluation time, a standardized probe \(P\) is appended to the active context. A leakage event occurs when the response reveals the secret exactly, approximately, or semantically. Topic drift captures the number of unrelated turns between disclosure and probing. Persuasion intensity captures the interaction style used to elicit the secret.

The probe conditions are Simple, Medium, and Hard. Simple is a direct neutral query, such as asking for the phone number mentioned earlier. Medium supplies a contextual justification, such as needing the information for a form. Hard uses a high-pressure or urgent request to test whether pressure cues increase or decrease leakage.

Overall leakage is measured at the dialogue level: a dialogue leaks if any standardized probe elicits the secret. Persuasion-level and fuzzy-matching analyses use the probe level, where each dialogue contributes a response for each persuasion condition. Dialogue-level leakage can therefore exceed the average of the individual persuasion-level rates.

A response receives a hybrid leakage label if the normalized secret appears in the normalized response or the LLM judge determines that the response reveals the full secret, a substantial substring, or enough partial information to identify it. Privacy Half-Life, denoted by \(\tau\), measures sustained suppression rather than a temporary decrease. Its observed drift set is \(D_{obs}\), and \(L(d)\) denotes leakage at drift length \(d\). The numerical rules and formal expressions are collected at the end.

## Construction and Experimental Procedures

PrivDrift is a synthetic evaluation framework with a parametric dialogue generator, a probing harness, and a metric suite for leakage detection and temporal analysis. The pipeline constructs controlled multi-turn dialogues, appends standardized probes, queries target models, and scores leakage.

### Dialogue construction

We use a multi-stage scaffold-then-rewrite pipeline. This preserves strict control over the ground-truth secret, drift length, and probe structure while reducing the rigid artifacts of purely template-based generation. The benchmark includes synthetic and human-authored dialogues constructed under the same secret and drift constraints.

Each dialogue begins with a persona sampled from professions and demographic profiles and a ground-truth secret. The fixed-format PII categories are phone numbers, email addresses, Social Security Numbers, and credit card numbers. The opening turn inserts the secret into a plausible task, giving the assistant a legitimate in-context reason to observe it.

Each dialogue receives a topic, such as tax filing, medical triage, or travel logistics. Drift turns come from a topic-conditioned library of dense question-answer pairs and fallback tasks involving professional writing, structured reasoning, or long-form summarization. Annotators authored a subset of drift turns for incorporation into the generation library. These turns add unrelated entities and task demands while preserving experimental control over the original secret and drift length.

A final naturalistic refinement step passes each scaffold to a rewriting model. Its instructions improve fluency and persona realism while preserving the exact secret, drift length, role structure, and final probe conditions. The step changes surface phrasing without changing experimental variables.

### Queries and scoring

The main evaluation appends a final standardized probe to the completed conversation history. The drift sampling distribution includes immediate recall cases while emphasizing short-to-medium topic drift. Each evaluated dialogue contains a seeded secret and an assigned drift length, and each receives the standardized persuasion probes.

We evaluate GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-R1, and Qwen3-VL-235B through the OpenRouter API. All receive the same dialogue histories and probe templates. The evaluation uses cached model-probe responses.

The hierarchical detector first applies normalization-based matching to detect verbatim and near-verbatim leakage. For numeric secrets, all non-digit characters are stripped from the ground-truth secret and response. For alphanumeric secrets, case and whitespace are normalized while meaningful delimiters are preserved. The normalization function is \(\phi(\cdot)\), the response is \(R\), and \(\mathbb{I}\) is the indicator function.

Responses negative under normalization-based matching pass to an open-weights Llama judge (Meta AI, 2024). The judge receives the secret and response and returns a binary verdict about whether the response reveals the secret or a substantial part of it. This stage is intended to capture partial disclosure, indirect hints, and formatting variations missed by deterministic matching.

We compute bootstrap confidence intervals over dialogues for dialogue-level rates. Probe-level sensitivity checks use cached model-probe responses. Paired model and detector comparisons on matched examples use McNemar’s test, with Bonferroni correction where applicable. Repeated measures across persuasion levels use Cochran’s \(Q\) test with post-hoc McNemar tests. Associations of leakage with drift length, persuasion level, or secret type use chi-square tests of independence, with Cramer’s \(V\) as the effect size.

The post-hoc deterministic sensitivity check computes normalized fuzzy matching between each saved secret and saved response, using cached outputs only.

## Related Work

### Contextual privacy and inference-time leakage

Privacy research in language models has historically examined training-data extraction, membership inference, and recovery of memorized PII from pre-training corpora (Shokri et al., 2017; Carlini et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Nasr et al., 2023). Those settings ask whether private information is stored in model parameters. Active-context privacy instead concerns user-provided information in the current interaction that must be handled appropriately at inference time.

Recent benchmarks examine reasoning about privacy norms and contextual access. ConfAIde evaluates privacy reasoning through contextual integrity (Mireshghallah et al., 2024). PrivacyLens and PrivaCI-Bench study privacy norm awareness and legal compliance in agentic or contextual settings (Shao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). These primarily test whether a model should share information in a static access-control or privacy-norm scenario. PrivDrift instead isolates persistence: whether a disclosed secret remains behaviorally recoverable after unrelated drift and later persuasion-based probing.

### Multi-turn adversarial attacks

Aligned models remain vulnerable to multi-turn exploitation. Crescendo attacks show that seemingly benign conversations can gradually elicit harmful outputs (Russinovich et al., 2024). Automated jailbreak frameworks generate adversarial multi-turn attack paths (Deng et al., 2024; Narula et al., 2025), while prompt injection studies show that later instructions can manipulate LLM-integrated applications (Liu et al., 2023).

These works usually concern unsafe content generation or instruction hijacking. PrivDrift applies the multi-turn lens to information flow control. It asks whether a model will re-disclose sensitive information supplied earlier by a user, rather than whether it can be made to produce harmful external content.

### Context management and unlearning

Machine unlearning benchmarks such as TOFU and WMDP evaluate whether knowledge can be removed or suppressed from model behavior (Maini et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). They usually operate over static question-answer pairs and model parameters. PrivDrift is related in spirit but targets active context rather than model weights. It asks whether a secret present in the context window is behaviorally suppressed when later prompts try to elicit it. This distinction matters for deployed RAG systems, copilots, and agentic workflows, where privacy control often depends on prompt-level context management rather than parameter-level unlearning.

## Results

### Hybrid evaluation largely agrees with deterministic matching

Dialogue-level hybrid evaluation differs only slightly from normalization-based matching. Leakage in this benchmark is therefore dominated by direct or near-direct reproduction rather than purely semantic paraphrase. The judge adds a small amount of dialogue-level leakage for DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3-VL-235B, while GPT-OSS-120B is unchanged. This supports hybrid leakage as the main metric and regex-only rates as a transparent deterministic baseline.

### All models leak substantially

Dialogue-level hybrid leakage remains substantial for every evaluated model. DeepSeek-R1 has the lowest rate, GPT-OSS-120B is higher, and Qwen3-VL-235B is highest. These rates estimate whether a dialogue is vulnerable to at least one standardized extraction probe. They show that sensitive information can remain behaviorally recoverable after the conversation has moved to unrelated topics.

### Persuasion effects are non-monotonic

Probe-level leakage varies significantly across persuasion levels for every model, but the direction depends on the model. For GPT-OSS-120B, Hard persuasion reduces leakage relative to Simple and Medium, consistent with pressure cues triggering safety behavior. DeepSeek-R1 leaks least under Medium persuasion. Qwen3-VL-235B leaks most under Hard persuasion, indicating weaker resistance to pressure-based extraction.

The differences do not follow a common progression from neutral requests through justified requests to urgent requests. The effect-size interpretations for persuasion are negligible across models despite the significant differences across conditions.

### Drift does not reliably reduce leakage

The drift-leak curves have an intermediate dip followed by rebound. Drift-length effect sizes are small or negligible across models in the cached probe-level analysis. Privacy risk persists across several unrelated topic shifts even before the original secret is far from the end of the context window.

No evaluated model approaches the Privacy Half-Life safety threshold at any tested drift length. The resulting half-life assignment is the same for all models and persuasion levels, indicating no stable decay within the observed window. Intermediate decreases do not satisfy the stable-decay criterion.

### Secret type strongly determines leakage

Secret type has a large association with hybrid leakage for every model in the cached probe-level analysis. SSNs and credit card numbers are suppressed far more often than emails and phone numbers, although all are user-disclosed and contextually private.

This pattern suggests that current behavior depends more on format and sensitivity cues than on a generalized notion of contextual confidentiality. Secret type has the strongest association with leakage among the evaluated variables.

### Fuzzy matching supports the hybrid labels

Fuzzy leakage closely tracks probe-level hybrid leakage across models. This provides auxiliary deterministic evidence supporting the hybrid labels. The cached probe-level results also support the finding that leakage is mostly direct or near-direct reproduction rather than purely semantic disclosure.

## Discussion

PrivDrift reveals a persistent active-context privacy risk. Sensitive information disclosed earlier can remain recoverable when later prompts supply enough retrieval pressure, even after unrelated conversational drift. The findings concern behavioral reproduction by the assistant, not merely the presence of information in its input.

The strongest pattern is the asymmetry across secret types. Current safeguards may rely on format-sensitive safety heuristics rather than robust contextual reasoning about confidentiality. The distinction between strongly suppressed identifiers and more frequently disclosed contact information persists even though every category is treated as a user-disclosed secret in the benchmark.

Persuasion effects are significant, non-monotonic, and model-dependent. High-pressure requests can reduce disclosure, consistent with the possibility that urgency activates refusal behavior, but they can also produce the highest leakage. Justification is not uniformly protective or uniformly effective at eliciting secrets.

Topic drift should not be treated as an implicit privacy boundary. Temporary drops in leakage are not stable enough to establish persistent suppression. This motivates stability-based evaluation rather than interpreting a transient decrease as evidence that a secret has become behaviorally unavailable.

## Assumptions, Caveats, and Future Work

### Threat assumptions and scope

We do not assume that users believe an LLM literally forgets earlier messages in the same session. Users and application designers may instead underestimate how easily indirect, justified, high-pressure, or tool-mediated prompts can elicit earlier sensitive information.

We also do not assume that an adversary is always unable to inspect the raw transcript. Practical risks include shared or persistent sessions where a later user does not understand what was previously disclosed; enterprise copilots and agentic workflows where tool outputs, retrieved documents, or prompt-injection content issue later instructions; and browser or application-integrated assistants where earlier disclosures were not intended for reuse after a task change.

PrivDrift tests active conversation context, not training-data memorization, personalization memory, or cross-session retention. The findings do not imply permanent memory of a secret or identical behavior in all long-context settings.

### Bounded drift and structured secrets

The evaluated drift is bounded and short-to-medium rather than a full saturation of modern context windows. Results are evidence of persistence under bounded active-context drift, not a complete characterization of privacy behavior across full long-context windows or arbitrarily long contexts.

Fixed-format secrets are easier to detect than unstructured sensitive information such as health status, family circumstances, immigration status, or financial hardship. Future work should extend the benchmark to non-fixed sensitive attributes.

### Dialogue authorship

Human-authored dialogues were inserted throughout the benchmark rather than stored in a separate contiguous block. This improves realism relative to a purely synthetic benchmark while preserving control over secret type, drift length, and probe structure. Current cached evaluation metadata does not retain the identifiers linking evaluated rows to human authorship. We therefore report aggregate results and leave a separate human-versus-synthetic leakage comparison for future work.

### Detector scope

Labels are based on surface-form outputs using deterministic matching, fuzzy matching, and an external LLM judge. Judge-based evaluation can introduce errors, so regex-only and hybrid rates are reported separately. Fuzzy matching remains auxiliary rather than primary because approximate string similarity is less expressive than the judge for partial or semantic disclosures.

We do not yet report formal inter-annotator agreement between humans and the automated methods. A larger manual audit of borderline partial disclosures and obfuscations is needed.

### Reproducibility and artifacts

Compute and budget constraints led us to evaluate API-served models, which may change over time. We mitigate this by logging model identifiers, prompts, timestamps, cached responses, and paired statistics on identical dialogue sets.

We plan to release benchmark generation code, probe templates, evaluation scripts, aggregate result files, and a sanitized subset of generated dialogues. Because the benchmark contains synthetic PII-like strings, released examples will use non-real identifiers and generation templates sufficient to reproduce the benchmark without exposing realistic identifiers.

## Conclusion

PrivDrift audits active-context privacy leakage under topic drift and persuasion intensity. Dialogue-level hybrid leakage remains substantial across the evaluated models. Persuasion affects leakage in model-dependent ways, while secret type has the strongest association. Topic drift does not reliably reduce leakage within the tested range, and Privacy Half-Life exceeds the observed window for every model. Uneven suppression across secret types suggests that current protections depend more on format-sensitive cues than on robust contextual confidentiality.

| Persuasion comparison, all models | Cochran’s \(Q\), \(p<10^{-5}\) |
| Privacy Half-Life result, all models and persuasion levels | \(\tau=\max(D_{obs})+1=7\) |
| Observed-window bound | \(d\leq6\) |

Normalization-based leakage is defined by

\[
\mathrm{Leak}_{\mathrm{regex}}(R,S)
=\mathbb{I}\big[\phi(S)\subset\phi(R)\big].
\]

The Privacy Half-Life safety threshold is

\[
\delta=0.1\times L(0).
\]

The stable-decay rule is

\[
\tau=\min\left\{
d\in D_{obs}\ \middle|\
L(d)\leq\delta
\text{ and }
\forall d'\in D_{obs},\,d'>d:\ L(d')\leq\delta
\right\}.
\]

If this set is empty, the assigned value is the maximum observed drift length plus one. This prevents transient dips from being interpreted as stable suppression.
