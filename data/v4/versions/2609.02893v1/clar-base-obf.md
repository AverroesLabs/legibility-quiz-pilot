# Probe Generalization as Subspace Selection for OOD Deception Detection

Daniel Yoo  
Adrians Skapars

###### Abstract

A probe is a classifier trained to read information from a model’s internal representations. A source probe is trained on one dataset. Out-of-distribution (OOD) performance measures how it transfers to different data. A direction is an axis in representation space; a subspace is the space spanned by a selected set of directions.

Directions heavily weighted by a source probe appear to encode source-specific surface features. Transferable directions appear to encode the same contrast more abstractly, in ways that natural language can describe. Probe OOD robustness appears largely determined by subspace selection.

Machine Learning, ICML (International Conference on Machine Learning)

## 1 Introduction

Linear probes classify examples using weighted sums of model-representation coordinates. They cheaply expose semantically meaningful information in otherwise uninterpretable language-model representations (Alain and Bengio, 2018; Hewitt and Liang, 2019). They detect behaviors and concepts, but sometimes fail on OOD data despite strong in-distribution performance—that is, performance on data from the training distribution (Kirch et al., 2026).

Behavioral concepts have linear representations (Marks and Tegmark, 2024; Park et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025). Probes should therefore have detection capacity. In practice, however, they appear to learn spurious correlations: patterns associated with the labels in the training data that do not transfer reliably (Orgad et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Such unreliability is unacceptable for harmful-interaction monitoring.

We work in coordinates obtained from principal component analysis (PCA), called N4D here. PCA identifies perpendicular directions ordered by how much variation they capture. We call these principal components (PCs), or Q7. Using source Q7 coordinates avoids privileging the model’s original latent dimensions—the individual coordinates of its internal representation. These source-derived coordinates provide data-driven representation structure.

Removing leading components improves downstream embedding performance (Mu et al., 2018). Projections onto leading components also serve as a probing baseline in Kantamneni et al. (2025). Here, projection means retaining an example’s coordinates along selected directions. The selection problem is to find dimensions that permit generalizing source-trained classification rather than classification based on spurious patterns.

## 2 Related Work

#### Linear concept directions in activation space.

Activations are the model’s internal numerical representations during processing. True/false representations exhibit linear structure shared across datasets (Marks and Tegmark, 2024). Park et al. (2024) formalize the linear representation hypothesis: concepts can be represented by directions in activation space. They connect this hypothesis to probing and steering, or changing behavior by modifying activations.

Honesty and emotion admit population-level activation directions that permit readout and intervention (Zou et al., 2025). A single direction mediates refusal (Arditi et al., 2024). An “assistant” axis corresponds to assistant-like behavior (Lu et al., 2026). Such simple features support mechanistic understanding and activation interventions. They leave unresolved which directions represent stable concepts and which represent context-dependent correlations.

#### Linear probes and their limitations.

Linear and lightweight probes have a long interpretability history (Alain and Bengio, 2018; Hewitt and Liang, 2019). Applications include latent truthfulness without direct supervision (Burns et al., 2024) and strategic deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025).

High accuracy need not establish that a model already represents the property being tested. The probe can learn the task or exploit surface correlates (Hewitt and Liang, 2019). Information encoded by the model and information merely recoverable by a probe remain distinct concerns (Ravichander et al., 2021). Strong in-distribution probes generalize unevenly (kramár2026buildingproductionreadyprobesgemini). Safety-probe evaluation must therefore distinguish robust internal signals from dataset artifacts.

#### Interpreting features with natural-language explanations.

Bills et al. (2023) introduced explanations generated by a large language model (LLM) from examples that strongly activate a neuron. Automatic feature labeling extends this approach to large dictionaries of sparse-autoencoder features ((Paulo et al., 2025), (Bricken et al., 2023)). A sparse autoencoder learns to reconstruct representations using a small number of active features.

Natural-language descriptions also support intervention. Casademunt et al. (2025) automatically identify concepts and ablate them—remove their contribution—during fine-tuning, reducing misaligned behavior.

## 3 Setup

The model is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, called E6 here (Grattafiori et al., 2024). We use its layer 15 residual-stream activations, the internal representation carried through that layer. This follows Ying et al. (2026), whose analysis assigns layer 15 the highest cross-domain discrimination. Each prompt is passed through E6. Activations are averaged over assistant-response tokens only, where tokens are the text units processed by the model.

We use four datasets from Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025):

- **Roleplaying (V8):** the sole source dataset used to train the probes.
- **Insider Trading Report (C2R):** a held-out target reporting task.
- **Insider Trading Confirm (W6):** a held-out target confirmation task.
- **Sandbagging (J9):** a held-out target underperformance task.

No other source dataset is used. Appendix A describes the tasks and gives label counts in Table 1. The response labeler is GPT-4o, called K8 here (OpenAI, 2024). It assigns honest responses label 0, ambiguous responses label -1, and deceptive responses label 1. Ambiguous responses are removed.

Each source activation coordinate is standardized to zero mean and unit variance. The same source means and standard deviations are used to standardize the targets. PCA is then fitted to the source activations, and the leading 100 Q7 directions are retained. A subset \(S\), meaning a selected set of these directions, defines the projection used before probe training and evaluation.

All classifiers use logistic regression, which predicts a binary label from a weighted sum of the input coordinates. Its regularization parameter is \(\lambda=10\). The full-representation baseline, called B8Q, instead trains directly on all standardized activation coordinates.

We retain 100 directions for interpretability comparability, not because low-index directions generalize better. Alignment with sparse-autoencoder decoder features, called U9, decreases with component index (Bricken et al., 2023). Decoder features are the vectors used to reconstruct representations from sparse-autoencoder features. This alignment makes lower-index directions more amenable to natural-language interpretation. Appendix C and Figure 6 show the comparison; Appendix A reports its rank correlations.

Error bars denote 95% confidence intervals (CIs), the reported intervals expressing uncertainty around estimates.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setting a baseline

We measure discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), called F3 here. This measures how well a classifier ranks deceptive responses above honest responses.

Transfer is reasonably good on W6, moderate on C2R, and poor on J9. B8Q F3 is 0.874 on W6, 0.706 on C2R, and 0.606 on J9 (Appendix A, numerical-observations table, “B8Q F3” row).

A classifier trained on each target, while remaining within the leading 100 source directions, separates deceptive from honest responses on every target. Its F3 is 0.950 on W6, 0.936 on C2R, and 0.869 on J9 (Appendix A, “Target-trained F3, 5-fold CV” row). Thus, relevant information is present in this source subspace; the source-trained direction fails to generalize.

Cross-validation (CV) divides data into folds, trains or selects using non-held-out folds, and evaluates on the held-out fold. Figure 2 uses 5-fold CV.

Figure 2: Target F3 for source-trained full representations, blue; source-trained leading 100 Q7, orange; target-trained leading 100 source Q7, green, with 5-fold CV.

### 4.2 Showing a transferable subspace exists

We use a target-guided greedy search, called the greedy oracle or H2, to select a transferable subspace. “Greedy” means that the search adds the best available direction at each step. “Oracle” indicates that it uses target labels for selection.

Small source-trained subspaces nearly match target-trained probes, particularly on C2R and J9 (Figure 3). H2 improves over B8Q by 0.22 F3 points on C2R and 0.23 on J9, as reported in Appendix A. The mean H2 subset sizes under target CV are 15 for C2R, 8 for W6, and 14 for J9 (Appendix A, “Mean H2 subset size, target CV” row).

The variance-selection control, called top-k PCs by variance or Y4, retains the \(k\) leading components, where \(k\) is the number of selected directions. With \(k\) matched to H2, Y4 matches H2 only on the easy W6 case. It falls far behind on the harder C2R and J9 tasks (Figure 3). Explained variance—the variation captured by a component—does not predict usefulness.

Figure 3: Target F3 for B8Q, blue; Y4 with \(k\) matched to H2, teal; H2, purple.

The search proceeds as follows:

1. Start with an empty subset.
2. Consider adding each available direction among the leading 100 source Q7 directions.
3. For each candidate subset, project source activations onto that subset and fit logistic-regression weights using source labels.
4. Evaluate the resulting source-trained probe on target data.
5. Add the direction that most improves target F3.

The search stops when no candidate improves F3 by more than 0.001. Under target 5-fold CV, selection uses the non-held-out target folds. The selected source-trained probe is evaluated on the held-out target fold.

Target labels guide subset selection. They never fit probe weights. This procedure is a transfer upper bound, not an unbiased OOD estimate.

### 4.3 Testing hypotheses for transferable components

We test two hypotheses. Transferable directions might be those that individually separate target classes strongly. Alternatively, they might be directions already heavily weighted by the source probe, with selection simply finding a compact version of that source signal.

Both rankings use a standardized coefficient, called X4: the absolute probe coefficient multiplied by the standard deviation of its corresponding Q7 coordinate. Rankings are computed on activations projected into the source PCA basis.

Two ranking probes provide these orderings:

- The **source-PCA probe (T6)** is trained on source data.
- The **target-PCA probe (M9)** is trained on target data.

For each ordering, the evaluated probe is then trained on source data projected onto the top \(k\) ranked directions. Thus, even the M9-ranked evaluation uses source-fitted probe weights.

Neither source-side nor target-side ranking recovers the transferable subspace or a basis that transfers better than B8Q. T6 transfers about as well as B8Q: source-reliant directions do not generalize. M9 does well on C2R, worse than H2 on W6, and below random on J9 (Figure 4). Target-class separation and transfer under source-fitted weighting select different directions. Appendix D lists the selected directions and their overlaps.

Figure 4: Target F3 for T6, red; M9, pink; H2, purple.

For these comparisons, H2 selects using the full target dataset. This supplies a stable set and \(k\), rather than fold-specific selections. Its resulting numbers differ slightly from the CV results. Appendix A reports full-target H2 \(k\) values of 13 for C2R, 7 for W6, and 14 for J9.

### 4.4 Interpreting the transferable subspace

Transferable subsets differ from the directions relied on by source or target classification. Interpretations of H2-selected directions express contrasts at a level of abstraction that survives domain changes. Recovery is only partial. Appendix E, Tables 3 and 4, gives explanations for shared H2 selections and leading T6 selections.

For each leading source direction, an LLM judge, called C7, receives the eight most-activating and eight least-activating source examples. Positive and negative activation values identify opposite poles of the direction. Following Bills et al. (2023) and Casademunt et al. (2025), C7 generates descriptions of the positive pole, negative pole, and whole direction.

C7 then assigns an OOD score, called R5, rating the direction’s expected usefulness for deception detection across distributions on a 1–10 scale. Appendix B gives the model and prompt.

Selection retains all directions that reach the observed maximum score in both scoring runs. The maximum observed R5 is 6, and 19 directions reach it in both runs (Appendix E, selection-observations table). Scores are noisy.

### 4.5 Selecting a subspace by interpreting components

The retained intersection of the two scoring runs defines the interpretation-selected PC probe, called L2. It uses 19 directions. Its controls are:

- The **interpretation-ablated PC probe (A7)**, trained on the complementary 81 directions.
- **Y4**, trained on an equal-dimensional subset of the leading variance-ranked components.

All are trained as source probes. The direction counts appear in Appendix E’s selection-observations table.

Language-only selection, without target data, meaningfully improves generalization over B8Q. L2 exceeds B8Q and the variance control on all targets, widely on C2R and J9. It also exceeds A7 everywhere (Figure 5).

Recovery varies sharply. On C2R, L2 reaches F3 0.881, compared with B8Q’s 0.706 and the H2 comparison value of 0.929. The reported recovery of the baseline–H2 gap is 78%. On J9, L2 reaches 0.662, compared with B8Q’s 0.606 and H2’s 0.832; reported recovery is 25% (Appendix A, corresponding F3 and gap-recovery rows). C2R therefore substantially closes the baseline–H2 gap, while J9 closes it only partially. W6 has little headroom and is uninformative: every selection method except A7 is roughly tied (Figure 5).

Figure 5: Target F3 for L2, red; B8Q, blue; Y4 at the L2 dimension count, teal; A7, brown.

## 5 Limitations

Claims remain narrow. We study one model and one source dataset. Other model families and datasets remain unresolved. W6’s already-high baseline makes its results uninformative; its B8Q F3 is 0.874 (Appendix A).

J9 is the weakest result and the most out-of-distribution target. Its modest improvement, from B8Q F3 0.606 to L2 F3 0.662, likely reflects distribution shift. C2R’s improvement, from 0.706 to 0.881, is more robust (Appendix A, “B8Q F3” and “L2 F3” rows).

The pipeline is fully linear: PCA projection followed by logistic regression. This makes the existence of a transferable subspace perhaps unsurprising. Extension to nonlinear probes or other readout locations remains open.

Statements about what directions “encode” are descriptive, not causal. They are based on extreme-activation examples and C7’s verbal descriptions. High-rated directions have not been ablated during inference to verify their apparent contrast.

## 6 Conclusion

Robust behavioral probing requires identifying where a linearly accessible signal is represented most robustly, not merely whether a linear classifier can access it. Full-representation probes may mix general behavioral features with dataset-specific surface cues. This can support high in-distribution accuracy while harming transfer.

Sparse directional subspaces—subspaces using relatively few directions—may isolate more general behavioral structure. OOD probing therefore involves representation selection as well as classifier fitting: preserve the intended behavioral feature and filter spurious correlation.

## Appendix C Lower Q7 Show Stronger Decoder Alignment

Lower-index directions align more strongly with sparse-autoencoder (SAE) decoder vectors, called U9. Alignment is measured using both the best-matching U9 and the mean of the top-feature alignments. We use `andyrdt/saes-llama-3.1-8b-instruct` (andyrdt, 2024).
