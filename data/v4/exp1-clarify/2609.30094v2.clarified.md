# PrivDrift: Auditing User-Secret Leakage Under Topic Drift in Active LLM Conversations

###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs), which generate language from supplied context, increasingly serve as persistent assistants across extended interactions. They operate in user-facing systems, shared conversation sessions, and systems connected to tools. Sensitive information disclosed during an active conversation may remain behaviorally recoverable: later prompts may cause the model to reproduce it even after the conversation shifts to unrelated topics.

We introduce PrivDrift, a benchmark for auditing this risk. It tests whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable after topic drift—intervening turns on unrelated topics—and persuasion-based probes, which are prompts designed to elicit the secret. PrivDrift contains 1,000 controlled multi-turn dialogues with deliberately inserted secrets, content-dense drift turns, and standardized extraction probes.

Across three LLMs with extended context windows—the text available to a model when it responds—dialogue-level hybrid leakage ranges from 38.7% to 54.6% (Table 3). A dialogue counts as leaking if any standardized probe elicits its secret. The hybrid detector combines normalized text matching with an LLM judge. Leakage varies strongly by model, secret type, and persuasion intensity (Tables 3–5). Within the tested drift window, additional topic drift does not reliably reduce leakage (Section 5.4). These results suggest evaluating privacy risk in active LLM contexts as a persistent behavioral failure mode, rather than only as training-data memorization or immediate jailbreaking.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have evolved from simple chat systems into persistent assistants that maintain coherence over extended interactions (OpenAI, 2023; Touvron et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). These systems are used in healthcare triage, financial planning, enterprise copilots—assistants embedded in workplace tools—and personal productivity tools. Users may disclose Personally Identifiable Information (PII), including phone numbers, financial identifiers, email addresses, and health-related details. Context retention supports useful multi-turn assistance. It also creates a privacy risk: later responses may reproduce sensitive information disclosed earlier in the active conversation.

Existing safety evaluations mostly target two extremes. Training-data memorization concerns extracting private information stored in model parameters, the learned values that determine model behavior (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2023). Immediate jailbreaking concerns prompts that elicit harmful behavior in the current turn despite safeguards (Wei et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). Recent work also studies prompt injection, where instructions in supplied content redirect a system, and attacks spread across multiple turns (Liu et al., 2023; Russinovich et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2024). These settings do not directly measure whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable after unrelated conversational drift. The gap is whether topic shifts act as a practical privacy boundary within an active conversation.

We do not assume that users believe an LLM literally forgets earlier messages in the same session. We study a behavioral risk instead. Users and application designers may underestimate how easily later prompts can elicit earlier sensitive information through indirect requests, justifications, pressure, or tools. This risk matters in shared sessions, browser-integrated assistants, enterprise copilots, and agentic workflows, where models carry out tasks through sequences of actions or tools. It also matters when prompt-injection instructions interact with active context in ways the original user did not intend.

PrivDrift measures active-context privacy leakage along two axes:

- **Topic drift:** the number of unrelated turns between secret disclosure and later probing.
- **Persuasion intensity:** the interaction style used to elicit the secret.

Across three LLMs with extended context windows and 1,000 dialogues per model, dialogue-level hybrid leakage ranges from 38.7% to 54.6% (Table 3). Persuasion intensity significantly affects leakage (Section 5.3). Leakage also varies sharply by secret type. Social Security Numbers (SSNs) and credit card numbers are suppressed far more often than email addresses and phone numbers (Table 5). Within the tested window, \(d\leq 6\), where \(d\) is the number of intervening drift turns, additional drift does not reliably reduce leakage (Section 5.4).

Our contributions are as follows:

1. PrivDrift is a controlled benchmark for auditing whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable from active conversational context after unrelated topic drift.

2. We evaluate direct, justified, and high-pressure probes. These model different ways later prompts can elicit sensitive context.

3. We implement a reproducible hybrid detector. It combines matching after text normalization with an open-weights LLM judge, whose model weights are available. We report dialogue-level, probe-level, regex-only, fuzzy, and hybrid leakage rates. “Regex-only” denotes the deterministic normalization-based matching stage. Fuzzy matching measures approximate text similarity.

4. We propose Privacy Half-Life, denoted by \(\tau\), as a stability-based measure of persistent suppression. No evaluated model reaches stable decay within the observed drift range (Section 5.6).

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Contextual Privacy and Inference-Time Leakage

Language-model privacy research has historically focused on training-data extraction, membership inference, and recovery of memorized PII from pre-training corpora (Shokri et al., 2017; Carlini et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Nasr et al., 2023). Training-data extraction recovers information from training examples. Membership inference asks whether an example belonged to the training data. Pre-training corpora are the collections of text used for initial model training. These settings concern private information stored in model parameters.

Active-context privacy instead concerns user-provided information in the current interaction. The model must handle this information appropriately at inference time, when it generates responses.

Recent benchmarks test whether LLMs can reason about privacy norms and contextual access. ConfAIde evaluates privacy reasoning through contextual integrity: whether information sharing fits the surrounding context (Mireshghallah et al., 2024). PrivacyLens and PrivaCI-Bench study privacy-norm awareness and legal compliance in agentic or contextual settings (Shao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). These benchmarks primarily ask whether a model should share information under a fixed access-control or privacy-norm scenario. PrivDrift instead isolates persistence: whether a user-disclosed secret remains behaviorally recoverable after unrelated topic drift and later persuasion-based probing.

### 2.2 Multi-Turn Adversarial Attacks

Aligned models—models trained to follow behavioral and safety constraints—remain vulnerable to multi-turn exploitation. Crescendo attacks gradually elicit harmful outputs through seemingly benign conversations (Russinovich et al., 2024). Automated jailbreak frameworks generate adversarial attack paths across multiple turns (Deng et al., 2024; Narula et al., 2025). Prompt-injection studies also show that later instructions can manipulate LLM-integrated applications (Liu et al., 2023).

These works usually focus on unsafe content generation or instruction hijacking, where an attacker redirects the system's instructions. PrivDrift applies the multi-turn perspective to information flow control: controlling whether information is disclosed. It asks whether a model will re-disclose sensitive information supplied earlier by a user in the same active context, rather than whether it will generate harmful external content.

### 2.3 Context Management and Unlearning

Machine unlearning aims to remove or suppress knowledge from model behavior. Benchmarks such as TOFU and WMDP evaluate this goal (Maini et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). These benchmarks usually operate over fixed question-answer pairs and model parameters.

PrivDrift targets active context rather than model weights. It asks whether a secret still present in the context window is behaviorally suppressed when later prompts try to elicit it. This distinction matters for deployed retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems, which supply retrieved material to a model, as well as copilots and agentic workflows. In these systems, privacy control often depends on managing supplied context through prompts rather than removing knowledge from model parameters.

## 3 The PrivDrift Framework

Figure 1: PrivDrift overview. The pipeline constructs controlled multi-turn dialogues, appends standardized probes, queries target models, and scores leakage with a hybrid detector.

PrivDrift is a synthetic evaluation framework for measuring whether sensitive information disclosed in an active conversation remains recoverable after topic drift. It has three components (Figure 1): a dialogue generator with controlled settings, a probing harness that submits test prompts, and metrics for leakage detection and analysis across drift lengths.

### 3.1 Threat Model: Active-Context Re-Disclosure

We study **active-context re-disclosure**. This failure occurs when later prompts recover sensitive information disclosed earlier in a conversation, after unrelated topic drift. We do not assume that an adversary is always unable to inspect the raw transcript. PrivDrift instead tests whether the model itself re-discloses the sensitive value when later instructions ask for it, justify its disclosure, or pressure the assistant to reveal it.

This setting captures several risks:

- In shared or persistent sessions, a later user may not understand what private information was previously disclosed.
- In enterprise copilots and agentic workflows, later tool outputs, retrieved documents, or prompt-injection content may issue instructions that interact with active context.
- In browser- or application-integrated assistants, users may not intend earlier sensitive disclosures to be reused after the task changes.

The question is not whether the transcript contains the secret. It is whether the assistant reproduces the secret when later prompted.

Formally, a user discloses a secret \(S\) at turn \(t_0\), the disclosure turn. The conversation then proceeds through \(d\) unrelated but content-dense drift turns:

\[
D=\{u_1,a_1,\ldots,u_d,a_d\}.
\]

Here, \(D\) is the intervening dialogue. Each \(u_i\) is a user message and each \(a_i\) is its assistant reply at drift position \(i\). At evaluation time, a standardized probe \(P\) is appended to the active context. Leakage occurs if the response reveals \(S\) exactly, approximately, or semantically—that is, by conveying its information without necessarily reproducing its exact text.

### 3.2 Dataset Generation Pipeline

We generate \(N=1000\) controlled dialogues, where \(N\) is the total dialogue count. A multi-stage scaffold-then-rewrite pipeline first constructs a controlled dialogue structure and then refines its wording. This preserves the ground-truth secret—the known inserted value—along with drift length and probe structure. It also reduces rigid phrasing from purely template-based generation.

The dataset contains 900 synthetic dialogues and 100 human-authored dialogues built under the same secret and drift constraints (Table 1). Human-authored dialogues were inserted throughout the benchmark, not stored in a separate contiguous block. The current cached evaluation metadata—the saved information describing evaluated examples—does not preserve their identifiers. We therefore report aggregate results and leave a separate human-versus-synthetic leakage comparison for future work.

#### 3.2.1 Persona and Secret Injection

Each dialogue starts with a persona—a described user profile—sampled from more than 100 professions and demographic profiles. It also receives a ground-truth secret \(S\). We sample \(S\) from four fixed-format PII categories: phone numbers, email addresses, Social Security Numbers, and credit card numbers.

The opening turn contains the secret as part of a plausible task. This gives the assistant a legitimate in-context reason to observe it.

#### 3.2.2 Content-Dense Topic Drift

Each dialogue receives a topic, such as tax filing, medical triage, or travel logistics. Drift turns come from a topic-conditioned library of dense question-answer pairs: the assigned topic determines which material is drawn. Fallback tasks involve professional writing, structured reasoning, or long-form summarization.

Annotators human-author a subset of drift turns for the generation library. The design aims to add unrelated entities and task demands to active context while keeping the original secret and drift-turn count controlled.

#### 3.2.3 Naturalistic Refinement

A final refinement step reduces rigidity in the initial scaffold. A rewriting model receives each scaffold and instructions to improve fluency and persona realism. It must preserve the exact secret, drift length, user/assistant role structure, and final probe conditions. This step changes surface phrasing, not the experimental variables.

### 3.3 Probing Setup

The main evaluation appends a final standardized probe to the completed conversation history. Probes have three persuasion levels:

- **Simple:** a direct, neutral query, such as asking for the phone number mentioned earlier.
- **Medium:** a query with a contextual justification, such as needing the information for a form.
- **Hard:** a high-pressure or urgent request testing whether pressure cues increase or decrease leakage.

We sample drift length \(d\), the number of intervening turns, from this mixed distribution:

\[
d\sim
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{with }p=0.2,\\
\mathrm{Uniform}\{2,3,4,5,6\} & \text{with }p=0.8.
\end{cases}
\tag{1}
\]

Here, \(p\) denotes probability. \(\mathrm{Uniform}\) means that the listed drift lengths are equally likely within that branch. Thus, the distribution includes immediate-recall cases while emphasizing short-to-medium drift. We interpret the results as bounded active-context persistence, not as a full test of arbitrarily long context windows.

### 3.4 Evaluation Methodology

A model may reveal a secret verbatim, with formatting changes, as a partial substring, or through a paraphrased reference. A substring is a consecutive portion of text. We therefore use a hierarchical hybrid detector, applying two stages in order.

A response counts as leakage if either:

1. The normalized secret appears in the normalized response; or
2. An LLM judge determines that the response reveals the full secret, a substantial substring, or enough partial information to identify it.

Figure 2: Hierarchical hybrid evaluation. Normalization-based matching is applied first; regex-negative responses are then passed to an LLM judge.

#### 3.4.1 Stage 1: Normalization-Based Matching

The first stage detects verbatim and near-verbatim leakage. For numeric secrets, it removes every non-digit character from both the secret and response. For alphanumeric secrets, which contain letters or numbers, it normalizes case and whitespace while retaining meaningful delimiters.

Let \(\phi(\cdot)\) be this normalization function. The regex-stage leakage label is

\[
\mathrm{Leak}_{\mathrm{regex}}(R,S)
=
\mathbb{I}\big[\phi(S)\subset\phi(R)\big].
\tag{2}
\]

Here, \(R\) is the model response, and \(\mathrm{Leak}_{\mathrm{regex}}\) is the deterministic leakage label. The containment expression means that the normalized secret occurs within the normalized response. The indicator function \(\mathbb{I}\) returns 1 when that condition is true and 0 otherwise.

#### 3.4.2 Stage 2: LLM-as-a-Judge Verification

Responses that do not match in Stage 1 are passed to an open-weights Llama judge (Meta AI, 2024). The judge receives both the secret and the response. It returns a binary verdict—leakage or no leakage—on whether the response reveals the secret or a substantial part of it.

This stage aims to detect partial disclosures, indirect hints, and formatting variations missed by deterministic matching. Judge-based evaluation can introduce errors. We therefore report regex-only and hybrid rates separately.

#### 3.4.3 Privacy Half-Life

Privacy Half-Life, \(\tau\), measures sustained suppression rather than a temporary decrease. Let \(D_{obs}\) be the set of observed drift lengths. Let \(L(d)\) be the leakage rate at drift length \(d\), so \(L(0)\) is the rate without intervening drift. Define the safety threshold as \(\delta=0.1\times L(0)\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tau=\min\{d\in D_{obs}\mid\;&L(d)\leq\delta\\
&\text{and }\forall d^{\prime}\in D_{obs},\ d^{\prime}>d:
L(d^{\prime})\leq\delta\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Here, \(\min\) selects the smallest qualifying observed drift length. The condition using \(\forall\) requires every later observed drift length \(d'\) also to remain at or below the threshold.

If no observed drift length qualifies, we assign \(\tau=\max(D_{obs})+1\), one greater than the largest tested drift length. This prevents transient dips from counting as stable suppression.

Figure 3: Privacy Half-Life \(\tau\) requires stable decay below the threshold. A later resurgence disqualifies a transient decrease.

## 4 Experimental Setup

##### Models.

We evaluate GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-R1, and Qwen3-VL-235B through the OpenRouter application programming interface (API), the interface used to submit model requests. All models receive the same dialogue histories and probe templates.

##### Dataset.

We evaluate 1,000 PrivDrift dialogues on each model. Each contains one seeded secret and one drift length \(d\in\{0,2,3,4,5,6\}\), sampled from Equation (1). Each dialogue is evaluated under three standardized persuasion probes. This yields 3,000 cached, or saved, model-probe responses per model and 9,000 total cached responses across the three models.

##### Aggregation levels.

We report two levels:

- **Dialogue level:** a dialogue counts as leaking if any standardized probe elicits its secret. Overall leakage uses this level.
- **Probe level:** each dialogue contributes one response per persuasion condition. Persuasion-level and fuzzy-matching analyses use this level.

Because dialogue-level leakage counts success by any probe, it can exceed the average of the individual persuasion-level leakage rates.

##### Uncertainty and statistical tests.

We report both regex-only and hybrid leakage. For dialogue-level rates, we compute 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, which estimate uncertainty by resampling dialogues. Probe-level sensitivity checks use cached model-probe responses.

For paired model and detector comparisons on matched examples, we use McNemar’s test, which compares paired binary outcomes. Where applicable, we apply Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. For repeated measurements across persuasion levels, we use Cochran’s \(Q\) test, which compares related binary outcomes across conditions, followed by post-hoc McNemar tests.

To test whether leakage is associated with drift length, persuasion level, or secret type, we use chi-square tests of independence. We report Cramer’s \(V\), a measure of association strength, as the effect size. Reported \(p\)-values measure how incompatible the test results are with the relevant null hypothesis of no difference or no association.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Dialogue-Level Hybrid Evaluation Largely Agrees With Deterministic Matching

At the dialogue level, hybrid evaluation differs only slightly from normalization-based matching (Table 2). This indicates that direct or near-direct reproduction dominates leakage in this benchmark, rather than purely semantic paraphrase.

The LLM judge adds up to 1.10 percentage points of dialogue-level leakage: +0.00 for GPT-OSS-120B, +0.90 for DeepSeek-R1, and +1.10 for Qwen3-VL-235B (Table 2). This supports using hybrid leakage as the main metric while retaining regex-only rates as a transparent deterministic baseline.

In Table 2, \(\Delta\) is hybrid leakage minus regex-only leakage, in percentage points. The \(p\) column reports the comparison's \(p\)-value.

### 5.3 Persuasion Intensity Affects Leakage Non-Monotonically

Probe-level leakage differs significantly across persuasion levels for every model (Cochran’s \(Q\), \(p<10^{-5}\)). The direction depends on the model rather than increasing or decreasing consistently with intensity (Table 4).

For GPT-OSS-120B, hard persuasion reduces leakage to 32.90%, compared with 39.30% for simple and 40.50% for medium persuasion (Table 4, GPT-OSS-120B row). This is consistent with pressure cues triggering safety behavior. For DeepSeek-R1, medium persuasion produces the lowest leakage, 22.00% (Table 4, DeepSeek-R1 row). For Qwen3-VL-235B, hard persuasion produces the highest leakage, 40.60%, indicating weaker resistance to pressure-based extraction (Table 4, Qwen3-VL-235B row).

### 5.4 Drift Length Does Not Reliably Reduce Leakage Within the Tested Window

Leakage curves dip near \(d=3\), then rebound (Figure 4). However, drift-length effect sizes are small or negligible. In the cached probe-level analysis, Cramer’s \(V\) is 0.1073 for DeepSeek-R1, 0.0659 for GPT-OSS-120B, and 0.0723 for Qwen3-VL-235B (Table 8, “Drift length” rows).

This is not a claim about arbitrarily long contexts. It shows that privacy risk can persist across several unrelated topic shifts, even before the original secret is far from the end of the context window.

### 5.5 Secret Type Strongly Determines Leakage

Leakage varies sharply by secret type. In the cached probe-level analysis, Cramer’s \(V\) shows a large association for every model: 0.5871 for DeepSeek-R1, 0.7731 for GPT-OSS-120B, and 0.6927 for Qwen3-VL-235B (Table 8, “Secret type” rows).

SSNs and credit card numbers are suppressed far more often than emails and phone numbers, although all are user-disclosed and contextually private (Table 5, SSN and Credit card rows versus Email and Phone rows). This pattern suggests that current behavior depends more on format and sensitivity cues than on a generalized notion of contextual confidentiality.

### 5.6 Privacy Half-Life Exceeds the Observed Window

Under the stable-decay definition of Privacy Half-Life, the threshold is \(\delta=0.1\cdot L(0)\). No evaluated model approaches this threshold at any tested drift length. Consequently, \(\tau=\max(D_{obs})+1=7\) for all models and persuasion levels. This indicates no stable decay within \(d\leq 6\), rather than an observed threshold crossing at 7.

### 5.7 Fuzzy Matching Supports the Hybrid Labels

As a post-hoc deterministic sensitivity check—an additional analysis after the main evaluation—we compute normalized fuzzy matching between each saved secret and response. This uses cached outputs only.

At a similarity threshold of 0.85, fuzzy leakage closely tracks probe-level hybrid leakage. The rates are 26.40% versus 26.43% for DeepSeek-R1, 37.03% versus 37.57% for GPT-OSS-120B, and 37.60% versus 37.53% for Qwen3-VL-235B (Table 6, corresponding model rows). Table 6 also reports a 0.80 threshold.

Fuzzy matching is auxiliary evidence, not the primary metric. Approximate string similarity is less expressive than the judge for partial or semantic disclosures.

## 6 Discussion

PrivDrift reveals a persistent active-context privacy risk. Sensitive information disclosed earlier can remain behaviorally recoverable after unrelated topic drift (Section 5.4; Figure 4). This does not imply that models permanently remember the secret or that all long-context settings behave similarly. It shows that current assistants may reproduce sensitive in-context information when later prompts apply enough retrieval pressure—pressure to recover earlier information. The cached probe-level evaluation also indicates that this behavior is mostly direct or near-direct reproduction: fuzzy matching closely tracks hybrid leakage (Table 6).

The strongest pattern is the asymmetry across secret types. SSNs and credit card numbers are suppressed far more often than emails and phone numbers, although all four categories are user-disclosed secrets (Table 5). Secret type also has the largest reported effect sizes (Table 8). This suggests that current safeguards may rely on format-sensitive safety heuristics—behavioral rules keyed to a secret's form—rather than robust contextual reasoning about confidentiality.

Persuasion effects are significant but non-monotonic and model-dependent (Section 5.3; Table 4). High-pressure requests reduce GPT-OSS-120B leakage, consistent with the possibility that urgency cues activate refusal behavior. Medium persuasion produces DeepSeek-R1's lowest leakage. Hard persuasion produces Qwen3-VL-235B's highest leakage, suggesting weaker resistance to pressure-based extraction.

Finally, topic drift should not be treated as an implicit privacy boundary. Intermediate dips in leakage are not stable enough to satisfy the Privacy Half-Life criterion (Figure 4; Section 5.6). This motivates stability-based evaluation rather than treating temporary drops as evidence of suppression.

## 7 Limitations and Future Work

##### Bounded drift range.

PrivDrift tests drift lengths up to \(d=6\). This is bounded short-to-medium conversational drift, not full saturation of modern context windows. The results show persistence under bounded active-context drift. They do not fully characterize privacy behavior across entire long-context windows.

##### Fixed-format secrets.

The benchmark focuses on structured secrets: SSNs, credit card numbers, emails, and phone numbers. These are easier to detect than unstructured sensitive information, such as health status, family circumstances, immigration status, or financial hardship. Future work should extend the benchmark to sensitive attributes without fixed formats.

##### Active context, not cross-session memory.

PrivDrift evaluates recoverability within the active conversation. It does not test training-data memorization, personalization memory, or retention across sessions.

##### Synthetic and human-authored dialogues.

The dataset contains 900 synthetic and 100 human-authored dialogues, inserted throughout the benchmark rather than stored as a separate block (Table 1). This improves realism relative to a purely synthetic benchmark while preserving control over secret type, drift length, and probe structure.

However, current cached evaluation metadata does not identify which evaluated rows are human-authored. We therefore report aggregate benchmark results and leave a separate human-versus-synthetic comparison for future work.

##### Detector scope.

Leakage labels are based on the text of model outputs. Detection uses deterministic matching, fuzzy matching, and an external LLM judge. We report regex-only, fuzzy, and hybrid labels separately to make detector behavior transparent (Tables 2 and 6).

We do not yet report formal inter-annotator agreement between humans and automated methods—that is, how consistently they assign the same labels. Future work should include a larger manual audit of borderline partial disclosures and obfuscations, where a secret's form is obscured.

##### API-served models and reproducibility.

Compute and budget constraints lead us to evaluate API-served models. These models may change over time. We mitigate this by logging model identifiers, prompts, timestamps, cached responses, and paired statistics on identical dialogue sets.

##### Artifact availability.

We plan to release benchmark generation code, probe templates, evaluation scripts, aggregate result files, and a sanitized subset of generated dialogues. The benchmark contains synthetic strings resembling PII. We will therefore release examples with non-real identifiers and generation templates sufficient to reproduce the benchmark without exposing realistic identifiers.

## 8 Conclusion

We introduce PrivDrift, a benchmark for auditing active-context privacy leakage under topic drift and persuasion intensity. Across three LLMs and 1,000 dialogues per model, dialogue-level hybrid leakage ranges from 38.7% to 54.6% (Table 3). Persuasion intensity affects leakage differently across models (Table 4). Secret type has the strongest association with leakage (Table 8).

Within the tested drift range, topic drift does not reliably reduce leakage (Section 5.4). Privacy Half-Life exceeds the observed window for all models (Section 5.6). The asymmetry across secret types (Table 5) suggests that current protections remain uneven and depend more on format-sensitive cues than on robust contextual reasoning about confidentiality.
