# Explanation Multiplicity: Circuit-Level Interpretability

Evidence Does Not Survive Defensible Analytic Variation

Ajay Pravin Mahale

12 August 2026

###### Abstract

The European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act) requires providers of high-risk systems to file technical documentation describing how the system reaches its decisions. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to explain a model’s behaviour through its internal computations. It is the obvious source of such evidence. Circuit discovery, which selects a network subgraph said to explain a behaviour, is its most developed instrument.

We ask whether this evidence survives the conditions under which it would be used: two competent analysts use the same system and tool, but choose different defensible settings.

We pre-registered a crossed grid of seven analytic axes: choices whose levels define an analysis. Every level came from a published implementation. We then used a deterministic claim map—a fixed rule with no random or language-model-generated output—to turn each discovered circuit into a structured statement for Annex IV of the Act.

The study used GPT-2 small, a language model, on indirect object identification: a task involving identification of the indirect object in a prompt. Across 15,840 pre-registered specifications, or combinations of analytic settings, 7,561 produced a claim. The derived statement differs on 73.2% of specification pairs. Its 95% confidence interval (CI), the bootstrap uncertainty interval used here, is 0.725 to 0.738. The modal claim, meaning the most frequent claim, accounts for 41.1% of the space. The evidence fails a filability criterion—a rule for whether evidence is stable enough to file—at every tolerance a conformity assessment body would plausibly accept.

Standardising the most influential choice, the evaluation metric, leaves a flip rate of 59.4%. Removing circuit size from the claim and comparing only circuits of the same size leaves 27.1% (95% CI 0.255 to 0.286). This remains above the pre-registered threshold.

The circuits behind these claims are structurally near-disjoint. Their median pairwise Jaccard overlap, the number of shared edges divided by the number of edges in either circuit, is 4%. They are also functionally uncorrelated: Cohen’s kappa, an agreement measure adjusted for agreement expected from the circuits’ individual response rates, is 0.015. Thus, the instability is not one mechanism described in different words.

We provide the filability criterion as a standalone protocol. We also report that one of the seven documented discovery objectives—the quantities the discovery procedure targets—does not execute on the library’s own canonical task. The study covers one model and one task. Whether the conclusion holds at scale is untested.

## 1 Introduction

An audit means two auditors reach the same conclusion. This is not a definition borrowed from machine learning. It is what makes an audit worth commissioning.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 requires providers of high-risk AI systems to file technical documentation. Annex IV point 2(b) asks for “the general logic of the AI system and of the algorithms”. Point 2(e) asks for “an assessment of the technical measures needed to facilitate the interpretation of the outputs”. Article 86(1) gives a person subject to certain automated decisions the right to “clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision taken”. These quotations come from the consolidated text as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, document identifier CELEX 02024R1689-20260727, read 11 August 2026.

Mechanistic interpretability offers to supply the technical content. Circuit discovery produces a subgraph said to explain a behaviour. It is the part of the field with mature tooling. If a provider files a circuit-level account of its system’s behaviour, that filing is worth something only if a second analyst, given the same system and tool, would file something compatible.

We measure how far a regulatory claim changes across defensible analytic specifications. We also provide a decision rule that a standards body could use. We do not propose a better circuit discovery method. We do not argue that interpretability is worthless. We argue that the evidence, as currently produced, does not meet the standard that filing it presumes.

#### Contributions.

(i) A pre-registered multiverse: an analysis across combinations of defensible choices. It covers seven analytic axes of circuit discovery. Every level comes from a published implementation. A deterministic map converts circuits into Annex IV statements.

(ii) A filability criterion, $\pi^{*}\geq 1-\alpha$, and its measured value on a canonical task. Here, $\pi^{*}$ is the share of specifications producing the most frequent claim. The tolerance $\alpha$ is the allowed share outside that claim.

(iii) Evidence that instability survives the most aggressive available controls: standardising any single axis, removing circuit size from the claim, and holding size fixed.

(iv) A methodological result for multiverse designs generally. When specifications select objects of different sizes, pooled statistics—statistics combining those sizes—can reverse direction. We show three such reversals.

## 2 Related work

#### Non-identifiability.

Non-identifiability means that the behaviour and explanatory criteria need not determine a unique explanation. Méloux et al. [6] ask whether a unique explanation exists for a given behaviour under mechanistic interpretability’s own criteria. They answer that it need not. Our premise is theirs. Our contribution is downstream: we measure what non-identifiability costs when an explanation is filed rather than published.

#### Faithfulness metric sensitivity.

A faithfulness metric measures how well a proposed circuit accounts for the model’s behaviour. Ablation-based measurement does this by removing or replacing internal activity. Miller et al. [8] survey design choices in such measurements. They find high sensitivity to seemingly insignificant changes. They conclude that faithfulness scores reflect the researcher’s methodological choices as well as the circuit.

They establish that one axis matters. We cross seven and propagate the results to a regulatory statement. We use their released library. Our study therefore stress-tests the tooling using its own definitions.

#### Structure versus function.

Bayat Makou et al. [1] vary input statistics while holding the task fixed. They find that structurally distinct circuits can implement the same computation. They call this pattern phantom specialisation.

This is the strongest objection to our design. If discovery merely samples from an equivalence class—a set of behaviourally identical subgraphs—then claim flips could measure only which member was sampled. We address this objection through a functional measurement in Section 7.6. Our circuits are not functionally interchangeable.

#### Variance as estimation.

Méloux et al. [7] treat mechanistic interpretability as statistical estimation and analyse the estimator’s variance, or variation in its output. Seed variance is one axis of our space. A seed controls which prompts are sampled here. Their framing is the natural statistical companion to our analysis of the whole specification.

#### Auditability.

Lan et al. [4] describe two papers that reached conflicting conclusions about the same behaviour. A third found both partly correct and incomparable. They call for auditing standards so findings can be certified in safety-critical settings. We answer that call with a measurement and criterion rather than a proposal.

Sharkey et al. [10] list open problems in the field, including the socio-technical problems this paper touches. Mueller et al. [9] argue that comparisons across methods require standardised evaluation. This is adjacent to our argument that certification requires standardised specification.

#### Multiverse method.

Our design follows Steegen et al. [13], who propose reporting results across defensible data-processing choices. It also follows Simonsohn et al. [12], whose specification curve—a display of results across specifications—we adapt.

Simmons et al. [11] named the underlying problem: undisclosed analytic flexibility lets an analyst present almost anything as significant. We apply this psychology methods literature to a machine learning evidentiary question. The transfer is substantive. A claim that an explanation is stable is exactly the kind of claim that flexibility can manufacture.

#### Explanation metrics under optimisation pressure.

Hsia et al. [3] show that sufficiency and comprehensiveness can be inflated without changing predictions or explanations. Sufficiency evaluates whether a selected explanation is enough to preserve behaviour. Comprehensiveness evaluates how much behaviour depends on what the explanation selects. These are two of our four evaluation metrics. This is one reason we report discard rates separately by metric rather than silently pooling them.

## 3 What this paper is not claiming

We state these limits early because the results do not support the following readings. We ourselves held two of them before running the decompositions.

We are not claiming that discovered circuits are no better than random. At fixed circuit size, their flip rate is lower than that of a size-matched random null, a comparison built from randomly selected circuits of the same sizes: 0.2746 against 0.4230 (Section 7.5). They carry information.

Nor are we claiming that filings differ where mechanisms do not. Mechanisms differ a great deal. Functional instability—the rate at which circuits disagree on whether individual examples are correct—is 0.3972 pooled and 0.4777 at the smallest circuits (Section 7.6).

Nor are we claiming that discovery samples one mechanism and describes it differently. Cohen’s kappa of 0.0146 says the circuits are functionally uncorrelated (Section 7.6).

Nor are random seeds the problem. Here, the seed selects which prompts are sampled. It therefore measures evaluation-set variability, not nondeterminism.

And we claim nothing about scale. This is one model and one task.

## 4 Formalism

A specification is a tuple of analytic choices:

$$
s=(o,a,d,m,\tau,P,r,g).
$$

The symbols mean:

- $o$: the discovery objective.
- $a$: the ablation operator, or rule for removing or replacing internal activity.
- $d$: the corruption distribution, which supplies altered inputs or activity used by an operator.
- $m$: the evaluation metric.
- $\tau$: the size threshold used in circuit selection.
- $P$: the prompt variant.
- $r$: the seed.
- $g$: the granularity, or level of detail in the claim.

Every level of every axis comes from a published implementation. None was invented for this study.

Circuit discovery returns $C(s)\subseteq E$. Here, $C(s)$ is the set of edges selected under specification $s$, and $E$ is the model’s full edge set. An edge is a connection in the computational graph. Vertical bars, as in $|E|$, denote the number of elements in a set.

For GPT-2 small, we use a factorised graph with separate query, key and value inputs: the three input types of its attention computation. This graph has $|E|=32{,}491$ edges. We confirm this count from the instrument and independently from a closed-form count over the computation order.

For block index $b$, attention receives from $1+13b$ sources at three input slots for each of 12 attention heads. An attention head is one attention component. The multilayer perceptron (MLP), a feed-forward component, in block $b$ receives from those sources plus its own block’s heads. The output receives from all 157 non-terminal sources. Circuits touch components drawn from 144 attention heads and 12 MLPs: 156 components in total.

#### Circuit instability.

For specifications $s_i$ and $s_j$, write $C_i=C(s_i)$ and $C_j=C(s_j)$. Their circuit distance is

$$
D(s_i,s_j)=1-\frac{|C_i\cap C_j|}{|C_i\cup C_j|}.
\tag{1}
$$

The intersection $C_i\cap C_j$ contains shared edges. The union $C_i\cup C_j$ contains edges in either circuit. Thus, $D$ is one minus Jaccard similarity. We write $\bar{J}$ for the mean Jaccard similarity over unordered pairs, counting each pair once.

#### Claim instability.

Let $\varphi$ be the claim map that converts a circuit into a structured Annex IV statement. The flip rate $F$ is the probability that two distinct specifications produce different statements:

$$
F=1-\frac{\sum_c n_c(n_c-1)}{N(N-1)}.
\tag{2}
$$

Here, $c$ indexes claim classes, $n_c$ counts specifications producing class $c$, $N$ is the number of specifications, and $\sum_c$ means to sum over all claim classes.

Equation 2 is the unbiased Gini–Simpson form: it gives the probability that two draws without replacement differ. We unit-test it against a brute-force calculation that checks every pair in randomised cases. The modal share is

$$
\pi^{*}=\max_c n_c/N,
$$

where $\max_c$ selects the largest claim-class count.

We also use a within-group flip rate. Let $G$ be a set of conditioning axes: axes whose levels must match within each comparison. Index the resulting groups by $g$, and let $n_{g,c}$ count specifications in group $g$ producing claim $c$. Let $n_g$ be the group’s total count. Then

$$
F_{\text{within}}(G)
=
1-\frac{\sum_g\sum_c n_{g,c}(n_{g,c}-1)}
{\sum_g n_g(n_g-1)}.
\tag{3}
$$

This weights every eligible pair equally, rather than every group equally. When $G$ is empty, it reduces exactly to Equation 2.

#### Filability.

Evidence is filable at tolerance $\alpha$ if and only if

$$
\pi^{*}\geq 1-\alpha.
$$

The flip rate has a ceiling: $F\leq 1-1/k$, where $k$ is the number of claim classes. A reported flip rate must therefore be read against the number of classes the map can produce. With the nine classes observed here, the ceiling is 0.8889. The observed 0.7316 is 82.3% of it. $F$ is not near one by construction.

## 5 The claim map

The claim map $\varphi$ is the paper’s most attackable choice. We therefore fixed it in the pre-registration, implemented it as deterministic code, and published it. It is not generated by a language model. That would add model variance to circuit variance without a way to separate them.

There are two addressees, following the regulation rather than convenience:

- $\varphi_{\text{overseer}}$ addresses the overseer. It is based on Annex IV 2(e) and 3, with Article 14(4)(c). It is keyed first on the dominant layer band—the grouping of model layers that the map identifies as dominant—then the size class, then the ranked input segment.
- $\varphi_{\text{affected}}$ addresses the affected person. It is based on Article 86(1). It leads with the input segment, or part of the input identified by the map, because the right belongs to the person the decision concerns.

The three granularities are COARSE, MEDIUM and FINE: increasingly detailed claim levels. They are nested by construction. A finer claim therefore refines, rather than contradicts, the coarser claim.

A reviewer may object that the size bins were chosen to produce the reported flip rate. They were instead returned by a committed calibration rule applied to a measured node-count curve: the measured number of components touched as circuit size changes. ## 6 Method

#### Model and task.

We use GPT-2 small on indirect object identification [15]. This choice is not an apology. The non-identifiability and faithfulness literature we extend works at this scale. Comparability is the point. The edge count also makes a size-matched random baseline meaningful.

#### Grid.

The pre-registered grid contains seven discovery objectives, seven ablation operators, four corruption distributions nested within the five operators that read them, four metrics, three thresholds, two prompt variants and five seeds. This gives 1,540 discovery cells and 18,480 specifications. A discovery cell is a combination of discovery settings whose outputs support the later metric-and-threshold specifications.

Corruption is nested rather than crossed with every operator. Two operators never read the corruption distribution. Crossing those operators with corruption levels would therefore produce duplicate specifications.

Discovery uses the edge attribution patching family [14] and integrated edge gradients, as implemented in the library released with Miller et al. [8]. These are edge-scoring discovery methods based on attribution and gradients, respectively. The automated circuit discovery lineage is Conmy et al. [2]. The four corruption levels come from Wang et al. [15].

The four metrics are comprehensiveness, sufficiency, Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and logit difference. KL divergence measures differences between output distributions. Logit difference compares pre-probability output scores.

#### Pre-registration.

The analysis plan was committed and tagged before the confirmatory sweep—the full pre-planned set of runs—began. Both abstracts were drafted in advance, so neither direction could be written up as a surprise. Deviations are recorded in an append-only file. Each entry includes its date and whether the decision preceded or followed inspection of the affected result.

#### Statistics.

We compute no $p$-value across specifications anywhere. A $p$-value would assess a result against a statistical null model. The specifications here are a designed grid, not an independent sample [13, 12].

Uncertainty comes from a nonparametric bootstrap: repeated resampling of the observed specifications without imposing a distributional model. It resamples specifications, never pairs, with $B=10{,}000$ bootstrap resamples. This is because $F$ and $\bar{J}$ are U-statistics over pairs, meaning statistics built by combining pair-level quantities. Pairs sharing a specification are dependent.

The intervals quantify uncertainty given the grid. They license nothing beyond it.

#### Two exclusions, criteria fixed before any circuit was seen.

One discovery objective does not execute. All 220 of its cells raise a runtime error inside the library’s own code. The error comes from computing mean squared error—the average squared difference between a prediction and target—against an integral, or integer-valued, target.

We verified the failure set using a digest, a content fingerprint, over the sorted failing cell identifiers. The set is exactly that objective’s arm and contains nothing else. We did not repair the error. The library is the instrument under test and is reproduced verbatim. The realised grid contains six objectives, 1,320 cells and 15,840 specifications.

The metric-relative threshold rule, which selects circuits relative to the evaluation metric, admits no circuit for 8,279 of 15,840 specifications. The discard rate is 52.3%. It varies sharply by metric (Table 2). This was disclosed before the pre-registration was locked. Comprehensiveness therefore makes up 52% of the surviving pool, against 25% by design. We report this composition rather than smoothing it.

### 7.1 Circuits are almost disjoint

Mean Jaccard similarity is $\bar{J}=0.1396$ (95% CI 0.1377 to 0.1418). We compute it exactly over all 28,580,580 specification pairs, rather than sampling pairs. The 7,561 specifications contain 3,218 distinct circuits. Reusing those distinct circuits makes exact computation tractable.

The distribution shows more than the mean. For pairwise distance $D$, the 1% quantile is 0.196, the 25% quantile is 0.788, the median is 0.960, and the 75% quantile is 0.998. A quantile is the value below which the stated share of observations falls. The median pair shares 4% of its edges. Only 1% of pairs exceed a Jaccard similarity of 0.8.

At the median circuit size of 200 edges, the analytic random reference $k/(2|E|-k)$ gives 0.0031. Here, $k$ denotes circuit size in edges. Observed overlap is therefore about 45 times chance. Beating chance at this scale is not evidence of much: a 500-edge circuit has expected random overlap of 0.008.

### 7.2 The claim flips on three quarters of pairs

The flip rate is $F=0.7316$ (95% CI 0.7247 to 0.7380). The pre-registered threshold was 0.20, with the interval’s lower bound required to exceed it (Table 3, H2). It clears that threshold by more than three times.

The modal share is $\pi^{*}=0.4109$. The evidence is therefore not filable at any pre-registered tolerance: 0.411 against requirements of 0.95, 0.90 and 0.80.

The evaluation metric is the largest single lever, but it is not enough. Standardising it completely, which is more than a conformity procedure could demand, leaves $F=0.5939$. The interval’s lower bound is 0.5803, still far above threshold (Table 6, Evaluation metric).

Different axes govern structure and claims. We use restricted maximum likelihood (REML), a method for estimating variance components, to decompose variation in $\log_{10}$ of selected circuit size. Here, $\log_{10}$ means the base-10 logarithm. The decomposition attributes:

- 78.8% of structural variance to the ablation–corruption interaction: their joint effect beyond separate effects;
- 11.5% to residual variance, left unexplained by the included components;
- 4.4% to ablation;
- 4.1% to the threshold;
- 0.7% to the metric.

Yet the metric is the largest driver of claim variance (Table 6). A standards body reading only the structural decomposition would standardise the ablation operator and remove almost none of the filing instability.

### 7.4 The result under maximum pressure

We remove every trace of circuit size from the claim. The statement then identifies only the dominant layer band. We also hold circuit size fixed using Equation 3.

The result is $F=0.2706$ (95% CI 0.2550 to 0.2859). The lower bound remains above the pre-registered threshold. Where in the model the behaviour is attributed still flips on 27% of pairs, even when size cannot contribute to the claim or vary within a comparison.

### 7.5 The random baseline, and why its pooled direction misleads

In the pooled comparison, the discovered multiverse is less stable than a size-matched random null drawn from the full 32,491-edge population. We use $R=1{,}000$ null multiverses, where $R$ is the number of random comparison multiverses. The flip rates are 0.7316 for discovered circuits and 0.6774 for the null. Their intervals are disjoint.

H3 is therefore rejected: the distributions are separated (Table 3, H3). We do not report the direction of that separation as a finding. It is an artefact of the size distribution, as Table 7 shows.

The null is degenerate above 1,000 edges: it can produce only one claim. A uniform draw, in which edges are selected with equal probability, touches all 156 components there. Holding size fixed reverses the ordering. At medium granularity, discovered circuits have a flip rate of 0.2746 against the null’s 0.4230. At coarse granularity, the rates are 0.2706 against 0.3822.

Once the comparison is fair, discovered circuits are more stable than random circuits. They are still not stable enough to file.

### 7.6 Filings differ more than mechanisms, over part of the range

Per-example functional agreement asks whether two circuits give the same correct-or-incorrect verdict on an example. We compute it using the measures of Bayat Makou et al. [1], reproduced verbatim.

Agreement is 0.6028. Functional instability, one minus agreement, is therefore 0.3972. The gap between the claim flip rate and functional instability is 0.3344 (95% CI 0.3297 to 0.3394). This exceeds the pre-registered threshold of 0.10 (Table 3, H4).

Cohen’s kappa is 0.0146. The 60% raw agreement is almost entirely explained by both circuits being right about the same fraction of examples, not the same examples. Kappa stays between 0.016 and 0.047 at every circuit size. Meanwhile, raw agreement rises from 0.52 to 0.92, tracking accuracy—the fraction of correct examples—from 0.51 to 0.95.

This answers the phantom specialisation objection. If discovery sampled from an equivalence class of behaviourally identical subgraphs, the circuits would be functionally interchangeable. At kappa 0.015, they are not.

H4 is supported on the pre-registered quantity. But the gap does not survive holding size fixed. It fails the 0.10 threshold at 6 of 10 sizes and is negative at 5. At both ends of the size range, mechanisms differ more than filings. The pooled figure is carried by the 500 to 2,000 edge band alone.

We report the pooled result because it was pre-registered. We report the decomposition beside it because the pooled result alone would mislead.

## 8 The filability protocol

Filability of an interpretability claim. For a filed claim $c$ derived from a circuit:

1. Declare the specification space $\mathcal{S}$: the analytic axes and all levels that a competent analyst could defend. Provide a citation for each level.

2. Declare the claim map $\varphi$ as deterministic, published code. Do not use a language model.

3. Compute $\pi^{*}=\max_c n_c/N$ over $\mathcal{S}$, and compute $F$ using Equation 2. Here, $n_c$ is the number of specifications producing claim $c$, and $N$ is the number producing claims.

4. The claim is filable at tolerance $\alpha$ if $\pi^{*}\geq 1-\alpha$.

5. Report $\pi^{*}$, $F$, the discard rate for each axis level, and the within-group decomposition of any pooled statistic.

A filing that cannot state its own $\pi^{*}$ has not been audited. It has been asserted. The protocol does not require the regulator to decide which analytic choice is correct. That is what makes it implementable. It requires only that the filer declare the space and report how much of it agrees.

## 9 Limitations

#### One model, one task.

Larger models have more components. Both the layer band and the size class therefore have more room. The direction of the effect on $F$ is not obvious. This is the limitation most likely to change the number.

A second-model replication is pre-registered but not yet run. It uses a 924-cell grid on Pythia-160m, a model from a different architecture family that performs the same task. Every axis is identical except the seed axis, which has three levels rather than five.

The analysis plan, reduced grid and criteria for replication rather than refutation were committed and timestamped before any cell ran. The result will be reported in a revision, whichever direction it takes. A second task remains designed and not run.

#### Circuit size is an outcome, not an axis.

The threshold rule chooses circuit size. Size has distorted three separate pooled quantities: $F$ itself, the direction of the null comparison, and the sign of the functional gap.

Fixing size reduces $F$ from 0.7316 to 0.2746 (Section 7.5). This is a larger reduction than standardising any pre-registered axis (Table 6). We take this to be a general hazard for multiverse designs whose specifications select objects of different sizes. We report every pooled statistic with its within-size decomposition.

#### The multiverse is as wide as the library, not the literature.

Optimal ablation [5], an ablation method, is a defensible operator that the instrument does not implement. It therefore never entered the space. The measured $F$ is thus a lower bound with respect to the field’s full set of defensible choices.

#### The discard rate is high and uneven.

The discard rate is 52.3%, and the surviving pool is imbalanced by metric (Table 2). The threshold rule was fixed in advance. No failing item was repaired. But this composition is a property of the reported set, and we state it rather than adjust for it.

#### The bootstrap is invalid for one reported family.

When conditioning groups contain only two to five specifications, retained self-pairs dominate. These arise when resampling retains repeated copies of the same specification. The intervals are unusable. We report those quantities as point estimates, without usable intervals, and attach the diagnostic.

We did not construct a bias-corrected estimator after seeing the naive intervals disagree. Choosing an estimator in response to a result is the behaviour this paper documents.

## 10 Discussion

#### What standardising one axis buys.

Less than it appears. The metric is the biggest lever. It removes 0.1377 from a flip rate of 0.7316 (Table 6, Evaluation metric). There is no single knob. A procedure that standardises one axis and declares the problem solved would be mistaken by a wide margin.

#### On the instrument.

One of seven documented, publicly exported discovery objectives does not run on the library’s own canonical task (Section 6, exclusions). We report this as a discard with its rate. We also report it as an observation about the maturity of tooling expected to support regulatory evidence.

This is not the paper’s headline. Nor is it a criticism of a library whose authors are among those calling for the field to address exactly this [10].

#### What a regulator can take from this.

Interpretability evidence is not worthless. Its evidentiary weight is a measurable property, not an assumed one. That measurement is cheap relative to the sweep that produced the circuit in the first place.

## 11 Reproducibility

The pre-registration is a timestamped commit—a recorded repository version—tagged `prereg-p1-confirmatory` in the public repository. It was made before the confirmatory sweep began.

Every reported number traces to a configuration, a seed and an environment fingerprint: a record identifying the execution environment. The confirmatory sweep used one environment across all 1,320 cells. We verified this from the results archive rather than merely asserting it.

We recovered the code commit by content-addressed comparison, which matches files by their contents, between the uploaded archive and the git tree, the repository’s recorded file structure. All 22 of 22 files were identical. The analysis layer runs without a graphics processing unit (GPU).

A verification script recomputes every headline quantity from raw results and checks it against the reported value. It performs 36 checks with 0 failures. It derives $F$ by three routes, including brute-force pairwise computation.

A second script checks that every numeric token in this manuscript matches a value derived from stored results. Every table is emitted directly from those results rather than transcribed. The test suite contains 343 tests.
