# Probe Generalization as Subspace Selection for OOD Deception Detection

Daniel Yoo

  
Adrians Skapars

###### Abstract

A linear probe is a classifier that reads information from a model’s internal numerical representations, or activations, using a weighted sum of those activations. Such probes can detect behaviors and concepts, but may fail on out-of-distribution (OOD) examples: examples from a distribution different from the training distribution.

We study probes for the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct language model on 3 held-out deception detection datasets. We project activations onto a small subset of principal components (PCs). PCs are mutually perpendicular directions that principal component analysis (PCA) identifies from variation in the training activations. This projection enables cross-domain transfer that nearly matches probes trained directly on the test distribution.

We also find that natural-language interpretations of PCs can identify some transferable PCs. A large language model (LLM) judge scores whether each PC’s most- and least-activating examples suggest a transferable deception direction. Training probes on the highest-scoring PCs closes 78% of the baseline-to-oracle gap on Insider Trading Report and 25% on Sandbagging (Section 4.5). Here, the baseline uses all activations, and the oracle selects PCs using labeled target examples.

Directions heavily weighted by a source-trained probe appear to encode source-specific surface features. Directions that transfer appear to encode the same contrast more abstractly, in ways that natural-language descriptions can capture. Broadly, our results suggest that OOD probe robustness is largely determined by subspace selection: choosing which activation directions the probe can use.

## 1 Introduction

Linear probes are standard tools for reading information from language model activations (Alain and Bengio, 2018; Hewitt and Liang, 2019). They are cheap to train. They also expose some semantically meaningful information in an otherwise uninterpretable representation space.

However, probes that perform well in-distribution—on examples drawn from their training distribution—can be unreliable OOD (Kirch et al., 2026). Existing work shows that key behavioral concepts are linearly represented in model activations (Marks and Tegmark, 2024; Park et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025). Probes should therefore have the capacity to detect these concepts. Yet, in practice, they appear to learn spurious correlations: associations that help on the training distribution but do not reliably transfer (Orgad et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). This is unacceptable for probe-based monitoring of harmful interactions. It raises a clear question: how can we build probes that learn only generalizing features?

We address this question using source-domain PCs rather than raw activation coordinates. The source domain is the distribution used to train the probe; a target domain is a different distribution used to test transfer. A basis is a set of directions used to express activation vectors. Using the source-domain PC basis avoids assuming that the model’s original activation coordinates form a privileged basis.

PCs offer a simple, data-driven way to study representation structure. Prior work also suggests that the choice of PCs matters. Mu et al. (2018) show that removing the top PCs of an embedding space—a numerical representation space—improves downstream performance. Kantamneni et al. (2025) use projections onto top PCs as a probe baseline. We therefore ask: which subset of PCs supports generalizable probe training, and how can that subset be identified?

We study deception probes on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024). We train on one source dataset, Roleplaying deception scenarios, and evaluate on 3 held-out target datasets: Insider Trading Report, Insider Trading Confirm, and Sandbagging (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025). In the source-domain PCA basis, we test ways to select training dimensions that avoid spurious correlations and transfer to target domains.

This work contributes the following findings:

- **A small transferable subspace exists (Section 4.2).** A target-supervised greedy search adds one source PC at a time using labeled target examples. It finds 8–15 components on which a source-trained probe nearly matches a target-trained probe.
- **Source-side and target-side rankings both fail to recover the subspace (Section 4.3).** Ranking PCs by source- or target-probe weights does not produce a basis that transfers better than the full-representation baseline.
- **An LLM judge partially recovers the subspace without target data (Section 4.5).** The judge can interpret and select PCs likely to encode transferable behavioral contrasts rather than surface patterns.

## 2 Related Work

#### Linear concept directions in activation space.

Related work asks whether high-level behavioral or semantic properties are represented as directions or low-dimensional subspaces in activation space. Marks and Tegmark (2024) report linear structure in true/false representations across datasets. Park et al. (2024) formalize the linear representation hypothesis—the idea that concepts are represented by linear directions—and connect it to probing and steering, or changing model behavior by intervening on activations. Zou et al. (2025) similarly model properties such as honesty and emotion as population-level directions that can be read from and intervened on in activations.

This perspective has also been used to explain and control behavior. Arditi et al. (2024) show that a single direction can mediate refusal behavior. This suggests that some high-level behaviors may be localized in simple linear features. Lu et al. (2026) identify an “assistant” axis corresponding to assistant-like behavior.

Together, these works suggest that linear structure can support mechanistic understanding and activation-space interventions. They also raise a question: when do these directions capture stable concepts rather than context-dependent correlations?

#### Linear probes and their limitations.

Linear and lightweight probes have a long history as tools for studying what information neural networks represent (Alain and Bengio, 2018; Hewitt and Liang, 2019). Recent work applies them to safety-relevant signals in LLM activations. Examples include latent truthfulness without direct supervision (Burns et al., 2024) and strategic deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025).

Methodological skepticism is also central to this literature. Hewitt and Liang (2019) argue that high probe accuracy alone does not establish that a model already represents the target property. The probe may itself learn the task or exploit surface-level correlates. Ravichander et al. (2021) similarly question whether probing establishes what information is encoded, rather than merely recoverable. Recent work also finds uneven OOD generalization despite strong in-distribution performance (kramár2026buildingproductionreadyprobesgemini). These concerns motivate testing whether safety probes identify robust internal signals rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

#### Interpreting features with natural-language explanations.

Another approach assigns natural-language descriptions to neurons or learned features. Bills et al. (2023) introduced LLM-generated neuron explanations based on high-activation examples. This approach has been extended to automatic feature labeling in sparse autoencoders (SAEs), models that reconstruct activations using sparsely active learned features ((Paulo et al., 2025), (Bricken et al., 2023)). This enables large-scale interpretation of learned feature dictionaries.

Recent work also tests whether natural-language feature descriptions can guide interventions. Casademunt et al. (2025), for example, identify concepts through automated natural-language interpretation and ablate them—remove their contribution—during fine-tuning to reduce misaligned behavior.

## 3 Setup

#### Datasets.

Following Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025), we use 4 deception detection datasets: Roleplaying, Insider Trading Report, Insider Trading Confirm, and Sandbagging. Appendix A describes them and gives label counts in Table 1.

Roleplaying is the source domain. The other 3 datasets are target test sets. GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) labels each response as honest (0), ambiguous (-1), or deceptive (1). We remove ambiguous responses from all experiments.

#### Language models.

Following Ying et al. (2026), we use layer 15 residual stream activations from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024). The residual stream is the model’s internal representation passed through its layers. Layer 15 had the highest cross-domain area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in their analysis. AUROC measures how well a classifier ranks deceptive examples above honest ones across classification thresholds.

To extract activations, we pass each prompt through the model. We average token activations only over the assistant’s response. Tokens are the text units processed by the model.

#### Probe training and evaluation.

We standardize source activations to zero mean and unit variance. We then standardize all target activations using those same source statistics.

We run PCA on the standardized source activations and retain the top 100 PCs, ordered by the variance they explain. All probes are logistic regression classifiers, which map a linear score to a class probability. Their regularization parameter is \(\lambda=10\).

For experiments using a subset \(S\) of the 100 PCs, \(S\) denotes the selected set of PC directions. We project activations onto \(S\), retaining only their coordinates along those directions, before training and evaluation. Baseline probes instead train directly on the full standardized activations.

We use the top 100 PCs because alignment with SAE decoder features—the directions used by an SAE to reconstruct activations—decreases with PC index (Appendix C, Figure 6). Lower-index PCs are therefore more amenable to natural-language interpretation and comparison with interpretability-driven selection methods. This is not a claim that low-index PCs generalize better. Section 4.1 checks that the 100-PC subspace retains enough information to separate deceptive from honest responses on every target.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setting a baseline

Figure 2: Probe AUROC on target datasets for source-trained probes on full representations (blue) and top-100 PCs (orange), and target-trained probes on top-100 source PCs (green, 5-fold CV). Error bars: 95% CIs

Here, CV means cross-validation: training and evaluation are repeated using different held-out portions, or folds, of the data. CIs are confidence intervals.

We first train probes on full source representations and evaluate transfer to each target. Figure 2 shows the results. Insider Trading Confirm transfers reasonably well, at 0.874 AUROC. Insider Trading Report performs moderately, at 0.706 AUROC. Sandbagging performs poorly, at 0.606 AUROC.

We also check whether the 100-PC subspace itself limits performance. Within that subspace, we train a probe directly on each target using 5-fold cross-validation. In the target order used here—Insider Trading Report, Insider Trading Confirm, and Sandbagging—the probe achieves 0.936 / 0.950 / 0.869 AUROC (Figure 2). Thus, the 100-PC subspace contains enough information to separate deceptive from honest responses on every target.

The difficulty therefore does not come from missing information. Rather, the source probe learns a direction in the subspace that does not generalize.

### 4.2 Showing a transferable subspace exists

Figure 3: Probe AUROC across the three targets for the full-representation baseline (blue), top-k PCs by variance (teal, k matched to greedy), and greedy oracle PCs (purple). Error bars: 95% CIs.

Here, \(k\) is the number of selected PCs.

Can a probe trained on source data close the performance gap between the baseline and target-trained probes? We test this with a greedy PC-selection algorithm.

The algorithm starts with an empty set. At each step, it considers adding each remaining PC among the top 100 source PCs. For each candidate subset, it projects source activations onto that subset and trains a logistic regression probe on those projections. It adds the PC that most improves target AUROC.

Probe weights are always fit on source data. PC selection, however, uses labeled target examples. We use 5-fold cross-validation over the target dataset. For each fold, greedy selection uses the non-held-out target folds, and the selected source-trained probe is evaluated on the held-out fold. Selection stops when no candidate improves AUROC by more than 0.001.

The search converges on small subsets: on average, 15 PCs for Insider Trading Report, 8 for Insider Trading Confirm, and 14 for Sandbagging.

Because selection uses target examples, the greedy probe provides an upper bound on transfer performance, not an unbiased OOD estimate. As a control, we select the top \(k\) PCs by source variance, matching \(k\) to the number selected by greedy. Figure 3 compares the methods.

Greedy selection comes close to a directly target-trained probe on Insider Trading Report and Sandbagging. It improves over the baseline by 0.22 and 0.23 points of AUROC, respectively (Figure 3). Thus, a transferable subspace exists in principle.

The variance control matches greedy only on Insider Trading Confirm, the easy case. It falls far behind on Insider Trading Report and Sandbagging, the harder transfer tasks (Figure 3). These results suggest that the choice of PCs matters, and that explained variance does not predict their usefulness.

### 4.3 Testing hypotheses for transferable principal components

Figure 4: Probe AUROC across the three targets for three PC-selection rules: ranking by source-probe weights (red), by target-probe weights (pink), and greedy oracle selection (purple). Error bars: 95% CIs.

What makes these PCs transferable? Can that property be detected using only source data? We test 2 hypotheses:

1. Transferable PCs are those that individually separate the target classes well across the test set. Greedy selection finds them because it uses target data.
2. Transferable PCs are those most heavily used by the source probe. Greedy selection merely recovers the source signal in fewer dimensions.

We compare 2 PC rankings. Both use standardized coefficients: the absolute value of a probe’s coefficient for a PC, multiplied by that PC’s standard deviation. The ranking probes are trained on activations expressed in the source-PCA basis.

The “source-PCA probe” ranking uses weights from a probe trained on source data. The “target-PCA probe” ranking uses weights from a probe trained on target data. Both use the source-PCA basis. After ranking, the probes evaluated for transfer are, in both cases, trained on source data projected onto the top \(k\) ranked PCs. If either hypothesis is correct, its ranking should recover greedy performance.

To set \(k\), we run greedy selection on the full target dataset rather than separately within folds. This gives one stable PC set per target: \(k=13\) for Insider Trading Report, 7 for Insider Trading Confirm, and 14 for Sandbagging. Because this uses the full dataset rather than cross-validation, the greedy numbers differ slightly from Section 4.2. Figure 4 shows the results.

Both hypotheses fail, but differently. The source-PCA probe transfers about as well as the full-representation baseline. The PCs on which the source probe relies are those that do not generalize (Figure 4). The target-PCA probe performs well on Insider Trading Report, but worse than greedy on Insider Trading Confirm. On Sandbagging, its transfer performance is below random (Figure 4).

Thus, the PCs that best separate target classes are not those that transfer best when their weights are learned from source data. Appendix D gives further PC-set comparisons.

### 4.4 Interpreting the transferable subspace

A small subset of source PCs supports cross-domain transfer. It differs from the directions used by source or target classification. Does it correspond to anything human-interpretable? We test this using the interpretation method of Bills et al. (2023) and Casademunt et al. (2025).

For each of the top 100 source PCs, we find the 8 most-activating and 8 least-activating source examples. We prompt an LLM to describe the positive pole, the negative pole, and the overall contrast. The poles are the opposite ends of the PC direction.

The LLM judge then rates each direction’s generalizability on a 1–10 scale, called the OOD score. Appendix B gives the judge model and full prompt. No PC scored above 6, so we treat 6 as the maximum observed score. We retain PCs receiving this maximum in both scoring runs. This yields 19 PCs.

There are 5 PCs selected by greedy for more than one target. Of these, 3 out of 5 receive the maximum OOD score: PC62, PC57, and PC86 (Appendix E, Table 3). This suggests that LLMs can partially recover transferable directions. By comparison, only 1 of the top 5 source-PCA-ranked PCs receives the maximum score: PC4 (Appendix E, Table 4).

Interpretations of greedy-selected PCs describe contrasts at an abstraction level that survives transfer to other domains (Appendix E, Table 3). Appendix E provides a full breakdown of PCs selected by each method.

### 4.5 Selecting a subspace by interpreting PCs

Figure 5: Probe AUROC across the three targets for the interp-selected subspace (red; 19 PCs) versus three controls: full-representation baseline (blue), top-19 PCs by variance (teal), and the remaining 81 PCs after ablating the interp-selected PCs (brown). Error bars: 95% CIs.

Can interpretation-based OOD scores recover a meaningful fraction of greedy performance without target data? We select PCs receiving the judge’s maximum OOD score and train a probe in that subspace. We call these PCs “interp-selected,” meaning selected through interpretation.

Because judge scores are noisy, we score twice and keep only PCs receiving the maximum in both runs. This gives 19 PCs. We compare them with a probe using the remaining 81 PCs—the “interp-ablated” control—and a probe using the top 19 source PCs by variance. Figure 5 shows the results.

The interp-selected probe outperforms the baseline on all 3 targets. It also outperforms the variance-selected control, by wide margins on Insider Trading Report and Sandbagging. As expected, it outperforms the interp-ablated probe on all 3 targets (Figure 5).

Recovery varies sharply by dataset:

- **Insider Trading Report:** interpretation-based selection closes 78% of the gap between baseline (0.706) and greedy oracle (0.929). The interp-selected result is 0.881 (Figure 5; Section 5).
- **Insider Trading Confirm:** baseline transfer is already near the ceiling, leaving little room for comparison. The result is therefore uninformative. All selection methods except interp-ablated perform roughly alike (Figure 5).
- **Sandbagging:** interpretation-based selection closes 25% of the gap to the greedy oracle (0.832). AUROC rises from the 0.606 baseline to 0.662 (Figure 5; Section 5).

Overall, selecting PCs solely from natural-language descriptions of what they encode, without target data, meaningfully improves baseline performance on the generalization tasks.

## 5 Limitations

Our strongest claims remain narrow. Readers should consider several limitations.

**Single model and source dataset.** All experiments use one model, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at layer 15, and one source dataset, Roleplaying (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025). Insider Trading Confirm is also uninformative because baseline transfer already reaches 0.874 (Section 4.1, Figure 2). Whether the findings generalize to other model families or datasets remains an open question for future work.

**Sandbagging margins are small.** Sandbagging gives the weakest results. Interpretation-based selection raises AUROC from 0.606 to 0.662, recovering only 25% of the gap to the greedy upper bound of 0.832 (Figure 5). Sandbagging is also the most OOD target, and the modest gain likely reflects this distribution shift. Insider Trading Report gives the more robust result: 0.706 to 0.881, closing 78% of the gap to greedy at 0.929 (Figure 5).

**Linear pipeline.** Our pipeline is fully linear: PCA projection followed by logistic regression. The existence of a transferable subspace is therefore perhaps unsurprising. Whether the findings extend to nonlinear probes or other readout locations—places in the model from which activations are extracted—remains open.

**No causal validation.** Claims about what each PC “encodes” rely on top-activating examples and an LLM judge’s description of their contrast. These claims are descriptive, not causal. We have not ablated high-scoring PCs during model inference to verify that they carry the contrast they appear to encode.

## 6 Conclusion

For probe-based deception monitoring, a small subset of source-domain PCs enables cross-domain transfer that nearly matches target-trained probes (Section 4.2, Figure 3). Source-side or target-side separability alone does not identify this subset (Section 4.3, Figure 4). An LLM judge’s interpretation score, based on each PC’s top-activating examples and no target data, partially recovers it (Section 4.5, Figure 5).

Robust probing therefore requires identifying not only whether a behavioral signal is linearly accessible, but where it is most robustly represented. Full-representation linear probes may combine general behavioral features with dataset-specific surface cues. These heuristics can support high in-distribution accuracy while hurting transfer. Sparse PC subspaces—subspaces using few PCs—may better isolate general behavioral structure.

This reframes OOD probing as a representation-selection problem. The goal is not only to fit a classifier. It is also to choose a subspace that preserves the intended behavioral feature while filtering out spurious correlation.

> This direction is bipolar:
>
> - Positive activations mean movement toward one pole.
> - Negative activations mean movement toward the opposite pole.
> - Larger magnitudes mean a stronger match to that pole.
>
> Each line is formatted as token\<TAB\>activation, with activations normalized to integers from -10 to +10.
>
> The examples below were selected from a dataset with this setup: The model is instructed to Roleplay in a simple scenario.  
> The model is asked a question which gives a strategic chance to lie.
>
> High-positive examples:
>
> {positive_examples}
>
> High-negative examples:
>
> {negative_examples}
>
> Infer the dominant contrast captured by the direction using both the positive and negative examples.
>
> Important instructions:
>
> - Do not assume the direction is a broad sentiment direction unless the examples clearly support that.
> - Prefer the narrowest accurate description over a broad generic one.
> - Do not overgeneralize beyond what is supported by the examples.
> - Rate how likely this direction is to be useful for out-of-distribution deception on a scale from 1 to 10, and briefly explain why.
> - Give low scores to directions that are mainly surface cues, lexical habits, or task-specific phrasing, even if they correlate with deception in these examples.
> - A direction that is noisy, brittle, context-dependent, or likely to fail under different wording or domains should usually be scored around 1 to 4, not 5 or above.
> - Reserve scores of 5 or higher for directions that seem plausibly tied to a more general deception-relevant strategy or behavior, rather than local wording.
>
> Write your answer in exactly this format:
>
> Positive pole: ...
>
> Negative pole: ...
>

>
