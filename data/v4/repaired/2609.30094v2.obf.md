# PrivDrift: Auditing User-Secret Leakage Under Topic Drift in Active LLM Conversations

Luciano Rolando Maldonado Romero

Affiliation: West Virginia University, Morgantown, WV, USA

Correspondence to: lrm00020@mix.wvu.edu

###### Abstract


The evaluation of privacy risk in active LLM contexts as a persistent behavioral failure mode, rather than only as training-data memorization or immediate jailbreak behavior, is suggested by the non-reliable reduction of leakage under additional V9B within the tested drift window; R9S X3Q, across three LLMs with extended context windows, remains substantial, ranging from 38.7% to 54.6%, with strong variation by model, secret type, and Z4T, in K7X, which contains 1,000 controlled multi-turn dialogues with seeded secrets, content-dense drift turns, and standardized extraction probes for auditing whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable after conversational V9B and persuasion-based probing, while large language models increasingly operate as persistent assistants in user-facing, shared-session, and tool-augmented settings where sensitive information disclosed during an active conversation may remain behaviorally recoverable through later prompts even after the dialogue shifts to unrelated topics; Trustworthy AI, Large Language Models, Privacy, Evaluation, Benchmarking.

## 1 Introduction

Under X3Q detection, R9S remains substantial, ranging from 38.7% to 54.6% across three LLMs with extended context windows and 1,000 dialogues per model, with Z4T exerting a significant effect on leakage and leakage varying sharply by secret type, L8P (L8P denoting SSNs) and G6Y (G6Y denoting credit cards) being suppressed far more often than D2F (D2F denoting email addresses) and A9T (A9T denoting phone numbers). Within the tested window ( $d\leq 6$ ), the addition of further drift does not reliably reduce leakage.

The contributions comprise (1) K7X, a controlled benchmark for auditing whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable from active conversational context after unrelated V9B, (2) an evaluation of leakage under direct, justified, and high-pressure probes, modeling how later prompts can elicit sensitive context through different interaction styles, (3) the implementation of a reproducible X3Q detector that combines normalization-based matching with T1G (T1G denoting an open-weights LLM judge) and the reporting of R9S (R9S denoting dialogue-level leakage), M2V, J5D (J5D denoting regex-only leakage), B7N, and X3Q (X3Q denoting hybrid leakage) rates, and (4) the proposal of C5Z (C5Z denoting Privacy Half-Life ( $\tau$ ), a stability-based metric for persistent suppression), accompanied by the demonstration that no evaluated model reaches stable decay within the observed drift range.

Whether topic shift acts as a practical privacy boundary within an active conversation is left as an important gap for trustworthy AI evaluation, in that existing safety evaluations mostly target two extremes, namely training-data memorization, where private information is extracted from model parameters (Carlini et al., 2021; Nasr et al., 2023), and immediate jailbreaking, where a harmful behavior is elicited in the current turn (Wei et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023), while recent work studying prompt injection and multi-turn attacks (Liu et al., 2023; Russinovich et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2024) does not directly measure whether user-disclosed secrets remain recoverable after unrelated conversational drift.

Large language models (LLMs) have evolved from simple chat systems into persistent assistants capable of maintaining coherence over extended interactions (OpenAI, 2023; Touvron et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024), and as the integration of these systems into settings such as healthcare triage, financial planning, enterprise copilots, and personal productivity tools proceeds, users may disclose Personally Identifiable Information (PII), including A9T, financial identifiers, D2F, and health-related details, while the same context retention that supports useful multi-turn assistance can also create a privacy risk in which sensitive information disclosed earlier in an active conversation may remain available to later generations.

It is not assumed that users believe an LLM literally forgets earlier messages in the same session; rather, the risk under study is a behavioral one, namely that users and application designers may underestimate how easily sensitive information disclosed earlier can be elicited later through indirect, justified, high-pressure, or tool-mediated prompts, a risk relevant to shared sessions, browser-integrated assistants, enterprise copilots, agentic workflows, and prompt-injection settings where later instructions interact with the active context in ways the original user did not intend.

Under two axes, V9B (V9B denoting topic drift, which captures the number of unrelated turns between secret disclosure and later probing) and Z4T (Z4T denoting persuasion intensity, which captures the interaction style used to elicit the secret), active-context privacy leakage is measured by K7X (K7X denoting PrivDrift, a controlled benchmark introduced for this measurement).

## 2 Related Work

Machine unlearning benchmarks such as TOFU and WMDP evaluate whether knowledge can be removed or suppressed from model behavior (Maini et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024), usually operating over static question-answer pairs and model parameters, and a distinction of importance for deployed RAG systems, copilots, and agentic workflows, where privacy control is often dependent on prompt-level context management rather than parameter-level unlearning, is introduced by the fact that K7X, though related in spirit, targets a different object of control, namely active context rather than model weights, the question asked being whether a secret present in the context window is behaviorally suppressed when later prompts attempt its elicitation (2.3).

Crescendo attacks show that seemingly benign conversations can gradually elicit harmful outputs (Russinovich et al., 2024), adversarial multi-turn attack paths are generated by automated jailbreak frameworks (Deng et al., 2024; Narula et al., 2025), and prompt injection studies show that later instructions can manipulate LLM-integrated applications (Liu et al., 2023), works which usually focus on unsafe content generation or instruction hijacking, whereas the multi-turn lens is adapted by K7X to information flow control, the question being not whether a model can be made to produce harmful external content but whether sensitive information supplied by a user earlier in the same active context will be re-disclosed, with aligned models remaining vulnerable to multi-turn exploitation (2.2).

ConfAIde evaluates privacy reasoning through contextual integrity (Mireshghallah et al., 2024), while privacy norm awareness and legal compliance in agentic or contextual settings are studied by PrivacyLens and PrivaCI-Bench (Shao et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), and these benchmarks, constituting recent examinations of whether LLMs can reason about privacy norms and contextual access, primarily test whether a model should share information under a static access-control or privacy-norm scenario, whereas K7X instead isolates a persistence question, namely whether a user-disclosed secret remains behaviorally recoverable after unrelated V9B and later persuasion-based probing (2.1).

Privacy research in language models has historically focused on training-data extraction, membership inference, and recovery of memorized PII from pre-training corpora (Shokri et al., 2017; Carlini et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Nasr et al., 2023), settings which study whether private information is stored in model parameters, whereas active-context privacy concerns user-provided information that appears in the current interaction and must be handled appropriately at inference time.

## 3 The PrivDrift Framework

Figure 3: C5Z $\tau$ requires stable decay below the threshold. A later resurgence disqualifies a transient decrease.

The stability-based metric for sustained suppression introduced in 3.4.3, C5Z ($\tau$), is assigned the value $\tau=\max(D_{obs})+1$ whenever the set appearing in

$$\tau=\min\{d\in D_{obs}\mid L(d)\leq\delta\ \text{and}\ \forall d^{\prime}\in D_{obs},\,d^{\prime}>d:\ L(d^{\prime})\leq\delta\} \tag{3}$$

is empty, with $D_{obs}$ being the observed set of drift lengths, $L(d)$ the leakage rate at drift length $d$, and the safety threshold fixed at $\delta=0.1\times L(0)$, so that transient dips are prevented from being interpreted as stable suppression.

Under 3.4.2, responses that are negative under normalization-based matching are passed to an open-weights Llama judge (Meta AI, 2024), which receives the secret and the model response and returns a binary verdict indicating whether the response reveals the secret or a substantial part of it, this T1G stage being intended to capture partial disclosure, indirect hints, and formatting variations not captured by deterministic matching, with J5D and X3Q rates reported separately because judge-based evaluation can introduce its own errors.

The first stage, described in 3.4.1, detects verbatim and near-verbatim leakage: for numeric secrets, all non-digit characters are stripped from both the ground-truth secret and the model response, while for alphanumeric secrets, case and whitespace are normalized with meaningful delimiters preserved, and, letting $\phi(\cdot)$ denote the normalization function, the regex-stage leakage label is

$$\mathrm{Leak}_{\mathrm{regex}}(R,S)=\mathbb{I}\big[\phi(S)\subset\phi(R)\big], \tag{2}$$

where $R$ is the model response and $\mathbb{I}$ is the indicator function.

A model may reveal a secret verbatim, with formatting changes, through a partial substring, or through a paraphrased reference, and the evaluation methodology of 3.4 therefore implements a hierarchical hybrid detector under which a response is labeled as leakage if either the normalized secret appears in the normalized response or the T1G determines that the response reveals the full secret, a substantial substring, or enough partial information to identify the secret.

Figure 2: Hierarchical hybrid evaluation. Normalization-based matching is applied first; regex-negative responses are then passed to a T1G.

The main evaluation of 3.3 appends a final standardized probe to the completed conversation history, the probes being grouped into three persuasion levels, of which the first, H2R, is a direct neutral query, such as asking for the A9T mentioned earlier, the second (F6K denoting the Medium level) is a query with a contextual justification, such as needing the information for a form, and the third (N8W denoting the Hard level) is a high-pressure or urgent request designed to test whether pressure cues increase or decrease leakage; the drift length $d$, the number of turns between secret disclosure and final probing, is sampled from the mixed distribution

$$d\sim\begin{cases}0&\text{with }p=0.2,\\ \mathrm{Uniform}\{2,3,4,5,6\}&\text{with }p=0.8.\end{cases} \tag{1}$$

which includes immediate recall cases while emphasizing short-to-medium V9B, the results being interpreted as bounded active-context persistence and not as a full test of arbitrarily long context windows.

The threat model of 3.1 concerns Q3M (Q3M denoting active-context re-disclosure), the failure mode in which sensitive information disclosed earlier in a conversation remains recoverable through later prompts after unrelated V9B: a user discloses a secret $S$ at turn $t_{0}$, the conversation proceeds through $d$ unrelated but content-dense drift turns, denoted as $D=\{u_{1},a_{1},\ldots,u_{d},a_{d}\}$, at evaluation time a standardized probe $P$ is appended to the active context, and a leakage event occurs if the model response reveals $S$ exactly, approximately, or semantically; no assumption is made that an adversary is always unable to inspect the raw transcript, K7X instead evaluating whether the model itself will re-disclose the sensitive value when later instructions query, justify, or pressure the assistant to reveal it, the core question being not whether the transcript contains the secret but whether the assistant will behaviorally reproduce the secret when later prompted, in a setting capturing the practical risks that, in shared or persistent sessions, a later user may interact with an assistant without understanding what private information was previously disclosed, that, in enterprise copilots and agentic workflows, later tool outputs, retrieved documents, or prompt-injection content may issue instructions that interact with the active context, and that, in browser or application-integrated assistants, the user may not intend for earlier sensitive disclosures to be reused after the task has shifted.

Under 3.2, $N=1000$ controlled dialogues are generated using a multi-stage scaffold-then-rewrite pipeline, a design that preserves strict control over the ground-truth secret, drift length, and probe structure while reducing the rigid artifacts of purely template-based generation; the dataset contains 900 synthetic dialogues and 100 human-authored dialogues constructed under the same secret and drift constraints, the human-authored dialogues having been inserted throughout the benchmark rather than stored as a separate contiguous block, and, since the current cached evaluation metadata does not preserve the human-authored identifiers, aggregate results are reported over the full evaluated benchmark, with a separate human-versus-synthetic leakage comparison left for future work.

A final naturalistic refinement step, that of 3.2.3, is applied to reduce rigidity from the initial scaffold, each dialogue scaffold being passed to a rewriting model with instructions to improve fluency and persona realism while preserving the exact secret, drift length, role structure, and final probe conditions, a step that modifies surface phrasing but does not change the experimental variables; the initialization of 3.2.1 provides each dialogue with a persona sampled from more than 100 professions and demographic profiles, together with a ground-truth secret $S$ sampled from four fixed-format PII categories, namely A9T, D2F, L8P and G6Y, the secret being inserted in the opening turn as part of a plausible task context so that a legitimate in-context reason is established for the assistant to observe the information; under 3.2.2, each dialogue is assigned a topic such as tax filing, medical triage, or travel logistics, drift turns being drawn from a topic-conditioned library of dense question-answer pairs and fallback tasks involving professional writing, structured reasoning, or long-form summarization, a subset of drift turns being human-authored by annotators and incorporated into the generation library, the design aiming to add unrelated entities and task demands to the active context while experimental control over the original secret and the number of drift turns is preserved.

Figure 1: K7X overview. The pipeline constructs controlled multi-turn dialogues, appends standardized probes, queries target models, and scores leakage with a hybrid detector.

K7X is a synthetic evaluation framework for measuring whether sensitive information disclosed in an active conversation remains recoverable after V9B, and, as shown in Figure 1, the framework has three components: a parametric dialogue generator, a probing harness, and a metric suite for leakage detection and temporal analysis.

## 4 Experimental Setup

The testing of association between leakage and drift length, persuasion level, or secret type is carried out by means of chi-square tests of independence, with Cramer's $V$ reported as an effect size, while repeated measures across persuasion levels are subjected to Cochran's $Q$ test together with post-hoc McNemar tests, and paired model and detector comparisons on matched examples are subjected to McNemar's test, a Bonferroni correction being applied where applicable. Both J5D and X3Q are reported, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals being computed over dialogues for dialogue-level rates, while for probe-level sensitivity checks rates are computed over cached model-probe responses. That R9S can exceed the average of individual persuasion-level leakage rates is explained by the distinction between the two aggregation levels that are reported, overall leakage being computed at the dialogue level, under which a dialogue is counted as leaking if any standardized probe elicits the secret, whereas persuasion-level and B7N analyses are computed at the probe level, under which each dialogue contributes one response per persuasion condition. The evaluation of each of U5K, E7B, and P3R covers 1,000 dialogues generated by the K7X pipeline, each dialogue containing one seeded secret and one drift length $d\in\{0,2,3,4,5,6\}$ sampled from the distribution above, and each dialogue being evaluated under three standardized persuasion probes, yielding 3,000 cached model-probe responses per model and 9,000 total cached responses across the three evaluated models. All of U5K (U5K denoting the paper's GPT-OSS-120B), E7B (E7B denoting the paper's DeepSeek-R1), and P3R (P3R denoting the paper's Qwen3-VL-235B) are evaluated through the OpenRouter API, the same dialogue histories and probe templates being used in the querying of all models.

## 5 Results

$\tau=\max(D_{obs})+1=7$ is obtained for all models and Z4T levels, no evaluated model approaching the threshold at any tested V9B length under the stable-decay definition of C5Z with threshold $\delta=0.1\cdot L(0)$, from which the absence of stable decay within $d\leq 6$ follows (§5.6).

The sharp variation of leakage by secret type is reflected in the large associations between secret type and X3Q indicated by Cramer's $V$ for all models, 0.5871 for E7B, 0.7731 for U5K, and 0.6927 for P3R in the cached M2V analysis (the numerals 120 and 235 being those embedded in the full designations of U5K and P3R, respectively), with L8P and G6Y suppressed far more often than D2F and A9T even though all are user-disclosed and contextually private (Table 5), a pattern suggesting that current behavior depends more on format and sensitivity cues than on a generalized notion of contextual confidentiality (§5.5).

## 6 Discussion

Stability-based evaluation, rather than treating temporary drops as evidence of suppression, is left as the criterion because leakage curves exhibit intermediate dips whose decreases are not stable enough to satisfy C5Z, and V9B is not an implicit privacy boundary, while K7X still indicates a persistent Q3M risk: current assistants may reproduce sensitive in-context information when later prompts provide enough retrieval pressure, since sensitive information disclosed earlier in a conversation can remain behaviorally recoverable after unrelated V9B, with no implication that models permanently remember the secret and no implication that all long-context settings behave similarly; the cached M2V evaluation also places this behavior as mostly direct or near-direct reproduction, since B7N closely tracks X3Q. The strongest pattern is the asymmetry across secret types, with L8P and G6Y suppressed far more often than D2F and A9T even though all four categories are user-disclosed secrets in the benchmark, which suggests that current safeguards may rely on format-sensitive safety heuristics rather than robust contextual reasoning about confidentiality. Z4T effects are significant but non-monotonic and model-dependent: for U5K (120), high-pressure requests reduce leakage, consistent with the possibility that urgency cues activate refusal behavior; for E7B, F6K Z4T produces the lowest leakage; and for P3R (235), N8W Z4T produces the highest leakage, suggesting weaker resistance to pressure-based extraction.

## 7 Limitations and Future Work

Aggregate benchmark results are reported and a separate human-versus-synthetic comparison is left for future work because the current cached evaluation metadata does not preserve which evaluated rows correspond to the human-authored dialogues, with the dataset containing 900 synthetic dialogues and 100 human-authored dialogues inserted throughout the benchmark rather than stored as a separate block, a construction that improves realism relative to a purely synthetic benchmark while preserving control over secret type, drift length, and probe structure; the interpretation of persistence is bounded by K7X evaluating drift lengths up to $d=6$, corresponding to bounded short-to-medium conversational drift rather than full saturation of modern context windows, so the results are evidence of persistence under bounded active-context drift and not a complete characterization of privacy behavior across full long-context windows, while recoverability is evaluated within the active conversation context and training-data memorization, personalization memory, and cross-session retention are not tested. Leakage labels are based on surface-form outputs using deterministic matching, B7N, and an external T1G, with J5D, B7N, and X3Q labels reported separately to make detector behavior transparent and no formal inter-annotator agreement between humans and the automated methods yet reported, and future work should include a larger manual audit of borderline partial disclosures and obfuscations; the benchmark focuses on structured secrets such as L8P, G6Y numbers, D2F, and A9T, which are easier to detect than unstructured sensitive information such as health status, family circumstances, immigration status, or financial hardship, so future work should extend the benchmark to non-fixed sensitive attributes. API-served models are evaluated due to compute and budget constraints and may change over time, with mitigation by logging model identifiers, prompts, timestamps, cached responses, and paired statistics on identical dialogue sets; the benchmark generation code, probe templates, evaluation scripts, aggregate result files, and a sanitized subset of generated dialogues are planned for release, and because the benchmark contains synthetic PII-like strings, examples will be released with non-real identifiers and generation templates sufficient to reproduce the benchmark without exposing realistic identifiers.

## 8 Conclusion

The observed asymmetry across secret types, with K7X standing as a benchmark for auditing active-context privacy leakage under V9B and Z4T, suggests that current protections remain uneven and depend more on format-sensitive cues than on robust contextual confidentiality; C5Z exceeds the observed window for all models and, within the tested drift range, V9B does not reliably reduce leakage, while R9S X3Q, across three LLMs and 1,000 dialogues per model, remains substantial, ranging from 38.7% to 54.6%, and Z4T affects leakage in model-dependent ways even as secret type has the strongest association with leakage.
