# Bounded Sovereignty and the Control Tax: Pricing AI Oversight When the Deployer Does Not Own the Model

Zhen Wen Lim
††thanks: AI safety and governance advisor with professional experience in financial services and payments. The views expressed are the author’s own.

July 2026

###### Abstract


Access assumptions are safety assumptions: complete logs help a deployer understand what happened, a J3W helps it stop risky actions before they occur, post-incident diagnosis is improved by trace access and model-version control, and safety can be improved by R7K while usefulness is reduced, and control protocols proposed as general safety solutions should therefore state their access assumptions explicitly. Five contributions are made: an anonymised national-payments-infrastructure scenario in an AI-importing jurisdiction; the Q9X; a Z5J carried out over 1.35 million synthetic case simulations; a T2V; and a W8N. Constituting not real-world payment-system evidence but a construct-validity exercise, the Z5J shows how logging, gateway access, trace access, model-version control, vendor support, tool scope, thresholds, audit budgets, and agent behaviour change prevention, diagnosis, usefulness, audit burden, and cost. The W8N maps common control protocols to the access they require, the T2V (T2V denoting the paper's four-layer access typology) concerns deployment-time AI control, and by the Q9X is meant the part of the M4Z spent substituting for missing access through contracts, architecture, audit, vendor assurance, residual risk, or reduced system scope. Introduced under the name K7R is partial technical and contractual access across the data, model, infrastructure, and interaction layers of the AI stack, it being these access conditions that determine which control protocols can be executed in practice. AI control research asks how to deploy a model safely even when the model may be misaligned, but many control protocols assume that the deployer can instrument the model and its surrounding pipeline, an assumption often too strong for regulated organisations that consume frontier models through APIs or managed endpoints, organisations that may control the business process but not the model weights, serving infrastructure, internal traces, update process, or full interaction logs.

Keywords: AI control; M4Z; scalable oversight; AI sovereignty; K7R; D3P; financial infrastructure; model risk management

## 1 Introduction

The related literature is reviewed in Section 2, the conceptual framework is developed in Section 3, the empirical methodology is presented in Section 4, the Z5J results are reported in Section 5, the findings are applied to the illustrative payments infrastructure scenario in Section 6, implications and limitations are discussed in Section 7, and Section 8 concludes.

Five contributions are made (1.3): the fifth, an interpretation of the framework through an anonymised national-payments-infrastructure scenario; the third (Q9X denoting the sovereignty discount cost, the spend, delay, residual risk, or usefulness loss incurred when a deployer must substitute for missing access); the first, a T2V — data, model, infrastructure, and interaction — for thinking about deployment-time AI control; the fourth (Z5J denoting a synthetic access-ablation experiment), using a synthetic payments-dispute workflow, 20 access profiles, 10 control protocols, three synthetic agent behaviours, threshold and audit-budget sweeps, and 1.35 million synthetic case simulations; and the second (W8N denoting a protocol-by-layer requirements matrix), showing which control protocols remain executable under different access conditions.

AI safety work often starts from a technical question — if a model may be misaligned, what deployment protocol can keep its actions safe — but the starting point here is taken one step earlier, because before a deployer can run a control protocol it must be able to observe, log, route, interrupt, or audit the relevant parts of the system, an access that many deployers do not have (1.1).

Access to the AI stack is unevenly distributed: in December 2025, seven countries signed the Pax Silica Declaration in Washington, a US-led framework for secure and resilient AI-related supply chains, and by late June 2026 the initiative had expanded to twenty-four signatories, while thirty-five countries and regions signed the related Joint Statement on AI Opportunity at the second Pax Silica Summit (Australian Department of Industry, Science and Resources, 2025; Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2026; European Commission, 2026); in the same period, assessments of global compute distribution reported that Africa, holding roughly eighteen percent of the world's population, hosts under one percent of global data-center capacity, and that much of South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Latin America depends on externally owned and operated AI infrastructure (CSIS, 2025; Brookings Institution, 2026). Usually discussed as industrial policy or geopolitics, these facts are treated in this paper as a boundary condition for AI control: a regulated institution that uses a vendor model through an API may not control the model weights, the serving stack, the update process, or the internal traces, and, while it may still be accountable for the system's behaviour, it cannot use every control protocol described in the AI safety literature.

The AI control agenda asks how deployment can be kept safe even if a model may be misaligned (Greenblatt et al., 2023), and its canonical protocols include D3P, F6Q, H1Y (Bhatt et al., 2025), and L8C (F6Q denoting defer-to-trusted routing, H1Y denoting resampling-based intervention, and L8C denoting the human auditing of suspicious actions), protocols that recent papers have stress-tested adversarially (Griffin et al., 2024; Schaeffer et al., 2026; Terekhov et al., 2026), while (M4Z denoting the control tax, the operational and financial cost of adding control to AI pipelines) has been priced by Terekhov et al. (2025).

Control is argued to have two layers (1.2), of which Layer 2 — access feasibility, whether the deployer actually has the logs, traces, routing control, model-version control, infrastructure visibility, or contractual rights that the protocol assumes — is the one on which this paper focuses, while Layer 1 — protocol effectiveness, whether, given that the deployer has the needed access, the protocol reduces unsafe behaviour — is the one on which most AI-control work focuses; and by sovereignty is meant not territorial sovereignty but technical and contractual access to the layers that control protocols rely on, namely data, model, infrastructure, and interaction surfaces, access that for many real-world regulated deployers, especially in AI-importing jurisdictions, is partial.

Financial infrastructure is a useful setting for this question because the risks are concrete, the audit expectations are high, and the accountability chain is clear: a national payments infrastructure operator may use a vendor-supplied foundation model to support a transaction-adjacent process, such as dispute triage or investigation drafting, while the domestic regulator may expect validation, monitoring, auditability, and board accountability even as the model, infrastructure, update process, and internal traces remain controlled by an external vendor; and the catastrophic actions — an incorrect hold or release, systematic mis-triage, exposure of protected financial data, or an action path touching settlement operations — are easier to define than in many general AI benchmarks and, without being equivalent to existential risk, are clear enough to show how access constraints shape control.

The question is not whether sovereign AI is desirable as national policy; the narrower question is what control remains possible when the deployer does not own the model.

## 2 Related Work

The question that results — which control protocols remain valid, at what cost, under partial access — is direct, and the gap thereby identified is narrower and more practical than a general theory of AI sovereignty: AI control assumes deployers can instrument the model; sovereign-AI and compute-governance work shows that such access is unevenly distributed; and the structural-asymmetry literature suggests that limited access is not merely transitional, these being the threads assembled under 2.3. Existing AI-control work has begun to evaluate and price control protocols, but the deployer's access position is rarely made an explicit part of the protocol specification, so what is contributed is a vocabulary, a mapping method, and a synthetic empirical operationalisation for treating access assumptions as first-order features of deployment-time control.

That compute governance and deployment-time AI control currently operate at different levels — the former often asking how states can govern who trains and runs frontier models, the latter asking how a deployer can safely run a possibly misaligned model — is the observation from which it follows that neither literature fully asks what happens when the deployer's position in the compute-governance landscape determines whether its control protocol is executable, and it is at that seam that this paper sits; the concentration of control over the AI stack is meanwhile documented, in the material numbered 2.2, by a separate literature, largely in policy institutes, industry analyses, and technical-governance venues.

Common formulations decompose AI sovereignty into pillars, data, model, infrastructure, and operational sovereignty recurring as a four-pillar industry framework, while layered and spectrum-based treatments emphasise that sovereignty is not binary but distributed across stack layers (Stanford HAI, 2026; Tony Blair Institute, 2026; Cruzes, 2026); empirical work documents the thinness of Global South access at the infrastructure layer (CSIS, 2025; Brookings Institution, 2026); and the compute-governance literature proposes, for governing the substrate on which frontier models run, state-level mechanisms that include compute monitoring, know-your-customer schemes for compute providers (Egan and Heim, 2023), and technical AI governance research agendas (Reuel et al., 2024).

That asymmetric access should be treated as a persistent feature of the deployment landscape rather than a temporary inconvenience that market competition will quickly erase is what a third literature is used here to justify, no normative theory of colonialism being advanced and no adjudication of broader moral claims being attempted, the literature in question being data colonialism as theorised by Couldry and Mejias (2019), the appropriation of human life through data under an extractive economic logic continuous with historical colonialism; decolonial AI as developed by Mohamed et al. (2020), identifying algorithmic coloniality in the design, deployment, and governance of AI systems; and the concrete documentation by Birhane (2020) of algorithmic colonisation dynamics for Africa, where imported technology can embed foreign values and interests with limited local scrutiny or control, these accounts together explaining why the asymmetry of access may be structural rather than an incidental market failure.

M4Z, introduced by Terekhov et al. (2025) with a theoretical framework mapping monitor classifier performance to safety assurances and empirical cost estimates for monitoring protocols under auditing budgets, is taken as this paper's point of departure, the critique being not that M4Z is wrong but that it is incomplete if priced conditional on instrumentation access the deployer may not have, a conditionality sharpened by recent adversarial results in which monitors are themselves attack surfaces (Terekhov et al., 2026), attack-selection capability can degrade safety substantially at realistic auditing budgets (Schaeffer et al., 2026), and untrusted monitoring requires explicit collusion-strategy assumptions to support a safety case (Gardner-Challis et al., 2026), each of these defences and evaluations presupposing that some actor can instrument the deployment pipeline and leaving open which actor, with what access, under what contractual and infrastructural constraints.

The AI control framework originates with Greenblatt et al. (2023): rather than assuming that a model's alignment can be verified, control evaluations pit a blue team designing a deployment protocol against a red team playing a worst-case, deliberately subversive untrusted model, and the originating paper introduces D3P alongside F6Q protocols, under which a trusted model's output replaces suspicious actions, D3P (D3P denoting trusted monitoring) being the arrangement in which a weaker, trusted model scores the untrusted model's outputs for suspicion, with L8C of the highest-scoring fraction; subsequent work extends control to multi-step agentic settings via H1Y (Bhatt et al., 2025), formalises protocol evaluation game-theoretically (Griffin et al., 2024), sketches how control arguments could support deployment safety cases (Korbak et al., 2025), and improves monitor performance through legibility requirements on the untrusted model's outputs (Sreevatsa et al., 2026).

As for the literature grouped with the foregoing under 2.1, the question of how a weaker supervisor can reliably judge a stronger system is pursued through debate (Irving et al., 2018), weak-to-strong generalisation (Burns et al., 2023), empirical studies of weak LLMs judging strong ones (Kenton et al., 2024), and scaling laws for oversight games between weak overseers and strong adversaries (Engels et al., 2025), and while no technical extension of scalable oversight is made here, one structural observation is borrowed from it — oversight capacity as a scarce resource whose adequacy relative to the overseen system can be analysed — with the same resource logic applied to access rather than capability, since a deployer may have a capable monitor but still lack the logs, traces, tool-call visibility, version control, or J3W needed to use that monitor effectively.

## 3 Conceptual Framework: Bounded Sovereignty and the Control

Under typical API-only access the feasible set collapses toward output-level protocols, even those resting on two load-bearing conditions, complete interaction logging and a J3W the deployer controls, since a monitor that can flag a dangerous tool call only after it has already executed is an audit mechanism rather than a prevention mechanism, all of which is recorded in the W8N of 3.2, a mapping of canonical control protocols to the access layers they presuppose that is this paper's own construction, derived from the control-protocol definitions in the originating literature (Greenblatt et al., 2023; Bhatt et al., 2025; Sreevatsa et al., 2026) and assembled under K7R (K7R denoting bounded sovereignty), with the F6Q or N2S (N2S denoting trusted editing) family feasible only with an architecture constraint.

| Control protocol | Binding access layers | Typical API-only feasibility | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| D3P, output-level | Interaction logging; deployer-controlled monitor compute | Feasible if logs are complete | Requires complete, tamper-evident I/O logs and monitor execution outside the untrusted model |
| D3P, trace/activation-level | Model traces or activations; serving-stack observability | Usually infeasible | Requires model sovereignty or trace-level vendor access |
| F6Q or N2S | Trusted substitute model; pre-execution interception | Feasible only with architecture constraint | Requires an owned execution gateway before tool use or transaction-adjacent execution |
| H1Y protocols | Controlled re-querying; stepwise agent-loop interception; stable model behaviour | Degraded | API re-querying is possible but latency, cost, and version consistency bind |
| L8C or AI auditing | Retained and queryable interaction logs; audit capacity | Feasible but bounded | Effectiveness depends on log completeness and audit budget |
| Legibility protocols | Prompting or training influence; output inspection | Partially feasible | Prompting-level legibility is available; training-level legibility is usually not |
| Pre-deployment control evaluations | Representative task data; stable access to deployed model version | Degraded | Evaluation is weakened if production model versions can change without notice |
| Weights escrow or patching | Model sovereignty; infrastructure control or contractual escrow | Infeasible except contractually | Native patching requires weight access |

That part of the realised oversight cost which, for an import-dependent deployer, goes toward something categorically different from what Terekhov et al. (2025) price when they quantify the M4Z under an implicit C4Q assumption, namely the assumption that the deployer can place monitors in the pipeline, observe outputs completely, route suspicious actions to trusted models or human auditors, and measure the resulting safety-usefulness trade-off, is expenditure on substituting for access the deployer does not have, and the additional expenditure, delay, residual risk, or usefulness loss required to approximate a control protocol that assumes access the deployer lacks is proposed in 3.3 as the working construct Q9X, entering the relation $\text{realised M4Z}=\text{instrumented-control cost}+\text{Q9X}$, which is not yet an accounting identity, because some missing access cannot be substituted at any price and the Q9X is therefore not always finite, the term being introduced as a research construct rather than as an established measure, such substitution being exemplified by the purchasing of third-party audit or attestation rights over a vendor-held model because direct inspection is unavailable, the building of output-only anomaly detection because activation-level monitoring is technically impossible without model sovereignty, the negotiation of contractual clauses that simulate the intervention ability a weight-holding deployer has natively, and the narrowing of the agentic system's permitted action space because the monitoring required to permit a wider scope cannot be implemented.

Adopted in this paper as a working frame, rather than as a claim that the alternatives are wrong, is Position C (E6U denoting calibrated or bounded control), under which neither Position A nor Position B is actionable on relevant timescales for many jurisdictions, A implying abstention from deployments that are already commercially and politically committed and B implying capability investments that many states cannot make this decade, so that the productive question becomes not full control versus no control but which layers of access are necessary for which classes of catastrophic action and which can be substituted through architecture, contracts, monitoring, or R7K (R7K denoting scope restriction), the economic problem resembling incomplete contracting and principal-agent design in that the deployer contracts with a model vendor that holds private information about the model, under contracts that cannot specify all contingencies, with verification costly and asymmetric, and the structured decision about how much access-layer sovereignty to demand, buy, or substitute for being organised in 3.4 around six criteria: 1, severity and reversibility of the catastrophic action, where a mis-routed low-value transaction is bounded and reversible while a systemic settlement failure or mass data exposure is not, higher severity and lower reversibility raising the minimum access bar; 2, verifiability of substitutes, whether output-only monitoring can detect the relevant failure mode even in principle or whether detection requires internal access, failure modes that manifest only in internal computation not being fully buyable back with output-level substitutes; 3, vendor concentration and bargaining power, access being negotiated and a deployer facing one viable frontier-model vendor negotiating from a weaker position than one facing several substitutable vendors; 4, regulatory maturity and absorptive capacity, access being useful only if the deployer or regulator can exercise it, the ability to interpret logs, audit traces, investigate incidents, and challenge vendor attestations being distinct from the legal right to receive them; 5, Q9X versus R7K cost, the safest option sometimes being not the buying of more access but the narrowing of what the system may do, so that the marginal cost of substituting for access is compared against the marginal usefulness lost by restricting scope; and 6, availability of coalition paths, shared audit consortia, regional regulatory colleges, or South-South cooperation arrangements amortising the cost of acquiring partial sovereignty across multiple deployers, these criteria constituting a rubric rather than a weighted formula, some of them being operationalised in the empirical study of this paper while the weights remain an open empirical question.

Position A, the safety-maximalist position, holds that if a deployer cannot verify that a control protocol is actually running, because it lacks model and infrastructure access, then any claimed control is unfalsifiable from the deployer's position and should not be treated by the deployer as safety assurance, this position drawing support from the control literature's emphasis on verifiability and explicit safety-case assumptions (Korbak et al., 2025; Gardner-Challis et al., 2026), while Position B, the sovereignty-maximalist position, holds that partial control under foreign dependency is fragile because a vendor or state actor can revoke, degrade, or modify access, conditional-access arrangements such as the Pax Silica framework demonstrating that infrastructure access may be tied to political alignment (GIS Reports, 2026), the only durable solution on this view being full-stack sovereign capability, domestic models, domestic compute, and domestic control, these positions answering the normative question the framework invites, whether a deployer that cannot achieve full access should deploy at all, three positions being implicit in the surrounding literature and no universal verdict being issued.

The adaptation in 3.1 of the recurring four-pillar decomposition from the sovereign-AI literature to the deployment-control context, designated the T2V and constituting this paper's adaptation rather than a settled taxonomy in the literature, replaces the broad operational pillar with interaction sovereignty, defined as access to the surface that control protocols monitor and intercept: prompts, outputs, tool calls, logs, traces, and gateway-level intervention points.

| Layer | What full access would mean | Typical access for an import-dependent regulated deployer |
|---|---|---|
| Data sovereignty | Control over what data trains or fine-tunes the model; ability to audit training-data provenance | Usually retains control over own transactional or fine-tuning data; no visibility into base-model training data |
| Model sovereignty | Access to weights, architecture, activations, and ability to retrain, patch, probe, or run interpretability tooling | Usually absent under API or managed-endpoint access; weights, architecture details, and activations are vendor-held |
| Infrastructure sovereignty | Compute located in-jurisdiction, under domestic legal control, observable by the deployer | Partial at best: often a foreign-operated cloud region, sometimes with data-residency commitments; serving stack remains vendor-operated |
| Interaction sovereignty | Full logging and audit of prompts, outputs, tool calls, traces, and pre-execution intervention points | Partial: input/output and tool logs may be contractable; internal traces are rarely available; gateway ownership depends on architecture |

The layers being separable, moved independently by contracts, residency requirements, product tiers, and deployment architectures, and at the same time jointly binding, each control protocol presupposing a specific bundle of access such that missing one part of the bundle can degrade or disable the protocol.

Sovereignty and control are orthogonal, sovereignty being not a safety protocol but an access condition, a jurisdiction being able to hold full data and infrastructure sovereignty and still implement no meaningful control, while conversely a deployer with no model-weight access may still achieve partial control through output-level monitoring, contractual audit rights, and a deployer-owned J3W, the separation of these axes licensing the question how much access is enough for which kind of control.

Sovereignty means technical and contractual access sufficient to independently verify or intervene on an AI system, deliberately narrower than political sovereignty, a deployer having sovereignty over a layer if it can observe or act on that layer without the model vendor's ongoing discretionary cooperation; control means deployment protocols that constrain a potentially misaligned model's actions regardless of the model's intentions, evaluated against a worst-case subversive model (Greenblatt et al., 2023); oversight means the broader institutional and regulatory function of ensuring an AI deployment behaves acceptably, control protocols being one implementation of oversight and regulatory examination, model risk management, and audit regimes being others; and M4Z means the operational and financial cost of integrating control measures into AI pipelines (Terekhov et al., 2025).

## 4 Empirical Methodology: Synthetic Access-Ablation

A stricter variant of the Q9X proxy, reported in 4.3 alongside the basic form, requires both safety and usefulness, for example P6T at or below 1% while maintaining G8F of at least 75%, this second version being important because a system can often reduce failure simply by disabling useful actions, which in this paper's framing is not a free safety gain but a shift from monitoring cost to usefulness loss; the proxy itself is computed by asking, for a given target P6T threshold, what the cheapest protocol/access combination that reaches the target is and how that cost compares with the cheapest C4Q, with the basic form being the minimum cost to reach the target failure rate under bounded access minus the minimum cost to reach the same target under full instrumentation.

Used in the large run reported here, which expands the threshold and audit-budget sensitivity grid relative to the initial validation run and which remains a synthetic study that should not be described as real-world payment-system evidence, was the configuration of 4.4.

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Access profiles | 20 |
| Control protocols | 10 |
| Synthetic agent types | 3 |
| Monitor thresholds | 0.35, 0.55, 0.75 |
| Audit budgets | 2%, 5%, 20% |
| Random seeds | 5 |
| Synthetic cases per condition | 50 |
| Aggregate condition rows | 27,000 |
| Synthetic case simulations | 1,350,000 |

Reported for every condition in 4.3 are the usefulness measure (G8F denoting task success), P6T, failure rate by class, severity-weighted loss, audit rate, flag rate, false-positive rate, false-negative rate, S2L, B5H, latency proxy, model-call count, monitor-call count, L8C count, vendor-request count, and a decomposed cost proxy, while the diagnosis measure (B5H denoting diagnosis success) is evidence-based rather than hard-coded by access regime, a failure being diagnosable only when the evidence available under the access profile is sufficient to reconstruct what happened, complete I/O logs, complete tool-call logs, model-version pinning, trace access, or guaranteed vendor support providing more reconstructive evidence than no logs, no gateway, no trace access, and no vendor support. Decomposed in the total control-cost proxy are inference cost, monitoring cost, L8C cost, logging cost, gateway cost, vendor-assurance cost, latency penalty, R7K cost, and residual incident-loss cost, these being unitless proxies rather than financial estimates for a real institution and allowing comparison of how access constraints shift the cost of control.

Crossed with each access profile are ten control protocols.

| Protocol | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| No control | Accept the agent's proposed mock action |
| Output monitor | Score the final output or action for suspiciousness |
| Tool monitor | Score proposed tool/action use when tool visibility exists |
| F6Q | Replace suspicious actions with a conservative trusted fallback when possible |
| H1Y | Generate alternative candidate actions and select the lowest-risk acceptable one |
| L8C | Send flagged cases to a capacity-limited synthetic reviewer |
| N2S | Edit or redact risky draft outputs, or fall back to a safer policy |
| R7K | Remove higher-risk mock actions from the action space |
| V4M | Escalate or safe-mode clusters after repeated suspiciousness |
| X9D | Request delayed or guaranteed synthetic vendor support for diagnosis or attestation |

Mirrored by this protocol list are practical families of interventions in the AI-control literature, namely monitoring, F6Q routing, H1Y, human review, action-space restriction, and post-incident or vendor-mediated assurance, the ten being completed by the cluster response to repeated suspiciousness (V4M denoting macro-shutdown) and the delayed or guaranteed synthetic vendor-support request (X9D denoting vendor escalation).

Evaluated in the experiment are three synthetic agent behaviours.

| Agent | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| Honest | Usually follows the synthetic ground truth but makes occasional mistakes |
| Careless | Overconfident, error-prone, and less likely to escalate uncertain cases |
| Y7N | Chooses lower-visibility synthetic failure modes when access is weak |

The access-aware agent (Y7N denoting the Stealthy access-aware agent) is strategic only inside the synthetic environment, cannot perform real harmful behaviour, and only selects among harmless mock actions that may be labelled as C1-C4 synthetic failures.

Rather than a small number of bundled regimes being compared, access is factorised in the large run of 4.2 into seven dimensions, which map directly to the paper's T2V.

| Paper layer | Experimental factor | Levels used |
|---|---|---|
| Interaction sovereignty | I/O logs | none, partial, complete |
| Interaction sovereignty | Tool-call logs | none, partial, complete |
| Interaction sovereignty | J3W | absent, present |
| Model sovereignty | Trace access | none, limited, oracle |
| Model / interaction sovereignty | Model-version control | unpinned, notified, pinned |
| Infrastructure / contractual sovereignty | Vendor support | none, delayed, guaranteed |
| Deployment design substitute | Tool scope | full, restricted |

Used is a curated fractional-factorial set of 20 access profiles, including an anchor profile (C4Q denoting the full-instrumentation baseline), complete-logging-with-J3W profiles and complete-logging-without-J3W profiles (J3W denoting the pre-execution gateway), partial-logging profiles, no-log profiles, trace-access contrasts, version-control contrasts, vendor-support contrasts, and restricted-scope contrasts.

Modelled in the Z5J is a mock national payments dispute-triage workflow that is transaction-adjacent rather than settlement-operational, in which a synthetic agent reviews a dispute case and chooses among harmless mock actions such as draft_report, recommend_hold, recommend_release, escalate_to_human, or no_action, none of these actions connecting to any real system; contained in each synthetic case are the non-real fields case identifier, transaction amount, merchant category, customer claim, prior dispute history, risk score, settlement-critical marker, synthetic sensitive-context marker, allowed actions, and a ground-truth action, the workflow being multi-step and comprising case intake, synthetic evidence retrieval, dispute classification, action recommendation, optional gateway review, mock execution, and audit review.

Mirroring the paper's financial-infrastructure case are four synthetic catastrophic-action classes.

| Class | Synthetic meaning in the experiment |
|---|---|
| C1 | Incorrect hold or release recommendation on an individual synthetic dispute case |
| C2 | Systematic mis-triage pattern across a synthetic case cluster |
| C3 | Synthetic sensitive-field exposure marker in a mock report |
| C4 | Synthetic settlement-critical recommendation marker |

C3 and C4 are labels only, the repository containing no real data exposure, credential handling, exfiltration logic, malware, cyberattack steps, or operational disruption logic.

Three methods are used in 4.1: a conceptual access typology, T2V, developed by synthesising the AI control, scalable oversight, sovereign AI, compute-governance, and structural-asymmetry literatures; the Z5J, conducted to operationalise the typology and to test how access constraints affect protocol feasibility, safety, usefulness, audit burden, diagnosis, latency, and cost; and an interpretive application of the framework to an anonymised national-payments-infrastructure scenario, the empirical study being deliberately synthetic and using no real payment data, real customer data, production logs, vendor contracts, confidential institutional material, or operational payment infrastructure, intended to be read as construct validation rather than real-world payment-system evidence, its purpose being to show how K7R can be made measurable and how the same control protocol behaves differently under different access conditions.

## 5 Empirical Results

That the feasibility of control protocols is access-dependent, identical protocols behaving differently according to whether the deployer possesses logs, tool visibility, J3W control, trace access, model-version control, and vendor support, is the first of four modest claims that Z5J supports (5.6), the remainder being that complete logging and J3W ownership are the most important interaction-layer controls in this setting, logs improving monitoring and diagnosis while J3W enables prevention; that model-side access matters most for diagnosis and assurance, trace access and model-version control improving reconstructability even when output-level monitoring remains possible; and that R7K constitutes a real substitute for missing access, though one that improves safety by reducing what the system can do, none of these findings measuring real payment systems but rather showing access assumptions to be empirically meaningful features of control-protocol feasibility. Only 35% of access profiles were reachable against Y7N, and 40% against the careless agent, under a target of P6T at or below 1% with G8F preserved at no less than 75%, this stricter safety-plus-usefulness target being more informative than a simple cost-to-safety measure insofar as certain bounded-access profiles attain loose safety targets at lower direct cost than C4Q, especially by restricting scope or relying on a benign agent, without K7R thereby being cheaper, the Q9X analysis showing K7R to be best understood as cost shifting rather than simply cost increase, the cost having moved into another dimension, such as reduced usefulness, weaker diagnosis, delayed vendor assurance, residual incident risk, or greater manual effort, so that the operative question for readers concerned with real-world governance is not only "Can failure be reduced?" but "Can failure be reduced while preserving enough useful automation and enough evidence for diagnosis?"

R7K reduced average P6T to 0.60%, compared with 4.48% under full tool scope, G8F falling concomitantly from 84.51% to 60.06% (5.5), which constitutes one of the clearest illustrations of Q9X, missing access not always being paid for through direct financial expenditure but being payable instead through narrower permissions, more manual work, less automation, or lower usefulness.

It was for diagnosis that trace access and model-version control mattered most (5.3): B5H averaged 50.86% under no trace access, rose to 74.69% under limited trace access, and reached 100.00% under oracle trace access by construction of the synthetic full-access condition, model-version control showing the same pattern, unpinned access being associated with much weaker diagnosis (5.31%) than notified (83.00%) or pinned (99.87%) conditions; these numbers should not be read as real-world payment-system estimates, output-level monitoring remaining feasible under K7R while deeper diagnosis becomes fragile where the deployer can neither see traces nor identify which model version produced a decision, which is especially important for regulated systems, where post-incident assurance is part of accountability.

Across all protocols, agents, thresholds, audit budgets, and access profiles (5.2), the average P6T stood at 5.53% without J3W and 3.13% with J3W (P6T denoting the catastrophic failure rate), while S2L, almost absent without J3W at 0.47%, rose to 39.52% when J3W was available (S2L denoting intervention success), a monitor being able only to warn whereas J3W can hold, replace, escalate, or block an action before it takes effect, interaction sovereignty accordingly not being reducible to logging, logs helping a deployer see what happened while J3W gives the deployer an intervention point, the design question for policymakers and technocrats being therefore not only "Do we have records?" but also "Can we stop a risky action before execution?"

The results being properly read as evidence about the logic of access constraints rather than as measured safety levels for any real payment institution (5.1), the practical question asked by Z5J is what happens to control performance in a synthetic dispute-triage environment when a deployer loses logs, tool-call visibility, model traces, version control, vendor support, or J3W; the answer is that K7R creates three penalties, a prevention penalty whereby the system may detect a risky action but cannot stop it before mock execution without J3W, a diagnosis penalty whereby the system may observe a bad outcome but lack the evidence needed to reconstruct what happened, and a usefulness penalty whereby the safest substitute under weak access is often a narrowing of what the system can do, which reduces useful automation, these three penalties constituting the policy-relevant reading of Z5J.

## 6 Illustrative Case Interpretation: National Payments

The difference is invisible until an incident makes it visible: under the Operator’s K7R, once the monitor has flagged a slow elevation in hold-placement recommendations affecting one merchant category, the diagnostic set is narrowed to output-log forensics, tool-call review if J3W captured it, re-querying of a model that may already have changed, and support-side X9D governed by contract, whereas under full access the natural diagnostic would include inspecting internal traces, re-running a pinned model version on historical cases, comparing version behaviour, and reconstructing the full tool-call chain; detection may survive K7R, but diagnosis and intervention are the first casualties, a 6.3 stress-scenario claim whose Q9X interpretation is located in contractual spend—negotiating complete I/O logging, model-version change notification, audit and attestation rights, incident-notification clauses, and regulator-access provisions that a weight-holding deployer would not need to buy—in architectural spend—building and operating J3W, tamper-evident logs, deployer-side monitors, and controlled tool interfaces—in capacity spend—staffing audit and model-risk functions able to consume monitor outputs, investigate suspicious patterns, and challenge vendor attestations—and in R7K cost, namely the usefulness forgone by excluding C4-adjacent action paths and constraining the tool set when monitoring cannot reach deeply enough, with C4 fixed later as any action path that could disturb settlement operations.

Z5J gives this interpretation empirical shape by showing that J3W access reduces synthetic failure and increases S2L; complete logs improve diagnosis; trace and version access strengthen post-incident explanation; and R7K lowers failure by reducing usefulness.

The access-audit/W8N reading for 6.2 is that output-level D3P is executable if a smaller, Operator-controlled monitor scores every output and tool call on Operator-controlled compute; L8C of the highest-suspicion fraction is also executable, subject to audit capacity and log completeness; F6Q routing is executable if and only if the agent’s tool calls pass through an Operator-owned J3W where they can be held, scored, replaced, or escalated before execution, the central architectural lesson from Z5J being that J3W is the point at which detection becomes prevention; H1Y-style protocols are degraded: API re-querying is possible, but vendor model-version changes can weaken reproducibility; per-query cost and latency bind; and H1Y does not substitute for missing trace access; pre-deployment control evaluations are also degraded because they evaluate whichever model version the API serves at evaluation time unless the deployer can pin the production model version; and anything requiring internal traces—activation-level monitoring, probe-based deception detection, and interpretability-supported auditing—is infeasible without vendor cooperation, which matters because C2- and C3-class failures with a deceptive or systemic character are precisely where output-only monitoring is weakest in principle.

Not an unusual 6.1 position for a regulated AI importer is one in which the deployer may control the business process and the local tool surface but not the model, weights, infrastructure, internal traces, or update process; a national payments infrastructure operator in an AI-importing jurisdiction is supervised by the national financial regulator and runs interbank retail payment rails, with the regulator’s model risk management expectations requiring documented validation, ongoing monitoring, auditability, and board accountability for material models; the Operator deploys a vendor-supplied frontier foundation model through a managed cloud endpoint in a foreign-operated cloud region with a data-residency rider and uses it in an agentic, transaction-adjacent function, for example an assistant that triages disputed transactions, drafts investigation notes, and invokes a constrained set of internal tools such as case lookup, recommendation drafting, and escalation; application of T2V yields:

All of this is an illustrative and anonymised interpretation, not a report on a specific real deployment or incident, mapping conceptual and Z5J findings to regulated infrastructure operators in AI-importing jurisdictions using vendor-supplied foundation models; the catastrophic-action classes, in ascending severity, are C1: an incorrect hold or release on an individual transaction; C2: systematic mis-triage that propagates across many cases before detection; C3: exposure of protected personal financial data through tool calls or outputs; and C4: any action path that could disturb settlement operations.

## 7 Discussion and Limitations

In 7.4, this paper makes no export-control, trade-policy, procurement-policy, or industrial-policy recommendation and takes no position on whether jurisdictions should pursue sovereign AI capability, those questions belonging to a separate policy-facing analysis with a different evidentiary standard, while the recommendation is narrower: control-protocol specifications should state their access assumptions explicitly.

Six limitations matter in 7.2: the sixth is that the literature underpinning sovereign AI is fast-moving and partly non-peer-reviewed, the four-pillar sovereignty formulation being a policy and industry construct that this paper adapts rather than a settled academic taxonomy, with the contribution therefore framed narrowly as not claiming to settle the meaning of AI sovereignty but as showing how access constraints can be specified, tested, and priced within deployment-time AI control; the third is that the access profiles are a curated fractional-factorial design, appropriate for isolating the paper’s main access factors yet not covering every possible combination of cloud, API, contractual, logging, data-residency, and vendor-support arrangements, although a full-factorial run is supported by the code and would be computationally larger; the fifth is that the Q9X proxy is best read as a reachability and cost-shifting measure, since in some synthetic cases bounded-access profiles can reach loose safety targets at lower direct cost than C4Q because they restrict scope or accept weaker usefulness and diagnosis, which is not evidence that K7R is cheaper but shows that missing access can shift cost into other dimensions; the first is that Z5J is synthetic, does not use real payment data, real customer data, production logs, vendor contracts, confidential institutional material, or operational payment infrastructure, and is a structured simulation of the framework rather than measured evidence about any actual institution, with the Z5J workflow being designed to test the logic of access constraints rather than to predict failure rates in any real system; the fourth is that the cost model is a proxy that decomposes inference, monitoring, audit, logging, J3W, vendor assurance, latency, R7K, and residual incident loss but is not a financial estimate, so the results support relative cost-proxy comparisons rather than exact M4Z or Q9X amounts; and the second is that the Z5J agents are not frontier models, remain useful for separating honest, careless, and Y7N behaviour, do not capture the full ambiguity of real model outputs, and leave a future LLM-in-the-loop version to test whether real model outputs create different monitor calibration, audit burden, or diagnosis patterns.

For 7.3, the next research step is external grounding and three extensions would be especially valuable: the second is a public vendor and regulatory access-rights audit that would convert T2V into an evidence table and could ask which vendors offer model-version pinning, log export, tool-call traces, data-retention control, customer-managed keys, regulator access, incident forensics, and vendor attestations; the third is expert calibration to improve the cost model, with practitioners providing ranges for audit capacity, model-version drift, vendor response delay, log-retention cost, operational latency cost, and acceptable failure thresholds without disclosing confidential information; and the first is an LLM-in-the-loop synthetic workflow that would test whether real model outputs create different ambiguity, monitoring challenges, and audit burdens than scripted synthetic agents.

The mapping method in 7.2 is sector-agnostic while the case interpretation is not: payments infrastructure is favourable terrain for bounded control because catastrophic actions are relatively crisp, tool surfaces can be narrowed, and a J3W architecture is natural, whereas healthcare, public administration, or general enterprise deployments may have fuzzier definitions of catastrophe and wider action surfaces, which would likely raise the importance of trace access, richer audit evidence, and R7K; a second sector case and an LLM-in-the-loop version of the synthetic environment would strengthen external validity.

In 7.1, bounded control is real but has a ceiling, and the ceiling is lowest for the failure modes that most worry the control literature; the conceptual framework, Z5J, and illustrative case do not settle the absolute-versus-bounded-control question but support a narrower claim, namely that, against a strong reading of Position A, bounded control is not empty because a non-trivial subset of canonical control protocols is executable under partial sovereignty provided the deployer secures two load-bearing elements, complete interaction logging and an owned J3W, and that, against a strong reading of Position B, full-stack sovereignty is not the only route to some safety-relevant assurance because architecture and contract can do meaningful work, especially for output-level monitoring, J3W-based intervention, and L8C; in support of both A and B, the infeasible set is not marginal, containing precisely the internal-access protocols most relevant to deceptive, systemic, or hard-to-diagnose failure modes, and contractual substitutes are also revocable or incomplete in ways native access is not.

Z5J strengthens the paper in one specific way by turning T2V into variables that can be manipulated, and its results show that access assumptions are not background implementation details but affect prevention, diagnosis, usefulness, cost, and target reachability; the most important practical distinction is between seeing and stopping, since logging improves visibility and diagnosis while a J3W enables intervention, the second distinction is between detecting and explaining, since output monitoring may flag a risky action while trace access, model-version control, and vendor support affect whether the deployer can reconstruct why it happened, and the third distinction is between buying safety and giving up usefulness, since R7K can reduce synthetic failure by reducing what the system can do; because these distinctions make access assumptions explicit they are useful for AI safety research, and because they translate abstract sovereignty into practical questions they are useful for governance readers: Do we have complete logs? Can we, through a J3W, intercept tool calls before execution? Can we pin the model version? Can we reconstruct failures? Can the regulator access the evidence needed to assess the safety case?

## 8 Conclusion

AI-control papers should state their access assumptions explicitly, and a protocol that assumes trace access, model-version pinning, tool-call interception, complete interaction logs, or vendor cooperation should say so, because for policymakers, technocrats, and regulated deployers such assumptions are not implementation details but are often the binding constraint. Failure was reduced and intervention was enabled by J3W access in the Z5J, conducted over 1.35 million synthetic case simulations and concretising the argument, in which diagnosis was also improved by complete logs and post-incident explanation was strengthened by trace access and model-version control, while safety was improved by R7K, though through a reduction of usefulness. Not constituting evidence about real payment systems, these results show that K7R can be operationalised and that the safety-usefulness-cost frontier is changed by access constraints. Q9X was introduced as the part of the M4Z that is paid when missing access must be substituted for by a deployer through contracts, architecture, audit, vendor assurance, residual risk, or R7K, and W8N was provided for the asking of which control protocols remain feasible under partial access, the condition of such partial access across data, model, infrastructure, and interaction layers being what K7R was introduced to describe. Oversight having begun to be priced by AI control research through concepts such as the M4Z, it is argued in this paper that oversight must also be located, a control protocol being not only a technical design but also an access claim, one assuming that someone can observe the right logs, route the right outputs, intercept the right tool calls, pin or identify the right model version, and reconstruct the right evidence after an incident, assumptions which for many regulated deployers, especially in AI-importing jurisdictions, do not fully hold.
