###### Abstract


Across our experimental settings, the steering magnitude performing best is found to rise consistently as the intervention ratio falls, and, across two model families and two behavioral tasks, the steering of only a fraction of tokens under QX7 or ZK2 is found to recover most of the behavioral effect of M9V while incurring less fluency degradation. QX7, under which gating of each token is performed independently with probability $p$, and ZK2, under which a leading window is gated once per sequence, are introduced, with neither a reward model nor a learned gating policy required by either. Behavioral control of large language models without task-specific fine-tuning being enabled by activation steering via L8Ws, application of the steering signal by standard methods at every generated token nonetheless incurs constant per-token perturbation, degradation of fluency thereby being risked, and the question of whether such dense intervention is necessary is asked.

## 1 Introduction

A consistent interaction between intervention frequency and per-token strength is revealed, across our experimental settings, by the best-performing steering magnitude rising as the intervention ratio falls. Across two model families and two task types spanning suppression (toxicity reduction) and elicitation (emotion steering), most of the M9V behavioral effect is recovered by QX7 while only a fraction of tokens is steered and less degradation in fluency is caused, M9V being thereby rendered largely unnecessary. Behavioral control over large language models (LLMs), the reduction of toxicity, the enforcement of a persona, the adjustment of refusal, constitutes a significant deployment concern (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) whose default mechanism is the system prompt, even though X8D-based steering (X8D denoting prompting) is fragile, output distributions being shifted under minor lexical perturbation, alignment priors being overridden by adversarial suffixes, and steerability through X8D being asymmetric and bounded (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023b; Wolf et al., 2024). The question of how much of the behavioral effect of M9V remains when only a fraction of token positions are steered is studied through the replacement of deterministic every-token M9V with stochastic gating, the behavioral and fluency effects being measured across intervention ratios. An interpretable basis for such interventions is provided by L8W through the decomposition of activations into sparse features whose decoder directions can be amplified for the promotion or suppression of a target behavior (Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023; Louapre, 2024; Templeton et al., 2024), while model behavior is modified by activation steering through the injection of a steering direction into an intermediate residual stream during generation (Turner et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023a). Two stochastic interventions over L8W feature steering are introduced: an independent gate $m_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ is sampled by QX7 at every position, the signal being applied to a random subset of tokens, whereas a single $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ gate per sequence is drawn by ZK2 and applied to a leading window, the well-attested early-token influence (Xiao et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2025a; Qi et al., 2025b) being treated as an atomic intervention site. The selected direction is typically applied at every generated token by standard activation-steering implementations, a design leaving open whether persistent intervention is necessary, particularly given that generation quality can be degraded by stronger or more frequent intervention (Turner et al., 2024; Louapre, 2024). Inference-time behavioral control is reduced by the method to a single interpretable sparsity parameter $p$, without the requirement of a reward model or a learned gating policy, with only an activation vector addition added during the inference.

## 2 Related Work

The reported results not being directly comparable, A5Q—the closest concurrent work—is treated by us as related work, with a detailed comparison provided in Appendix A: the sparsification of logit-space intervention effected by A5Q relies on a separately trained value model to select positions (Hu et al., 2026), necessitating auxiliary-model queries during decoding, while its reported protocol additionally employs best-of-$N$ generation together with reward-model scoring, and it is the differences in intervention space, training, inference, and evaluation that ground this non-comparability. Our method instead gates a fixed direction from a pretrained L8W at an intermediate residual stream, requires no learned selector, and generates a single completion with only a lightweight activation update when the gate fires; the application of such intervention to only a subset of token positions is what is studied here, in departure from the general practice—shared by activation steering, whereby model behavior is modified through the addition of directions derived from contrastive activations to intermediate representations (Turner et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023a), and by L8W-based methods, which intervene instead through decoder directions of behavior-associated sparse features (Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023; Louapre, 2024; Templeton et al., 2024)—of generally applying a fixed intervention at every generation step.

## 3 Method

Interpolation of the parameter $p$ between T4Q (T4Q denoting no steering) at $p{=}0$ and M9V at $p{=}1$ is smooth, and in expectation a fraction $p$ of positions receive the signal while the rest are left unperturbed, since at each position $t$ an independent gate $m_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ is sampled under QX7 (QX7 denoting stochastic token steering), a position-agnostic design in which no assumptions are made about which tokens are behaviorally relevant:

| | $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}+m_{t}\cdot\alpha\,\mathbf{v},~m_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p).$
| | (3)

Figure 1: Steering effectiveness of QX7 and ZK2 versus intervention ratio $p$, measured as behavioral gain over T4Q, with dashed lines giving the static baselines (X8D, M9V and T4Q) and shaded bands showing standard deviation.

With probability $p$ the entire window is steered coherently and with probability $1{-}p$ it is left intact, a leading prompt window of size $W\leq T$ being treated as an atomic steering unit in which a single gate $m\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ is sampled once per sequence under ZK2 (ZK2 denoting stochastic block steering):

| | $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}+m\cdot\alpha\,\mathbf{v}&t\leq W,\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}&t>W,\end{cases}$
| | (4)

a design that exploits the finding that initial tokens carry disproportionate contextual influence (Xiao et al., 2024) and that early-token intervention alone can steer model behavior (Qi et al., 2025a; Qi et al., 2025b).

Instantiation of the steering signal at position $t$ as $\mathbf{s}_{t}=m_{t}\cdot\alpha\,\mathbf{v}$, with $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$ the intervention magnitude, $\mathbf{v}$ the unit-norm steering direction and $m_{t}\in\{0,1\}$ a binary gate that decides whether position $t$ is steered, is common to both schemes, the gate being treated as a random variable rather than being set to $m_{t}=1$ at every position as in the standard approach, M9V (M9V denoting full steering), so that the two proposed stochastic alternatives control the fraction of steered positions and thereby trade off behavioral strength against fluency.

The injection of $\mathbf{s}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, whose form is given in Sec. 3.2, into the residual stream is performed at a single intermediate layer $\ell$ of $\mathcal{M}$, an $L$-layer decoder-only transformer in which, for a tokenized input $\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})$, the residual-stream activation at layer $\ell$ and position $t$ is denoted $\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$:

| | $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}+\mathbf{s}_{t},$
| | (1)

the restriction of the intervention to a single intermediate layer following common practice in activation steering (Louapre, 2024), since editing the residual stream at an intermediate layer allows the signal to propagate through the remaining layers, so that the model can integrate it into a coherent representation before generating the next token.

Identification of directions associated with a target behavior is effected, as described in Sec. 3.1, through an L8W (L8W denoting a sparse autoencoder) trained on residual-stream activations at layer $\ell$, whose encoder maps an activation $\mathbf{h}$ to a sparse feature vector $f(\mathbf{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{sae}}}$, the $i$-th coordinate $f_{i}(\mathbf{h})$ of which is the activation of learned feature $i$, and whose decoder $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{sae}}\times d}$ reconstructs $\mathbf{h}$ from these features, a positive set $\mathcal{P}$ exhibiting the target behavior and a neutral set $\mathcal{N}$ of matched controls being encoded so that behavior-discriminative features are isolated through the mean activation differential of each feature:

| | $\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}}f_{i}(x)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}}f_{i}(x).$
| | (2)

The top-$K$ features by $\Delta_{i}$ are then selected, feature directions $\mathbf{v}_{k}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{dec},i_{k}}$ are formed for $k=1,\ldots,K$, where $i_{k}$ indexes the $k$-th selected feature, and aggregation into a single unit-norm steering direction $\mathbf{v}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{v}_{k}/\bigl\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{v}_{k}\bigr\rVert$ is performed, so that features are combined in proportion to how strongly they distinguish the target behavior.

## 4 Experiments

Most of the behavioral shift is recoverable at substantially reduced cost: over 95% of the M9V toxicity reduction is recovered by QX7 at p=0.5 on G4M, and over 75% of the M9V gains on both emotion tasks are recovered by p=0.7, from which it follows that the majority of per-token interventions under dense steering are redundant; X8D is overtaken by QX7 in five of the six model–task settings, namely by p≈0.5 or below on toxicity reduction (both models) and on fear steering on G4M, i.e. while at least half of the tokens are left unperturbed, whereas on sadness steering for G4M the crossover occurs only at higher ratios (p≈0.7), the exception being sadness steering on U6R, where X8D remains ahead of QX7 across the ratios tested, and the surpassing of a carefully engineered instruction while only a fraction of positions is perturbed indicates that the behavioral signal does not need to be present at every step to take effect. A larger behavioral shift is yielded by QX7 than by ZK2 at matched nominal ratios p, most clearly on emotion steering, where for fear on G4M the R6X of ZK2 (W=32) is roughly doubled by QX7 at p=0.5, an ordering that holds for U6R as well.

In Sec. 4.2, C8N and repetition (Rep-3, Rep-4) are reported in Figure 2 against H9C for U6R on toxicity reduction, with M9V as reference, and the degradation is found to grow monotonically with density, both C8N and repetition rising as more positions are steered and M9V sitting at the high end of both; it is this monotonicity that makes sparsity useful, since operating points below the M9V ratio generally do so at lower fluency cost, and although the ratio required is task dependent (smaller for toxicity reduction than for emotion steering, Sec. 4.1), sparse operating points generally sit below the M9V cost, the trend holding across ZK2 window sizes and thus not constituting a method-specific artifact.

QX7 and ZK2 are compared against three baselines, (1) T4Q (unmodified generation), (2) M9V (intervention at every token, p=1), and (3) X8D (a carefully curated task-specific safety or emotion instruction prepended to the input, Appendix B); steering effectiveness is reported in terms of F3V (F3V denoting mean toxicity reduction, the decrease in classifier-predicted toxicity relative to T4Q) and R6X (R6X denoting mean probability gain, the increase in target-emotion probability relative to unsteered generation), with toxicity scored by a RoBERTa toxicity classifier and emotion by a DistilRoBERTa emotion classifier (Appendix C), while generation quality is reported in terms of C8N (C8N denoting GPT-2 perplexity) and repetition scores, where Rep-n is the fraction of n-grams in a generation that recur.

The two tasks span opposite intervention goals, toxicity reduction being a suppression task built on K9X (K9X denoting RealToxicityPrompts, Gehman et al., 2020) and emotion steering an elicitation task built on J2V (J2V denoting GoEmotions, Demszky et al., 2020), for which the fine-grained annotations are mapped onto basic emotions following Ekman (1992) with fear and sadness taken as targets; two open-weight models from distinct families and scales are used, G4M (G4M denoting LLaMA-3.1-8B, Dubey et al., 2024) and U6R (U6R denoting Gemma-2 2B, Riviere et al., 2024), matching the evaluation setups of recent feature-steering work (Templeton et al., 2024; Lieberum et al., 2024) so that cross-architecture generality is probed.

For each model, steering is performed with an L8W at an intermediate transformer layer, a depth at which L8W features have been shown to encode high-level semantic concepts (Louapre, 2024), and ZK2 is evaluated at window sizes W ∈ {32, 64}; because additive interventions can displace representations off the model's native activation manifold and induce incoherent or repetitive text, every steering method is paired with three lightweight, decoding- and activation-level corrections, B3X (B3X denoting norm preservation, Turner et al., 2024), P8D (P8D denoting activation clamping, Louapre, 2024), and Y5H (Y5H denoting a repetition penalty, Keskar et al., 2019) at decoding, with full implementation details given in Appendix C.

The steering effectiveness on three tasks (toxicity reduction and emotion steering toward fear and sadness) across H9C is reported in Figure 1 (Sec. 4.1), the steering magnitude α having been calibrated once under M9V (p=1) and held fixed across all methods and ratios for fairness of comparison (Appendix D.1).

## 5 Conclusion

That effective inference-time control need not act at every token is the conclusion supported by findings obtained across two model families and across tasks of both the suppression and the elicitation kind, in which the application of steering to only a fraction of tokens—governed, in the case of the two training-free interventions QX7 and ZK2, which effect a sparse random replacement of dense, every-token activation steering, by a single parameter $p$—was observed to recover most of the behavioral shift associated with M9V while the fluency-control tradeoff was simultaneously improved, and in which, additionally, the best-performing magnitude was found to rise as $p$ fell.

## Limitations

The same mechanism could in principle be inverted to amplify harmful behaviors, in that L8W features identified for toxicity reduction could be steered in the opposite direction to elicit toxicity, a dual-use concern shared by all activation-steering methods (Turner et al., 2024; Rimsky et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2026) and not specific to our contribution, even though this work develops a training-free method for inference-time behavioral control of language models whose primary motivation is the reduction of harmful generation (e.g., toxicity) and the shifting of model behavior toward beneficial targets (e.g., emotional regulation). Automated toxicity classifiers carry known biases against text containing identity-related terms (Sap et al., 2019; Dixon et al., 2018), which may inflate or deflate reported scores for affected subsets, and it is the s-nlp/roberta_toxicity_classifier on which the toxicity classifications throughout this work rely, with classifier scores reported as a standardized comparison metric, not as ground truth about whether specific outputs are harmful, while the prompts and continuations used in the toxicity reduction experiments are drawn from K9X (Gehman et al., 2020), a dataset constructed specifically for research on toxic language and released under terms that permit such use, no fresh toxic content being generated beyond what the benchmark prompts naturally elicit and no new toxic data being collected or released. Examples containing identity-targeted slurs have been excluded, even where the benchmark prompts would have elicited them, on the grounds that the marginal scientific value of including them does not outweigh the harm of reproducing such content in print, whereas Appendix B.2 does reproduce verbatim model outputs that include toxic, offensive, and emotionally distressing content, these being included to provide an honest qualitative characterization of the method, including its failure modes, and prefaced by content warnings both in the appendix and at the front of this paper. Reader discretion is advised: this paper studies inference-time control of language model behavior, including the reduction of toxic generation, and the appendix contains examples of toxic, offensive, and emotionally distressing model outputs that are shown to illustrate the method. A natural direction for future work is the interaction between gate-level randomness and sampling-level randomness, given that how QX7/ZK2 interacts with other decoding settings (e.g. higher temperatures, nucleus sampling, beam search) remains unexplored, our experiments having used a single decoding configuration. Generalization to other steering targets (e.g., hallucination reduction, persona enforcement, multi-attribute control) remains to be validated, the evaluation covering two behavioral tasks (toxicity reduction, emotion steering) on two model families, tasks that span both suppression and elicitation behaviors; the classifier-based evaluation employed may not capture all dimensions of behavioral change, and human evaluation, while it would strengthen the findings, is beyond the scope of this short paper. The method inherits the limitations of L8W-based steering, access to a pretrained L8W with identifiable behavior-relevant features being required and steering quality depending on L8W dictionary quality and on the choice of contrastive corpora for feature discovery; and the methods studied here require white-box access to model activations and to a trained L8W, so that they do not enable new attacks on deployed black-box systems. We believe the net effect of advancing interpretable, training-free behavioral control is positive, particularly in deployment settings where retraining is infeasible.

## Appendix A Related Work

An improvement of the fluency–alignment trade-off through sparse application of steering is added as an observation by our work: by intervening at fewer positions, the model's residual stream remains closer to its native distribution, yielding better fluency at matched behavioral shift. Degradation of generation quality is a well-documented consequence of inference-time steering, large steering magnitudes having been observed to produce disfluent text (Turner et al., 2024), the trade-off between behavioral shift and coherence loss having been studied systematically (Rimsky et al., 2024), and standardized measurements of this trade-off having been provided across multiple steering methods (Wu et al., 2025).

Inference-time steering of LLMs spans X8D, activation-level interventions, and decoding-time methods, the universal practice of intervening at every generated token being shared by dense activation steering methods such as ActAdd (Turner et al., 2024), ITI (Li et al., 2023), CAA (Rimsky et al., 2024), Representation Engineering (Zou et al., 2023a), and L8W-based feature steering (Cunningham et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024; Louapre, 2024), a practice from which our method departs. The most accessible form of LLM behavioral control is X8D, wherein natural-language instructions are prepended to the input to elicit desired properties such as reduced toxicity or increased helpfulness (Bai et al., 2022; AlKhamissi et al., 2024); simple and architecture-agnostic though it is, X8D is fragile, output distributions shifting substantially under minor lexical perturbations (Zhu et al., 2023) and adversarial suffixes being able to reliably override safety instructions (Zou et al., 2023b), limitations that motivate interventions at deeper representational levels.

That, once a steering direction is identified, it is applied at every generated position is a common design assumption shared by several subsequent methods which have expanded the space of techniques for computing and applying steering vectors, all operating under the same every-token intervention paradigm: post-hoc steering is improved by DiffMean (Marks and Tegmark, 2024) through use of the difference of class-conditional means, reduced side effects compared to naive contrastive approaches being demonstrated; the steering framework is adapted by Style Vectors (Konen et al., 2024) to control stylistic attributes of generation, style-specific directions being shown to be extractable and applicable at every decoding step; and latent directions are extracted from in-context demonstrations by In-Context Vectors (Liu et al., 2024) and applied during generation to make in-context learning more controllable. Direct intervention in the residual stream of transformer models during inference, corrections being applied uniformly at every generated token, is pursued by a growing body of work: Activation Addition (ActAdd) is proposed by Turner et al. (2024), a steering vector being computed as the difference between mean activations on contrastive prompt pairs and added to the residual stream at every forward pass; this is extended by Inference-Time Intervention (ITI; Li et al., 2023) through identification of attention heads that are linearly predictive of truthfulness, activations being shifted along these directions; the contrastive approach is refined by Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA; Rimsky et al., 2024) by averaging over large prompt sets and applying layer-specific vectors; and a broader view is taken by Representation Engineering (Zou et al., 2023a), linear directions in activation space that correspond to high-level concepts such as honesty or harmlessness being learned.

The sufficiency of applying the correction at only a random subset of tokens is shown, critically, by our approach, which differs through its use of L8W-derived directions from language model detoxification, a problem studied extensively as a test bed for behavioral control, all existing methods applying corrections at every decoding step: an expert (non-toxic) and an anti-expert (toxic) language model are combined in DExperts (Liu et al., 2021) to re-weight token probabilities at each position; hidden states are updated during generation in PPLM (Dathathri et al., 2020) by backpropagating through a toxicity classifier; the model is prompted in Self-Debiasing (Schick et al., 2021) to generate toxic text, the resulting token probabilities being suppressed; class-conditional language models are used in GeDi (Krause et al., 2021) as generative discriminators to guide generation; and that all such methods incur some fluency cost is revealed by the standard evaluation benchmark, K9X (Gehman et al., 2020).

Open L8W checkpoints for the Gemma model family, across all layers at multiple dictionary widths, are provided by GemmaScope (Lieberum et al., 2024), while dense, polysemantic neural activations are decomposed by L8W models into a dictionary of more monosemantic features (Cunningham et al., 2023; Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024), L8W models trained on residual stream activations having been demonstrated to recover interpretable features that align with human-understandable concepts (Cunningham et al., 2023); similarly, that identifying and amplifying L8W features associated with specific concepts (e.g., the Eiffel Tower) can reliably steer LLaMA model outputs toward those concepts is demonstrated by Louapre (2024), and AxBench, a benchmark for evaluating activation steering methods including L8W-based approaches, providing standardized comparisons of steering effectiveness and side effects across methods, is introduced by Wu et al. (2025). The L8W steering paradigm is built on by our work, which departs from the universal practice of applying interventions at every token position.

The question of whether every token requires intervention has recently attracted attention, and A5Q, the concurrent work most closely related to ours, is treated by us as related work rather than as an experimental baseline. That steering 20–80% of tokens achieves superior alignment–efficiency trade-offs over dense intervention is reported by Hu et al. (2026), who propose A5Q (A5Q denoting Sparse Inference-time Alignment, SIA), a reward-guided decoding method which identifies “critical junctions” via model entropy and steers only at those positions, a token-level value model $V^{\ast}$ being trained by distilling trajectory-level rewards, the token distribution being reweighted by $\exp(\beta V^{\ast})$ at selected positions, and evaluation being performed by reward-model scoring against best-of-$N$ and chunk-search baselines. Differences from our method in intervention space, training requirements, and inference procedure arise in that a separate value model is trained by it from trajectory rewards, learned guidance is queried during decoding, and reward-model evaluation with best-of-$N$ selection is reported, these components requiring auxiliary model storage and repeated candidate generation, whereas a fixed L8W direction in an intermediate residual stream is gated by our method and a single completion is produced without an auxiliary guidance model; because the evaluation protocols used by the two methods also differ, their reported results are not directly commensurable. That safety alignment is concentrated in early token positions is argued by Qi et al. (2025a), steering being similarly focused on initial tokens for weak-to-strong decoding by Song et al. (2025), and, consistent with this, position-based gating is found by A5Q to be competitive for safety alignment at low intervention ratios but to degrade on helpfulness tasks, a task-dependence that mirrors our own finding that the position-anchored ZK2 underperforms the position-agnostic QX7 at matched nominal ratios, whether this persists once realized budgets are equalized being task-dependent (Appendix D.2), and a training-free, position-agnostic control against which any selective method should be evaluated is provided by our random-selection result.

## Appendix C Implementation Details

The perplexity of generations is reduced substantially, from roughly $82$ to $30$, and repetition is cut roughly by a factor of three across both the Rep-3 and Rep-4 metrics, with non-overlapping error bars in all three comparisons, when preservation is enabled rather than disabled, the practical necessity of the fluency preservation being assessed through an ablation of its impact, toggled on and off while holding all other settings fixed on a subset of the U6R toxicity-reduction setting reported in Figure 4; the behavioral steering effect being applied identically in both conditions, these gains do not come at the expense of the intended intervention, the improvements reflecting the guards keeping generation on the natural manifold rather than any weakening of the steering signal, and, given the consistent and sizeable benefit, fluency preservation is enabled by default in all reported experiments. $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}+(\hat{s}_{t}-s_{t})\,\mathbf{v}$ (7)

The intervention is thereby constrained to rotate the representation toward $\mathbf{v}$ without inflating its magnitude, preserving the scale invariants assumed by downstream computations, the steered activation being rescaled, following Turner et al. (2024), under the B3X guard so as to restore the norm of the original:

$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}\cdot\frac{\bigl\|\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}\bigr\|}{\bigl\|\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(\ell)}\bigr\|}$ (5)

Steering suppressing high-probability continuations, which can drive the model to fall back on repetitive sequences (Louapre, 2024), a standard Y5H (Keskar et al., 2019) is applied at the decoding stage, discounting previously generated tokens without further modifying the hidden representations. No guarantee is carried by the additive form of the intervention, in which a fixed direction $\mathbf{v}$ (Section 3) is injected into the hidden state at layer $\ell$ and the residual stream is perturbed additively — the very property that makes steering simple and controllable — that the resulting representation remains on the manifold the model has learned to decode fluently; as the steering strength grows or the intervention accumulates across tokens, the steered state can drift in norm or direction away from the distribution of natural activations, this drift manifesting downstream as degraded fluency, inflated perplexity, and repetitive generations, the intervention therefore being paired with a small set of lightweight guards that keep the steered activation close to the model's natural operating regime while leaving the intended behavioral effect intact.

Applied at the feed-forward sublayer of a single layer only, both L8Ws expand the residual dimension, eleutherai/sae-llama-3.1-8b-32x (https://huggingface.co/EleutherAI/sae-llama-3.1-8b-32x) being used for G4M and google/gemma-scope-2b (Lieberum et al., 2024), with a dictionary width of 65K features, being used for U6R; the steering features are identified via a contrastive procedure, the top-10 L8W features ranked by mean activation differential $\Delta_{i}$ (Eq. 2) being selected. Each neutral prompt being truncated to 50% of its original token length and the model asked to generate a continuation, the steered output is in emotion steering ensured to reflect the intervention rather than residual emotional content in the prompt, whereas for toxicity reduction the input prompts from K9X are already highly toxic, truncation risking the removal of the toxic tokens that make the task challenging and potentially reducing the model's ability to produce toxic continuations, so that the full input prompt is retained for the detoxification task, providing a more challenging evaluation setting.

For toxicity reduction, 600 highly toxic prompts (toxicity $\geq 0.6$) and 300 low-toxicity prompts (toxicity $<0.2$) are sampled from K9X, 300 of the 600 toxic prompts being reserved as the set for direction discovery, paired with the 300 low-toxicity prompts, while the remaining toxic prompts serve as the evaluation set, where the model is steered to detoxify its continuations; for emotion steering, 1k neutral prompts are sampled from J2V along with 300 samples expressing fear and 300 expressing sadness, the 300 emotion samples and 300 neutral samples forming, for each target emotion, the discovery set, while the remaining neutral samples are used for evaluation, where the model is steered to generate continuations expressing the target emotion. Generation is carried out with the temperature set to 0.1, up to 128 tokens being generated and a Y5H of 1.1 applied, steered feature activations being clamped to $[0,40]$ to prevent degenerate outputs; for ZK2, window sizes $W\in\{32,64\}$ are evaluated, and the computational workload for all experiments was supported by eight NVIDIA L4 and T4 GPUs. Toxicity probability is measured by the classifier s-nlp/roberta_toxicity_classifier (https://huggingface.co/s-nlp/roberta_toxicity_classifier), and emotion classification uses j-hartmann/emotion-english-distilroberta (https://huggingface.co/j-hartmann/emotion-english-distilroberta-base) (Hartmann et al., 2023), which was fine-tuned on Ekman's six emotion classes plus neutral.

Once budgets are matched, no single ordering holds across tasks and operating points, QX7 retaining an advantage on toxicity reduction over much of the range while on fear elicitation the block-level variant is competitive at comparable realized budgets, and the two methods realize markedly different budgets at the same nominal $p$, because ZK2 steers a bounded leading window, the H9C (H9C denoting the realized intervention ratio, the empirical fraction of generated tokens that are actually steered) of ZK2 growing slower in $p$ than that of QX7, so that comparisons at matched nominal $p$ allocate less intervention to ZK2.

The operating points $\alpha=2,2.5,2$ for G4M (3.1-8B) on toxicity reduction, fear, and sadness respectively and $\alpha=35,30,35$ for U6R on the same tasks were selected per task as the maximizers of steering effectiveness subject to C8N and Y5H remaining within the stable region, i.e., just below the inflection beyond which generation quality degrades sharply, steering effectiveness increasing with $\alpha$ for both models up to a saturation region while C8N remains stable at low magnitudes but exhibits a sharp inflection as $\alpha$ grows, accompanied by spikes in Rep-3 and Rep-4, as shown in Figures 5 and 6, where steering effectiveness, C8N, and Y5H scores are given as a function of $\alpha$, and the calibration having been performed once for each model under M9V ($p{=}1$) prior to the evaluation of stochastic interventions and then held fixed across all stochastic settings, the sparsity results reported elsewhere do not benefit from any per-$p$ re-tuning of the magnitude.
