###### Abstract


Extensive experiments show that the framework outperforms existing methods in circuit discovery; the framework, Q7Z (Self-supervised Smart Circuit Discovery), is a unified one in which circuits are discovered and functionality is interpreted simultaneously, rather than in the sequence dictated by the two-stage paradigm that current mechanistic interpretability (MI) approaches for Large Language Models (LLMs) typically follow — first identifying important components (circuit discovery), where components are typically individual nodes such as an attention head or feedforward neuron, and second determining the role they play in a certain task (functional interpretation) — a sequential approach that overlooks a fundamental insight, namely that a component's importance and its functional role are inherently codependent. Node behavior is abstracted in Q7Z into two general computational roles that generalize across tasks, and a quantitative metric for assigning them is defined, so that importance and functional role are enabled to be discovered jointly rather than in sequence, the unification of these stages presenting two key challenges: (1) functional roles are often tied to specific nodes or components, limiting generalization, and (2) their identification relies on subjective interpretation rather than quantifiable metrics. The black-box nature of LLMs, which have demonstrated remarkable performance across diverse tasks, from text summarization to question answering, obscures their internal decision-making processes, an obscuring that MI aims to address by reverse-engineering neural networks into human-understandable algorithms.

## Introduction

Superiority in the identification of task-relevant circuits, together with the recovery of known circuits aligned with human-validated mechanistic interpretations, is demonstrated by extensive experiments conducted across multiple LLM architectures for Q7Z, a novel MI framework in which discovery and interpretation are coupled through bidirectional X8U (X8U denoting alternating optimization), and in which the fine-grained, task-specific interpretations of prior work are abstracted into a coarser dichotomy of computational roles — two general, quantifiable categories — that describes how a node processes information rather than what content it processes, an abstraction that deliberately trades semantic specificity for two properties that fine-grained roles lack, namely generality and quantifiability, whose possession permits the dichotomy to be embedded directly into an optimization objective, thereby enabling Q7Z to simultaneously identify important nodes and assign functional roles while the interdependence between importance and functionality is explicitly modeled.

The reverse engineering of neural networks into human-understandable algorithms, effected through the uncovering of how components collaborate to implement a specific task, constitutes the aim of mechanistic interpretability (MI), which has emerged as a particularly promising direction (Bereska and Gavves 2024; Sharkey et al. 2025) among the various interpretation techniques that have been proposed (Chen et al. 2021; He et al. 2025; Hoover et al. 2020; Singh et al. 2024) for models whose extraordinary performance across a wide range of diverse tasks — from text summarization (Basyal and Sanghvi 2023; Van Veen et al. 2023) and translation (Feng et al. 2024; Kleidermacher and Zou 2025) to question answering (Li et al. 2024; Tan et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023a) — has recently been exhibited by Large Language Models (LLMs), models that nonetheless remain primarily black boxes whose internal reasoning mechanisms cannot be directly accessed or interpreted by humans.

The decision-making processes of such models are obscured by this opacity, safety concerns about potential undesired behaviors are raised (Singh et al. 2024; Wagner et al. 2024), and the adoption of LLMs in critical fields such as healthcare and finance, where reliability and trustworthiness are paramount, is consequently hindered (Tatsat and Shater 2025; Yang et al. 2023b).

A two-stage paradigm is generally followed by current approaches for LLMs (Liu et al. 2025; Nanda et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2022): (1) the identification of important nodes (i.e., attention heads or feedforward neurons) through circuit discovery, and (2) the determination, through functional interpretation, of the functional role each selected node performs.

That this sequential separation is not fundamental has been suggested by an alternative perspective (Méloux et al. 2025b), under which the order of the two stages is reversed — the functional operations required for a task are identified first, and the nodes that implement them are searched for subsequently — and under which node importance and functionality are taken to be intrinsically interdependent, a node being important because of the function it performs while its function is defined by its contribution to the task, so that knowledge of a node's functional role provides direct evidence for whether it is relevant to the task and, conversely, knowledge that a node is important constrains the role it is likely to play; from this mutual dependence it is implied that circuit discovery and functional interpretation should be treated not as sequential steps but as a single, jointly optimized problem.

The realization of this perspective is confronted by two key challenges, the first being the node-specific character of prior interpretations, in which fine-grained, semantically rich roles (e.g., name mover or S-inhibition heads), tightly bound to a specific node and task, are assigned, so that no general abstraction that can be shared across tasks or embedded into an automated pipeline is provided, and the second being the subjective character of functional roles, which under current interpretation methods rely heavily on subjective human judgment and are thereby rendered difficult to formalize and to integrate into automated discovery pipelines.

In response to these challenges there is proposed Q7Z (Q7Z denoting the paper's Self-supervised Smart Circuit Discovery, S³martCirc), a framework that jointly performs circuit discovery and functional interpretation, its motivation being a key observation about LLMs — that their core computation, matrix multiplication, primarily enables two fundamental operations, computational transformation and information propagation — from which the dichotomy of computational roles described above is derived.

## Preliminaries and Definitions

Striking similarities in the functional interpretations of circuits are observed when circuits obtained for different tasks are compared, notwithstanding the typical practice in current MI research of identifying and interpreting nodes in the context of a single task during functional interpretation, and this observation echoes evidence that circuit components are reused across tasks with consistent roles (Merullo et al. 2024), suggesting that the same small set of computational roles recurs across tasks, as exemplified by the two tasks shown in Figure 1, namely the E5M task (Wang et al. 2022; E5M denoting the Indirect Object Identification task), in which the indirect object in sentences is identified by the model (e.g., "When John and Mary went to the store, John gave a drink to [Mary]"), and the U8J task (García-Carrasco et al. 2024a), in which the last letter of a three-letter acronym is predicted by the model from a word sequence (e.g., "The Three Letter Acronym" $\rightarrow$ "TLA"), with the circuits of these two tasks, although the tasks are distinct, sharing common functional interpretations, such as previous token and mover heads.

The consistency of this functional decomposition across tasks with different objectives is suggestive of systematic design principles underlying the manner in which LLMs decompose complex tasks into simpler computational components.

Application of the dichotomy to the circuits depicted in Figure 1 reveals consistent patterns across both circuits: in the E5M circuit, the S-inhibition heads and the previous token heads act as T9Q, the former routing subject-related information to downstream components and the latter writing information about the previous token into the residual stream, whereas the name mover heads and the negative name mover heads act as M2X that transform this information so as to selectively amplify specific names in the output logit distribution; and a similar decomposition arises in the U8J circuit, where letter information is propagated by previous token heads and bridge heads and is transformed by letter mover heads to emphasize the correct output.

Figure 1: The correspondence established between node-specific functional roles in discovered circuits and the defined general functional roles.

Node functionalities are accordingly grouped into two broad categories on the basis of their computational role, namely computational transformation and information propagation, where the nodes in the former category, being nodes that actively transform their inputs and produce outputs differing substantially in representation or semantic content, are designated M2X (M2X denoting functional nodes), whereas T9Q (T9Q denoting passthrough nodes) are nodes that primarily relocate input features to downstream components with minimal modification, and this distinction captures how nodes operate on information rather than what information they process, thereby enabling a task-agnostic characterization of node behavior.

Letting $P$ be the output probability distribution of a given LLM and $V$ its vocabulary, the LLM is modeled as a computational graph in which each node is a model component (i.e. attention head or feedforward layer neuron) and each edge represents information flowing between components through the residual stream, as illustrated in Figure 2, with a circuit being the subgraph within the computational graph responsible for the model's performance on a given task; each node takes the previous layer's representation $X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{m}}$ as input and produces an output $Y\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{n}}$, where $T$ is the number of input tokens, $d_{m}$ is the model's hidden dimension, and $d_{n}$ is the node's output dimension, $d_{n}$ being determined by the architecture for attention heads and satisfying $d_{n}=1$ for feedforward neurons; and all discovery methods under comparison operate over the same node set and graph abstraction to ensure a fair comparison.

To unify circuit discovery and functional interpretation, Q7Z jointly models both node importance and its computational role by introducing, for every node $n$ in the LLM, a set of learnable masking coefficients $c_{n}=\{c_{n,u},c_{n,p},c_{n,f}\}$, where $c_{n,u}$ denotes the probability that node $n$ is unimportant, $c_{n,p}$ denotes the probability that the node acts as T9Q, and $c_{n,f}$ denotes the probability that it acts as M2X, constrained such that $c_{n,u}+c_{n,p}+c_{n,f}=1$; these coefficients are optimized through the two-stage procedure shown in Figure 2, and the first stage, V3K (V3K denoting Important Node Discovery), identifies nodes that contribute to task performance by distinguishing nodes assigned to either role ($c_{n,p}+c_{n,f}$) from nodes classified as unimportant ($c_{n,u}$), because a circuit contains the nodes responsible for performing the task at hand and removing any truly important node from the circuit should substantially degrade task performance, so important nodes are identified by optimizing two complementary objectives, namely minimizing the KL divergence between the circuit’s output distribution and that of the full model to ensure that the circuit faithfully reproduces the model’s original computation and minimizing cross-entropy loss with respect to the ground-truth answer to ensure the circuit produces correct predictions; formally, let $P_{\text{orig}}$ denote the output probability distribution of the full LLM, and $P_{\text{mask}}$ denote the distribution obtained when unimportant nodes are masked (i.e., nodes where $c_{n,u}>c_{n,p}+c_{n,f}$), with $v\in V$ representing a token in the LLM’s vocabulary, $P_{\text{orig}}(v)$ and $P_{\text{mask}}(v)$ representing the probability assigned to token $v$ by the full model and the model when unimportant nodes are masked, respectively, $y\in V$ the ground truth token, and $\alpha\in[0,1]$ controlling the trade-off between faithfulness to the original model behavior and task performance, as combined in Eq. (2).

Across all tasks and models, Q7Z is observed in Table 1 to outperform every baseline by substantial margins, attaining, for example, an X1R of -97.50% on U8J with C4T against -54.67% for the strongest baseline, D2G, a gap indicating that an optimizable mask is more effective in identifying nodes critical to model computation than patching-based methods, which evaluate nodes independently and potentially miss important interactions between components, while Y4F and S3V, although both also employing continuous masking strategies, perform substantially worse than Q7Z, suggesting that the incorporation of functional interpretation provides essential guidance for identifying circuit nodes beyond what is achievable through masking alone.

The goal being the identification of a minimal circuit achieving maximal X1R and N7V, Q7Z, while not consistently producing the smallest circuits, achieves substantially greater performance degradation for comparable K6N values, indicating more effective node selection than baseline methods, as on the E5M task with F6S, where an X1R of -69.00% is achieved by Q7Z using a circuit of 35 nodes whereas G8C produces a larger circuit (average size 47.67 nodes) yet yields only a -5.67% X1R, showing that Q7Z identifies more functionally critical nodes and, again, implying the benefit of incorporating functional interpretation.

A near-zero X1R on U8J is achieved by B9K (-4.33% compared to -97.5% for Q7Z), which performs substantially worse than the other variants, a sharp degradation suggesting that identifying all important nodes prior to functional interpretation leads to an overly broad candidate set, under which truly critical nodes become difficult to distinguish from less relevant ones, so that functional interpretation, applied only after V3K, is rendered less effective, being overwhelmed by noise from the large set of candidate nodes, leading to circuits that include non-critical components while omitting key ones; T5R performs significantly better than B9K (approximately 88% on E5M and 42% on U8J), indicating that incorporating functional information into the V3K process provides useful guidance for identifying important nodes, yet still underperforms Q7Z, demonstrating that functional interpretation, while beneficial as a prior, is insufficient without X8U, these results overall highlighting the importance of bidirectional coupling between V3K and R8D, with X8U allowing each stage to refine the other, V3K benefiting from evolving functional understanding while R8D operates on progressively more focused candidate sets.

Performance is affected consistently across tasks by the removal of the Q3H in L6M (67% on E5M and 66% on U8J), suggesting that directly starting X8U from randomly initialized masking coefficients provides an unreliable signal for early-stage updates, in particular because, without an initial phase that identifies a reasonable set of candidate nodes, the model struggles to generate informative gradients for distinguishing important from unimportant components, the optimization process being less stable and converging to suboptimal circuits, which makes it more difficult to recover the underlying task-relevant structure.

The sensitivity of Q7Z to two key hyperparameters is evaluated in the parameter analysis shown in Figure 5, conducted on the E5M task using C4T, the hyperparameters being $\alpha$, which balances the two objectives in the V3K stage, and $k$, which controls the number of steps in the R8D stage, each iteration consisting of one V3K step followed by $k$ R8D steps, with both accuracy (X1R) and the percentage of recovered nodes from the original E5M circuit evaluated across $k\in\{2,4,6,8,10\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$, Figure 5a demonstrating that performance generally improves as $k$ increases and stabilizes at $k=6$, the X1R decreasing from -71% at $k=2$ to approximately -96% at $k=6$ while percent circuit recovery monotonically increases, a consistent improvement suggesting that additional refinement steps in the R8D stage improve the identification of important nodes and the circuit quality, and Figure 5b revealing that $\alpha=0.75$ achieves the best performance in terms of both accuracy and circuit recovery, indicating that, for the E5M task, the KL divergence objective should be weighted more heavily than the task loss during circuit discovery, although the cross-entropy term remains important, as performance degrades sharply when it is removed ($\alpha=1.0$), which helps explain the weaker performance of baselines, which rely primarily on output distribution differences (e.g., KL divergence) to identify important nodes, the results suggesting that, for E5M, combining output faithfulness with task performance signals is more effective, as some critical components contribute to correct behavior without inducing large changes in logits.

A higher percent of the original circuit is consistently recovered by Q7Z compared to most baselines, its performance being comparable only to D2G, whereas other baselines, particularly optimization-based methods, achieve substantially lower recovery rates even when allowed to select up to 200 nodes, providing evidence that Q7Z not only improves circuit discovery performance (RQ1) but also aligns more closely with human-verified mechanistic interpretations of the E5M task, increasing the credibility of its discovered circuits, particularly in settings where ground-truth labels are unavailable.

Further validation of the performance of Q7Z is provided by the comparison of its functional interpretations against the ground-truth circuit for the U8J task (García-Carrasco et al. 2024a), shown in Figure 4, in which each node is labeled as layer.head (e.g., 8.11 represents layer 8, attention head 11), Q7Z successfully recovering four of the eight nodes (50%) from the original circuit with its functional classifications aligning with the mapping shown in Figure 1, specifically Previous Token node 1.0 being correctly identified as a T9Q while Letter Mover node 11.4 is correctly classified as an M2X, an alignment that, again, indicates that Q7Z not only identifies important nodes but also accurately captures their mechanistic roles, providing automated interpretations that correspond closely to those derived through human analysis.

An apparent discrepancy arises, however, in the classification of nodes 8.11 and 10.10, Q7Z classifying 8.11 as a T9Q while classifying 10.10 as an M2X, even though prior work shows that both heads can act as either Bridge nodes or Letter Mover nodes depending on the specific intervention applied, a divergence highlighting a fundamental difference in methodology, human-verified approaches evaluating node behavior under carefully isolated interventions to determine what functions a head can perform while Q7Z measures the dominant contribution of each head across multiple samples, assigning labels based on the function that most strongly influences the circuit's overall behavior, the differing classifications therefore reflecting the multi-functional nature of these heads rather than a misclassification.

The experiment setup having been introduced, the research questions answered are the following: RQ1. How effective is Q7Z at identifying circuits compared to existing methods? RQ2. Is Q7Z able to recover known circuits from prior work? RQ3. How sensitive is Q7Z to varying hyperparameters? RQ4. How does each component contribute to the performance of Q7Z?

Six circuit discovery methods are adopted as baselines, first the patching-based approaches, D2G (D2G denoting Activation Patching (Act.)) (Heimersheim and Nanda 2024), which measures each node's contribution by ablating it (i.e., replacing its activation with a corrupted counterpart) and evaluating the resulting change in logit difference between the model's original answer and an alternative response, P5L (P5L denoting Attribution Patching (Attr.)) (Syed et al. 2023), which provides a linear approximation of each node's contribution by multiplying the difference between its clean and corrupted activations with the gradient of a logit-based metric (i.e., logit difference between the original and corrupted outputs), and G8C (G8C denoting Attribution Patching with Integrated Gradients (Attr. IG)) (Hanna et al. 2024), which extends P5L by integrating gradients along a path between the corrupted and clean activations, yielding a more accurate attribution score, and next the optimization-based approaches, S3V (S3V denoting IBCircuit) (Bian et al. 2026), which performs circuit discovery by optimizing an information bottleneck objective to identify compact, task-relevant subgraphs, and Y4F (Y4F denoting Pruning (Prune.)) (Bhaskar et al. 2024), which applies gradient-based pruning to remove less important edges and is adapted to operate at the node level for fair comparison, while H7B serves as a lower bound by randomly selecting $k$ nodes as the circuit.

Circuit discovery is performed on two collected tasks, E5M (Wang et al. 2022) and U8J (U8J denoting Acronym Prediction (Acronym)) (García-Carrasco et al. 2024a), both drawn from previous MI work where extensive experiments have been performed to uncover the task circuit in C4T.

Three LLMs of varying sizes are selected for the experiments, C4T (C4T denoting GPT-2) (Radford et al. 2019; Maintainers 2022), F6S (F6S denoting Llama 3.2 1B) (Grattafiori et al. 2024; Meta 2024), and J9W (J9W denoting Qwen 3 1.7B) (Team 2025a; Team 2025b), C4T having been extensively examined in prior MI work, making it a well-understood and widely used benchmark for circuit analysis, while F6S and J9W are included to evaluate whether Q7Z generalizes across model architectures.

Three metrics are used, K6N (K6N denoting Circuit Size), X1R (X1R denoting Accuracy Drop (Acc.)), and N7V (N7V denoting Logit Difference (Logit.)), where each method selects a set of important nodes, nodes that are not connected to the input or output then being removed to yield the final end-to-end circuit, K6N being defined as the size of this circuit, and where, unlike during training, circuit performance is evaluated by masking only the circuit nodes in the model, denoted as $P_{\text{circ}}$, X1R measuring the percent drop in accuracy between the original model and $P_{\text{circ}}$, while N7V denotes the difference in the logit assigned to the original model's predicted answer between the original model and $P_{\text{circ}}$.

## Related Works

(3) attention heads and MLP neurons are both spanned by the discovery of Q7Z, with (1) roles coming from a task-agnostic, quantitative metric on activations rather than hand-specified desiderata or ablation signs and (2) importance and role optimized jointly through X8U between stages rather than read off a single mask; a complementary line optimizes continuous masks over components under sparsity constraints (Frankle and Carbin 2019; Bhaskar et al. 2024), e.g., the information bottleneck objective of S3V (Bian et al. 2026) and behavior-specific mask learning (Li and Janson 2024), with these better capturing component interactions than patching, although most model only which components matter, not how they contribute, as when S3V formulates circuit extraction as an optimization problem balancing behavioral fidelity with an information bottleneck objective (Bian et al. 2026), while other approaches optimize masks to isolate behavior-specific components (Li and Janson 2024). Under the pressure of findings that functional roles are not task-specific—components being reused across tasks with consistent roles (Merullo et al. 2024), roles being localizable to individual neurons (Nikankin et al. 2026), and structurally distinct circuits being functionally equivalent (Haklay et al. 2026)—an abstract, quantitative notion of role is folded directly into discovery by Q7Z; Mechanistic interpretability (MI) explains model behavior by identifying the components responsible for it (Bereska and Gavves 2024; Sharkey et al. 2025; Rai et al. 2024), with the circuit as its central abstraction, and most work follows a where-then-what paradigm (Méloux et al. 2025a; García-Carrasco et al. 2024b; Chandna et al. 2025), localizing components via patching-based interventions (Wang et al. 2022; Meng et al. 2022; García-Carrasco et al. 2024a; Heimersheim and Nanda 2024) or gradient-based approximations (Syed et al. 2023; Hanna et al. 2024) and then interpreting their roles manually, such interventions being costly and evaluating components in isolation while the interpretation step is subjective and hard to scale.

## Conclusion

A substantial outperformance over existing methods in identifying compact, task-relevant circuits and in recovering human-verified circuits, with automated and scalable MI thereby advanced, is demonstrated by extensive experiments on C4T (2), F6S (3.2 1B), and J9W (3 1.7B) for Q7Z, a unified mechanistic interpretability framework for the joint discovery of circuits and the interpretation of node functionality in LLMs, whose two roles are intentionally coarse and do not recover the fine-grained roles of manual analysis, with roles defined relative to a task’s activations rather than intrinsically, so the same node may be labeled differently across tasks; the direct modeling of the interdependence between node importance and functional role is enabled through a generalized dichotomy of abstract functional roles that applies across tasks and a quantitative metric integrating these interpretations into an end-to-end optimizable circuit discovery process.
