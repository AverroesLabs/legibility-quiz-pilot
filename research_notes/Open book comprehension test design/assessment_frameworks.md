# Assessment-design frameworks and cognitive-science research on testing deep understanding of a text (open-book focus)

Scope note: almost every finding below comes from studies of human students (undergraduates, medical students, school pupils). Wherever a mechanism depends on human memory limits, motivation, or study behaviour, it may not carry over to LLM readers that hold the whole document in context. These caveats are flagged in the Inferences sections. Coverage was limited to about 20 tool calls, so several sub-questions are only partly sourced. See Gaps.

## 1. What do ECD, revised Bloom, SOLO and UbD's six facets prescribe for writing items that elicit evidence of understanding?

### Takeaway
ECD gives the logic: first define the claim about the reader (student/proficiency model), then the observable evidence that would support it (evidence model, including scoring rules), then the task features that would produce that evidence (task model). Revised Bloom, SOLO and the six facets supply vocabulary for the claim and the observable. For whole-document understanding, the most useful targets are Bloom's "Understand" subprocesses (inferring, explaining, comparing) and "Analyze/Evaluate" (attributing, critiquing), SOLO's relational and extended-abstract levels, and the UbD facets of explanation, interpretation, application and perspective.

### Cited Findings
**Evidence-centered design (Mislevy, Almond & Lukas 2003, ETS RR-03-16)**
- ECD has three core models. The **student model** (later renamed the **proficiency model**) states which knowledge, skills or attributes are assessed and is represented as a fragment of a Bayesian network. The **evidence model** links latent competencies to observables: it has evaluative rules that turn work products into scores and a statistical submodel that combines evidence across tasks. The **task model** specifies the situations designed to elicit that evidence, i.e. the features a task needs to produce observable evidence of the target skill. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019 (reproduces the Mislevy, Almond & Lukas 2003 figure)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00853/pdf); [Mislevy et al. ECD technical report, PADI TR9](https://padi.sri.com/downloads/TR9_ECD.pdf); [ERIC ED483027](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED483027.pdf)
- (I did not open the full ETS report. The model definitions above come from secondary descriptions in the search results.)

**Revised Bloom's taxonomy (Anderson, Krathwohl et al. 2001; Krathwohl 2002 overview, Theory Into Practice 41:4, 212–218)**
- The revision has two dimensions. **Knowledge**: A Factual, B Conceptual ("the interrelationships among the basic elements within a larger structure that enable them to function together"), C Procedural, D Metacognitive. **Cognitive Process**: 1 Remember (1.1 Recognizing, 1.2 Recalling); 2 Understand, defined as "determining the meaning of instructional messages" (2.1 Interpreting, 2.2 Exemplifying, 2.3 Classifying, 2.4 Summarizing, 2.5 Inferring, 2.6 Comparing, 2.7 Explaining); 3 Apply (3.1 Executing, 3.2 Implementing); 4 Analyze, defined as "breaking material into its constituent parts and detecting how the parts relate to one another and to an overall structure or purpose" (4.1 Differentiating, 4.2 Organizing, 4.3 Attributing); 5 Evaluate (5.1 Checking, 5.2 Critiquing); 6 Create (6.1 Generating, 6.2 Planning, 6.3 Producing). — [Krathwohl 2002 (PDF)](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)
- The hierarchy is relaxed: "some cognitive processes associated with Understand (e.g., Explaining) are more cognitively complex than at least one of the cognitive processes associated with Apply (e.g., Executing)." — [Krathwohl 2002](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)
- The **Taxonomy Table** (knowledge × process) classifies objectives, instructional activities *and assessments*, so misalignment shows up as cells that are empty or over-represented. Historically the taxonomy's main use has been to "classify curricular objectives and test items in order to show the breadth" of coverage. — [Krathwohl 2002](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)

**SOLO taxonomy (Biggs & Collis 1982)**
- SOLO has five levels of increasing structural complexity: prestructural, unistructural, multistructural, relational, extended abstract. At the multistructural level "connections being made, but significance to overall meaning is missing". At the relational level there is "full connections made, and synthesis of parts to the overall meaning". At the extended-abstract level the response "goes beyond subject and makes links to other concepts; generalises." — [University of Stuttgart summary of Biggs](https://www.zlw.uni-stuttgart.de/dokumente/Hochschuldidaktik/Lernziele-formulieren-SOLO-Taxonomy.pdf); [CUHK SOLO description](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/policy/assessment/SOLO-description.pdf)
- Example verbs for extended abstract: "Create, Synthesise, Hypothesise, Validate, Predict, Debate, Theorise" (CUHK); "theorizes, hypothesizes, reflects" (another summary). Verb lists differ from source to source, and I did not see the original 1982 tables. — [CUHK](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/policy/assessment/SOLO-description.pdf); [Shakespeare Reloaded](https://shakespearereloaded.edu.au/node/80)

**Understanding by Design, six facets (Wiggins & McTighe)**
- The facets are explanation, interpretation, application, perspective, empathy and self-knowledge. They were "conceived as six equal and suggestive indicators of understanding… used to develop, select, or critique assessment tasks and prompts. They were never intended to be a hierarchy." Not every facet suits every domain: application, interpretation and explanation fit mathematics most naturally. — [SERC Carleton summary](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/1008.html); [Duke handout, Six Facets](https://sites.duke.edu/aagc/files/2022/08/C9-SixFacetsUnderstanding.pdf)
- Explanation facet: "Sophisticated and apt explanation and theories that provide knowledge and justified accounts of events, actions and ideas. Why is this so?" — [Duke handout](https://sites.duke.edu/aagc/files/2022/08/C9-SixFacetsUnderstanding.pdf)
- Within backward design, facet-based tasks are meant to be culminating performance tasks. — [SERC](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/1008.html); [ASCD 1998](https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/understanding-by-design-Decermber-1998)
- All of these frameworks are design guidance and conceptual schemes, not controlled evidence that particular item types are valid. Treat them as **advice**.

### Inferences
- ECD applied to paper quizzes would run as follows. *Claim*: "the reader has a correct situation model of method M, result R, and the conditions under which R holds." *Evidence*: a rubric-scored answer that correctly predicts R′ under a modified condition and gives the causal reason. *Task model features*: the item states a perturbation the paper does not discuss, so the answer cannot be copied out, and the rubric scores the reasoning chain, not keyword overlap.
- Mapping the requested item types onto the frameworks:
  - Counterfactual prediction maps to Bloom 2.5 Inferring / 2.7 Explaining on Conceptual knowledge, SOLO extended abstract ("predict, hypothesise"), and UbD explanation/application.
  - Edge cases and failure conditions map to Bloom 5.1 Checking / 5.2 Critiquing and UbD perspective.
  - "Why does the result follow?" maps to Bloom 2.7 and SOLO relational.
  - "What is the paper's overall argument?" maps to Bloom 4.2 Organizing / 4.3 Attributing.
- The SOLO levels can double as a rubric scale. Unistructural/multistructural (lists correct facts but does not connect them to the conclusion) versus relational (connects method to result to conclusion) is the textbase-versus-situation-model distinction in rubric form.
- Empathy and self-knowledge facets transfer poorly to LLM readers. A rough analogue of self-knowledge is calibrated confidence (see the three-tier items in section 3).

### Gaps
- I did not open the primary ECD report (Mislevy, Almond & Lukas 2003), Wiggins & McTighe's book (with its facet-specific prompt stems), or Biggs & Collis's original verb tables. Facet prompts for interpretation, application, perspective, empathy and self-knowledge were not retrieved from a primary source.
- I found no controlled study validating that items written to higher Bloom/SOLO levels actually measure understanding better. These remain design heuristics.

## 2. Kintsch's construction-integration model: textbase vs situation-model measures; McNamara & Kintsch 1996; what makes text "hard" for high-knowledge readers

### Takeaway
Under the CI model, readers build a textbase (the propositions the text states explicitly) and a situation model (text integrated with prior knowledge). Recall and questions about explicit content measure the textbase. Inference questions, inference verification, problem-solving and transfer measure the situation model. McNamara & Kintsch (1996) show the two kinds of measure can dissociate: a fully coherent text helps recall and multiple-choice performance, but high-knowledge readers do better on open-ended inference questions after reading a *low*-coherence text. Gaps in a text force readers to generate inferences, and that is how the situation model gets built.

### Cited Findings
- Kintsch (1994), "Text comprehension, memory, and learning," *American Psychologist* 49, 294–303, doi 10.1037/0003-066x.49.4.294. Subjects recalled texts and verified old statements as well as inference statements. The difference between remembering a text and learning from it "appears to be a matter of how complete and elaborate a situation model is constructed". Textbase and situation model are described as contributing links and nodes to a single network. Kintsch draws an analogy to problem solving with understanding versus rote memorization of procedures, and notes that reading can feel understood with very little effort. — [Kintsch 1994 (Scribd copy)](https://www.scribd.com/document/584304292/Kin-Tsch); [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/text-comprehension-memory-and-learning/812135695781986304-11948)
- Measurement convention: textbase measures are recall of explicit ideas and answers to questions about explicit information. Situation-model measures are tasks that require inference. In a study with spatial texts, free recall depended on the propositional textbase while inference questions required a situation model. In sentence verification, novices made quick judgments from surface and textbase features while experts also used their situation model. — [Long et al. 2006, "Assessing text representations" (PDF)](https://sites.uw.edu/ccdl/files//2013/12/Long_Assessing-text_2006.pdf); [Schmalhofer & Glavanov / Bern "Propositional and situational representations of text"](https://boris.unibe.ch/53006)
- McNamara & Kintsch (1996), *Discourse Processes* 22(3), 247–288, doi 10.1080/01638539609544975. In two experiments, the high-coherence text produced better recall and multiple-choice performance. High-knowledge readers did better on **open-ended questions after reading the low-coherence text**. Reading times suggested the low-coherence text required more inference processes, which "are more likely to be successful and useful for high-knowledge readers." — [Scilit abstract](https://scilit.com/publications/74c26bef2b7c9f5a1f1ef4c44095b868); [Benjamins record](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/28451)
- Companion study: McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), *Cognition and Instruction* 14, 1–43. Low-knowledge readers benefit from coherent text. High-knowledge readers benefit from minimally coherent text (the "reverse cohesion effect"). — [same search summary; Scilit / Benjamins records above](https://scilit.com/publications/74c26bef2b7c9f5a1f1ef4c44095b868)
- Replication caveat: later work reports that the knowledge × coherence interaction was not consistently found. — [Utrecht: Coherence marking, prior knowledge, and comprehension of informative texts](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/coherence-marking-prior-knowledge-and-comprehension-of-informativ/)
- Chi et al. (1994) used a related split. Understanding was measured with complex questions, including inducing a component's function when the text stated it only implicitly, and with a mental-model analysis of protocols. High self-explainers all reached the correct mental model of the circulatory system, whereas many unprompted and low explainers did not. — [Andy Matuschak notes on Chi et al. 1994](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Chi_et_al._-_1994_-_Eliciting_self-explanations_improves_understanding); [ASU self-explanation summary](https://education.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/self-explanation_effect.pdf)

### Inferences
- The McNamara–Kintsch dissociation is the core reason recall-style or MCQ items cannot stand in for understanding. A text (or reader) can score well on textbase measures while showing no situation-model gain, and the reverse.
- For LLM readers in an open-book setting, the textbase is effectively perfect because the text is in context. Every explicit-content item therefore measures retrieval only. Situation-model items (bridging inferences across distant sections, consequences the paper never states, predictions under perturbation) are the only ones that discriminate.
- The "reverse cohesion" idea suggests a legibility test should not equate "easy to read" with "understood". A paper that spells everything out may produce high textbase scores, while one that leaves inferential gaps may produce better situation models in a knowledgeable reader. This is a hypothesis for LLMs, not established.
- An LLM reader may answer inference items from its prior knowledge of the field instead of from the paper, the analogue of a high-knowledge human reader. Items should hinge on paper-specific details (this paper's numbers, ablations, setup) so that prior knowledge alone fails. A no-document baseline condition would detect this.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary sources for **sorting tasks** (e.g. card-sorting of concepts as a situation-model measure) or for specific **bridging-inference** verification paradigms. Searches returned nothing direct.
- I did not open the full McNamara & Kintsch 1996 paper, so the exact question taxonomy (text-based vs bridging-inference vs elaborative-inference vs problem-solving questions) and effect sizes are unverified here. My background recollection is that they used separate text-based and bridging question categories plus problem-solving and sorting tasks. Confirm this before citing.
- I found no research on what makes a text hard for high-knowledge readers beyond the reverse-cohesion literature.

## 3. Transfer (near vs far) and concept inventories / two- and three-tier diagnostic items

### Takeaway
Two-tier diagnostic items (Treagust 1988) pair a content answer with a reason tier, and both tiers' distractors come from documented misconceptions. Getting credit requires correct reasoning as well as a correct answer, which cuts lucky guesses and separates a memorized answer from an understood one. Three-tier variants add a confidence rating to distinguish misconceptions from lack of knowledge. The supporting sources I reached were thin. The Force Concept Inventory and the transfer literature were not retrieved from primary sources in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Treagust (1988) introduced two-tier tests with a ten-step development procedure in three phases: define the content, gather information on student misconceptions (from the literature and interviews), and develop the diagnostic test. Tier 1 is a multiple-choice content question. Tier 2 asks for the reason, either multiple choice with misconception-based distractors or open-ended to surface unanticipated alternative conceptions. — [DergiPark article on two-tier tests](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/1136174); [DergiPark 2026 article](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4854227)
- One secondary source claims the two-tier format lowers the probability of a correct guess to about 4%. This is unverified against the original. — [DergiPark 2026](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4854227)
- Two-tier tests have been used in medical education but, per one source, not yet with confidence ratings in that field. — [DergiPark 2026](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/4854227)
- Durning et al. (2016) list one included open-book study that used Barnett and Ceci's transfer taxonomy along with Bloom's taxonomy (72 undergraduates, 2-day delay, GRE-style passages and MCQs). Transfer frameworks have thus been applied to open-book testing research. — [Durning et al. 2016, Acad Med 91(4) (PDF)](https://blogs.cornell.edu/vetess/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)
- Agarwal & Roediger (2011): expecting an open-book test impaired long-term retention *and transfer* compared with expecting a closed-book test, despite better initial open-book performance. — [WUSTL profile: Expectancy of an open-book test decreases performance on a delayed closed-book test](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expectancy-of-an-open-book-test-decreases-performance-on-a-delaye/)

### Inferences
- The two-tier structure maps directly onto rubric grading for LLM readers: score the conclusion and the justification separately, and give full credit only when both are right. Misconception-based distractors become "plausible wrong readings of the paper", such as confusing a correlational finding with a causal one, generalizing beyond the tested model scale, or misattributing which ablation drove the effect. These can serve as foils in MCQ items or as named failure modes in rubrics.
- Confidence tiers could measure calibration, i.e. whether the LLM knows when the paper does not settle a question. That also gives a natural place for "the paper does not say" or "cannot be determined" options (compare the "Not in the text" option in Sedlmayr & Weissenbacher, section 4).
- Near versus far transfer for paper quizzes: *near* means applying the paper's method to a slightly modified setup in the same domain. *Far* means applying the paper's insight to a structurally analogous problem in a different domain or threat model. Far-transfer items are least answerable by lookup but hardest to grade against a rubric.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve primary sources on the Force Concept Inventory (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer 1992), on its use of common-sense misconceptions as distractors, or on three-tier tests (e.g. Peşman & Eryılmaz 2010). These are background-knowledge claims only.
- I did not retrieve primary transfer reviews (Barnett & Ceci 2002 taxonomy; Bransford & Schwartz 1999 "preparation for future learning"). Cite them only after checking.

## 4. Open-book exams: what open access changes, and how to write questions that cannot be answered by lookup

### Takeaway
Controlled and classroom evidence shows open-book (OB) testing raises initial scores on lookup-able questions, and the gain disappears on delay. Overall OB and CB scores are often similar, but the two formats appear to measure partly different abilities, and time spent looking things up correlates *negatively* with score. A small 2025 within-subject study found that comprehension tests with the text available had *better* external validity than text-removed tests. The commonly stated rationale for OB exams is that they free the examiner to ask questions "that cannot simply be looked up", but the reviews say this has rarely been tested directly with item formats designed for OB.

### Cited Findings
**Agarwal, Karpicke, Kang, Roediger & McDermott (2008), Applied Cognitive Psychology 22, 861–876 (controlled lab study)**
- Subjects studied prose passages, then restudied or took an open-book test (passage available), a closed-book test with or without feedback, or a "simultaneous answering" test (reading and answering at once). A final closed-book test came one week later. Questions were short-answer, in passage order, and some asked why (e.g. "Why did the Voyager have instruments that would measure ultraviolet and infrared light?"). — [Agarwal et al. 2008 PDF](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2008/2008_Agarwal_etal_ACP.pdf)
- Initial test: open-book conditions M = .82 vs closed-book M = .70, d = 1.12. One week later: all test conditions beat study-once; closed-book with feedback .68, open-book .65, closed-book without feedback .59, non-studied control .18. "Open-book testing led to better initial performance than closed-book testing, but this benefit did not persist." — [Agarwal et al. 2008](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2008/2008_Agarwal_etal_ACP.pdf)
- The authors' interpretation (Bjork & Bjork retrieval strength vs storage strength): open-book tests raise the momentary accessibility of information, and retrieving easily accessible information produces small storage gains. They also note that students who expect an open-book test may prepare less (citing Ioannidou 1997; Boniface 1985; Pauker 1974). — [Agarwal et al. 2008](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2008/2008_Agarwal_etal_ACP.pdf)

**Durning et al. (2016), "Comparing Open-Book and Closed-Book Examinations: A Systematic Review," Academic Medicine 91(4), 583–599**
- Searched MEDLINE, ERIC, Embase and PsycINFO through June 2013. Included 37 of 4,192 studies across all fields. Outcomes: preparation inconclusive (students may prepare more for CBEs); OBEs take longer to complete; performance studies "favored CBE, particularly when preparation for CBE was greater"; little difference in testing effects. Conclusion: insufficient evidence to use either format exclusively. — [Durning et al. 2016 PDF](https://blogs.cornell.edu/vetess/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf); [GWU HSRC record](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/smhs_hs_facpubs/130)
- The review states the OB rationale explicitly: the format "frees the examiner to focus questions on the test taker's ability to apply knowledge (i.e., testing what cannot simply be 'looked up')", and lookup time can *increase* difficulty. Caveats: in most studies students had little or no OBE experience, and "different questions across different examination formats may… be required to enable the advantages of OBEs to be recognized". Most included exams were MCQ. — [Durning et al. 2016](https://blogs.cornell.edu/vetess/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)
- Item-level findings from the review's evidence table (individual studies, not all attributed in the extracted text): "No differences in scores overall; **recall items (15%) showed higher OBE performance**". In another study, group averages did not differ, but "the 2 types of examinations appear to measure different abilities" (Test 1–Test 2 correlation was higher when both were CB than when the second was OB). In another, "large negative correlations between the amount of time devoted to consulting notes and texts and exam score (R = −0.44…)". "OBE takes examiners more thought and skill in designing tests." Medical students did better on OBE than CBE, while practicing physicians showed no difference. — [Durning et al. 2016, Appendix 1](https://blogs.cornell.edu/vetess/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)
- Block (cited in Durning): learners expecting a CBE performed 10% better on a later test than those expecting an OBE. — [Durning et al. 2016](https://blogs.cornell.edu/vetess/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)

**Larwin, Gorman & Larwin (2013), Educational Psychology Review (meta-analysis)**
- Testing aids had a moderate effect on post-secondary exam performance: student-prepared aids (crib sheets) d = .402 and open-textbook exams d = 0.257. — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-013-9227-1); [PubPeer](https://pubpeer.com/publications/798F794F4DC8A54E45BA44A3ED4647)
- Conflicting newer evidence: a 2025 meta-analysis (Permzadian & Cho) found no significant overall format effect on learning (k = 44, N = 3,499, g = −0.22). — [Scholars' Mine (Missouri S&T)](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/psysci_facwork/353) (secondary snippet; full text not opened)

**Heijne-Penninga et al. (2008), Medical Education 42(10), 967–974**
- With Year 2 (n = 423) and Year 3 (n = 306) medical students, the hypothesis that OB tests stimulate deep learning more than CB tests was *not supported*. CB tests stimulated a deep learning approach more (Deep Information Processing questionnaire). In the curriculum studied, OB tests focused on knowledge application and CB tests on recall and application of basic knowledge. A 2011 follow-up found a negative relation between deep learning and OB test time in second-year students. — [Scilit record](https://www.scilit.net/publications/240fa6eca914ae71f84c41d6aa9b91f7); [Med Teach 2011 follow-up](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.3109/0142159X.2011.530315)

**Sedlmayr & Weissenbacher (2025), "Reading comprehension assessment for student selection: advantages of text availability in terms of validity," Frontiers in Education 10:1524561 (brief research report)**
- Within-subject design, N = 107 university students. Four 500–600-word education texts and 88 items answered "Right / Wrong / Not in the text", with varying inference demands. Two texts had the text available while answering; two had it removed. Order was confounded with condition. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1524561/full)
- Item difficulty was the same (.70 in both conditions). Item-total r was .31 (available) vs .36 (removed); internal consistency .65 vs .72. Cross-condition correlation was only r = .34 after item selection, suggesting the two formats measure different things. Validity: with the text available, scores correlated with verbal intelligence (r = .24) and language grades (r = −.26, where lower grades are better). With the text removed, no validity measure correlated significantly. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1524561/full)
- Prior work cited there: Ozuru et al. (2007), text availability changes which aspect of comprehension is measured; Ferrer et al. (2017), removing the text induces careful, repeated reading to memorize; Reed et al. (2019), available text allows switching and selective search; Schaffner & Schiefele (2013), stronger intelligence correlations in the text-*unavailable* condition (middle-school students, between-subjects). That last result conflicts with Sedlmayr & Weissenbacher. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1524561/full)

### Inferences
- Across these studies, open access mostly inflates scores on items whose answer is a locatable sentence (recall items, verbatim "why" questions answerable by quoting). For LLM readers this inflation is total: any item answerable by locating and paraphrasing one passage gives no information about understanding.
- The negative correlation between lookup time and score (humans) suggests that in an OB setting, needing to search is itself a symptom of weak understanding. An LLM analogue might be heavy quote-copying or citing passages that do not support the claim. Rubrics could penalize answers that restate text without the inferential step.
- Item-writing heuristics consistent with the evidence. These are inferences or advice, not tested for LLMs:
  - (a) The answer should require combining two or more separated parts of the paper (method and result, ablation and claim).
  - (b) Pose a condition the paper does not state, such as a counterfactual setup, new model or distribution, or changed hyperparameter, and ask for a prediction plus mechanism.
  - (c) Ask which stated conclusion would fail under scenario X and why.
  - (d) Include a "not determinable from the paper" option or rubric credit, following the Right/Wrong/Not-in-text format.
  - (e) Score the reasoning tier separately (two-tier logic).
- Durning's point that OB advantages may need different item formats means much of the human OB-vs-CB literature compared the *same* (often recall-heavy MCQ) items across formats. It therefore says little about well-designed OB items.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study that directly compares OB vs CB performance broken down by item cognitive level beyond the one Durning table entry (recall items favoured OB). I could not identify that study's author from the extracted table.
- I did not find empirical item-writing studies demonstrating "lookup-proof" OB question formats. Existing guidance is mostly teaching advice.
- Theophilides & Dionysiou and Krarup et al. (1974) are cited in Durning but I did not extract their item-level findings.

## 5. "Explain why", self-explanation, prediction and "what-if" questions as measures of understanding

### Takeaway
The self-explanation literature (Chi et al. 1989, 1994) shows that generating explanations during reading is associated with, and when prompted causes, better mental models. Those models were detected with items that required inference beyond the text and with mental-model analysis of explanation protocols. The evidence covers explanation prompts as *learning* interventions more than as *assessment* items. Their value as measures rests on the CI-model logic that only a situation model supports correct implicit-function, prediction and transfer answers.

### Cited Findings
- Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser (1989), *Cognitive Science* 13(2), 145–182: students who produced many self-explanations learned with greater understanding than low explainers. The finding is correlational. — [ASU summary](https://education.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/self-explanation_effect.pdf); [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zXnZ6uskmpYYVwxcij9upCF)
- Chi, De Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994), *Cognitive Science* 18(3), 439–477: a controlled experiment. One group was prompted to self-explain after every sentence of a circulatory-system text; the control group read it twice to equalize time. The prompted group showed greater gains, particularly on complex questions requiring knowledge the text did not state explicitly (e.g. inducing a component's function). High explainers all reached the correct mental model and "rarely referred to the text on post-test questions." — [Matuschak notes on Chi 1994](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Chi_et_al._-_1994_-_Eliciting_self-explanations_improves_understanding); [NTNU record](https://scholar.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/zh/publications/eliciting-self-explanations-improves-understanding-2/)
- UbD's explanation facet uses "Why is this so?" as its canonical prompt (advice). — [Duke handout](https://sites.duke.edu/aagc/files/2022/08/C9-SixFacetsUnderstanding.pdf)
- SOLO places "predict" and "hypothesise" at the extended-abstract (highest) level (advice). — [CUHK](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/policy/assessment/SOLO-description.pdf)
- The Agarwal et al. (2008) materials show that a "why" question is not inherently deep. Their "Why did the Voyager have instruments…?" item was answerable from one passage sentence, and open-book scores were higher on it. — [Agarwal et al. 2008](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2008/2008_Agarwal_etal_ACP.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Explain why" items test understanding only when the explanation is not written in the text. If the paper states the reason, the item is a textbase/lookup item. Good "why" items for papers ask why a result follows from the method when the paper leaves that implicit, or why an alternative explanation is ruled out by a particular control.
- Prediction and what-if items (counterfactual: "if the reward model had been trained on X, what would happen to result Y?") match the "inducing function not explicitly stated" category in Chi 1994 and the problem-solving/transfer category in the CI literature. They are the strongest candidates for discriminating situation models in LLM readers.
- For LLMs, fluent self-explanation can be produced without a correct model (confabulation). Rubrics should check that explanation steps are anchored in paper-specific facts and reach the correct prediction, rather than reward explanation length or fluency.
