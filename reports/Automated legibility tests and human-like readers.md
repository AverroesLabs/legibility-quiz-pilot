# No one has validated a legibility quiz

No one has built an automated legibility test for research papers that has been validated against measured human understanding. No one has made a language model fail at reading the way humans do at the level of a whole document either. The two questions have a shared answer: both are open problems, and our pilot is already working on the most direct version of them. The closest existing systems each miss in a different way. greCAPTCHA uses an LLM-written quiz on real manuscripts, but it tests whether a *person* understands a paper, not whether the paper is legible. KnowledgeGain's simulated reader matches human answer distributions in aggregate, but correlates only about 0.26 with humans per topic, and only on science-news abstracts. OpenAI's prover-verifier work showed that legibility to a small model carried over to humans, but the evidence is the direction of change in 45-second checks of grade-school math. LLM readability judges agree moderately with human *ratings* (r ≈ 0.5–0.76) but are nearly uncorrelated with what readers actually learn. They can also be gamed by presentation-only rewrites. On making models fail like humans, the evidence points one way: weaker models, bounded memory and calibration to human data help, while telling a strong model to act like a novice does not. The pilot fits the literature closely: models' ratings collapse on obfuscated text while their quiz scores barely move. One finding changes how to read our null result. **In humans too, clearer writing moves perceived readability far more than measured comprehension**, so some of the pilot's small obfuscation effect may be realistic rather than a model artifact. The most promising path is a behavioural quiz taken by deliberately *bounded* weak readers, compared within-item across text versions, and calibrated against human data. Existing datasets can supply that human data before we pay for our own.

## No existing test measures human understanding of papers at scale

The search turned up four families of near-misses. None of them meets all three requirements: research papers, validation against human comprehension, and automation at scale.

**greCAPTCHA is structurally the closest system to ours, but it points the other way.** GPT-5.6 Sol writes 8 questions per manuscript with rubrics, in four families: planted-error, unstated rationale, background knowledge and failure mode. An automated grader scores the answers. In a study of 31 researchers it separated authors from non-authors with **AUC 0.90**. The planted-error multiple-choice family alone reached only AUC 0.595, because it was largely Ctrl-F-searchable, and dropping it raised AUC to 0.934 ([greCAPTCHA](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20481)). Two details matter for us. One non-author scored 50.6 on an unfamiliar paper after about 5 minutes of preparation, against 53.1 on their own paper, so open-book quizzes partly measure retrieval. Participants also called the grader overly rigid. No grader-human agreement figure appeared in the text that was read. Most important, greCAPTCHA scores the *reader*. Its non-author condition is the nearest human data point to our construct, "is this document legible to a non-author?", but nobody has turned it around to score documents. Comprehension Audits (Bodkin, Sokhansanj, Hadfield) is the governance counterpart: 90-minute Socratic oral audits of lab staff. It is a proposal, the authors say its rubrics are untested, and it uses no LLM-generated quiz ([Comprehension Audits](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10064v1)).

**QA-based measures are the only automated family with any evidence of tracking human comprehension, and the effects are small.** In Guo et al.'s crowdsourced study of plain-language summaries, QAEval was the only one of 10 automatic metrics significantly associated with human multiple-choice comprehension on the rigorous subset (N = 459). Its effect was **β = 0.039 per standard deviation**, p = .035 ([Guo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10409v1)). QAEval checks whether a QA model can extract answers from the summary, so it is close to our quiz. KnowledgeGain goes further. It ran a pre/post quiz on 30 STEM undergraduates and built LLMSim, a simulated reader that matched aggregate human answer distributions closely (KL 0.007–0.12). But the authors report that simulated and human gains are "only weakly correlated" per topic (0.263), and that **"LLMSim does not reliably predict topic-level KGain"** ([KnowledgeGain](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31099)). It works as a development proxy, not as a per-document gate, and its texts are abstracts, news stories and tweets, not papers.

**Prover-verifier games give the only human transfer result for legibility training, but on a narrow construct.** Kirchner et al. trained provers to produce grade-school math solutions that a small verifier could check. Contractors judging the solutions under a 45-second limit became more accurate on helpful-prover output across training rounds, and less accurate on sneaky-prover output ([Kirchner et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.13692v2)). The authors call the transfer "a surprising empirical finding". No verifier-vs-human correlation is reported, exact values exist only in figures, and the sneaky-prover human results come from a pilot campaign the authors flag as possibly confounded. Verifier size mattered. The stable range was 10^-3 to 10^-1.8 of prover compute, and verifiers the same size as the prover did not produce legible provers. The 2026 decoupled "translator" follow-up removes the accuracy cost on paper but ran **no human evaluation** ([Kim & Lee 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23248v1)). Roytburg et al.'s "transfer utility" is the closest analogue to our weak-reader design: how much a weak model gains from prefixes of a strong model's reasoning trace. It is validated only against weak-model monitoring, and the authors state that legibility to humans was not measured ([Roytburg et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20508v2)). One of its results bears directly on research papers. On knowledge-heavy GPQA, **no signal survived length control**, which the authors attribute to knowledge that small models lack.

**AI-scientist venues have not measured human legibility.** Agents4Science 2025 used three LLM reviewers whose scores included clarity, with human experts assessing only the top submissions ([Agents4Science](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15534)). No human-comprehension measure was reported. Those details were seen only through a search summary of the paper. The gradual-disempowerment literature names loss of human understanding as a safety risk but offers no metric ([Kulveit et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946)). Our project would fill a gap that several groups describe and none has addressed.

## LLM ratings track human perceptions, not human understanding

Our pilot found that model legibility ratings collapsed on obfuscated text (Gemma 9.7 to 2.9) without tracking quiz scores. The literature explains why. LLM readability judges outperform classic formulas, but only against human *ratings* of reading ease. On 60 scientific summaries, LLM judges reached Pearson 0.45–0.56 with MTurk ratings, against at most 0.37 for any formula ([Cachola et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.19221v1)). On the general-English CLEAR corpus, GPT-4-class ratings reached **r = 0.74–0.76**, and CLEAR's own split-half reliability is only 0.63 ([Trott & Riviere 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.14028v1)). Human ratings themselves rest on surface vocabulary: "Wording/Terminology" was the most common justification annotators gave ([Belem et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15345v1)).

Where comprehension was measured directly, the judges failed. In KnowledgeGain, an LLM judge's *clarity* score correlated with measured human learning at **Spearman 0.089**. Human-rated clarity was flat between two systems (4.08 vs 4.06) even though comprehension differed ([KnowledgeGain](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31099)). Guo et al. found LLM-written plain-language summaries rated as good as human-written ones, while readers understood the human-written ones significantly better ([Guo et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10409v1)). Our measured result is that ratings and quiz scores diverge. Our inference is that the ratings are doing what they were built to do, which is to predict perceived difficulty. They should not be expected to predict understanding.

Ratings also fail as a gate for papers written by optimizing AI researchers. Presentation-only revisions, changing the abstract, framing and discussion but not methods or results, **raised AI-reviewer scores in 75.1% of attempts, by +1.21 on a 10-point scale** ([Yang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13044)). The same paper's figure of r = .58–.61 between AI-reviewer and human scores was seen only in a snippet. Reported LLM-human agreement on clarity scores in peer review is moderate at best: ICC ≈ 0.45–0.60 on abstract clarity, also snippet-only ([arXiv 2601.19925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19925v1)). One agent-reviewer paper claims "high" presentation correlation, but its numbers appear only in a figure ([GAR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.10415)). Even the human side of that agreement is a perception score, not a comprehension measure.

## Models fail like humans only when handicapped and calibrated

Nobody has made an LLM reproduce human comprehension failures on long documents. The partial successes share a pattern. Each worked by **limiting the model** or **fitting it to human data**, never by prompting a strong model to pretend.

The strongest simulation result is in math, not reading. Open-source LLMs role-played a classroom of 4th-, 8th- and 12th-graders, and an IRT model fit to their answers predicted NAEP item difficulty at **r = 0.75–0.82**. Asking a model to judge difficulty directly worked poorly. **Gemma, the weaker model at math, predicted human difficulty better than the stronger Llama and Qwen** ([Acquaye et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09953)). A 20-model study of medical and math items found that models "struggle to simulate the capability limitations of students even when being explicitly prompted to adopt specific proficiency levels" and that "high performance often impedes accurate difficulty estimation" ([arXiv 2512.18880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18880)). Reading comprehension is worse. Across 42 LLMs, the best response-based signal for human item discrimination was a rank correlation of **0.231**, and it came from differences between models, not from proficiency prompts ([Chen et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18709)). Benedetto et al. report only a "small but positive" correlation for reading pretesting; that wording comes from a snippet, and no coefficient was seen ([Benedetto et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.663/)). A further r = 0.35 for reading and science exams is snippet-only and unverified ([SMART](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1274.pdf)). The one high reading-adjacent figure, r = 0.93, came from **fine-tuning on real student responses**, and it is a correlation of whole-test scores, not of items ([Zelikman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06837)).

Models are insensitive to text organization in a way humans are not. Reading-comprehension models kept **95.4% of their performance with sentences shuffled** across 10 datasets ([Sugawara et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.09241)). Unconstrained LMs "rely excessively on context information, resulting in superhuman predictions" ([Kuribayashi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01615)), and larger models fit human reading times *worse* ([Oh & Schuler 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.tacl-1.20/)). Bounding memory reverses some of this, but so far only at the level of words and sentences. LMs with restricted memory or dual-task load approximate human reading costs and strategies better ([arXiv 2604.26351](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26351v1)). Lossy-context surprisal, GPT-2 with a resource-rational memory limit, predicts nested-structure difficulty in humans that neither surprisal nor memory locality predicts alone ([Hahn et al. 2022](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2217108119)). A 2026 hierarchical resource-rational reader claims to reproduce findings "from lexical effects to comprehension outcomes", but its abstract gives no numbers ([Bai et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06006)). The only document-level simulator that came close to humans, LLMSim, used a **memory bottleneck**: it writes a short gist and answers from that, not from the full text. It also used empirically fitted personas, an explicit "I don't know" option, and tuning to human answer distributions ([KnowledgeGain](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31099)).

For the pilot, the inference is direct. Our readers had the whole obfuscated paper in context with unlimited re-reading. That is exactly the condition under which models ignore disorder, because they can attend straight to the relevant span wherever it sits. A human pays for reordering and undefined abbreviations through working memory and integration, and our readers never had to. Model knowledge adds a second effect. An LLM reads like a *high-knowledge* human reader, and in humans high-knowledge readers gain little from added cohesion, and on deep measures are sometimes hurt by it ([Ozuru et al. 2005](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0tw744hb)). That may explain why making the key insight implicit moved nothing: the readers can rebuild it from their priors. Transfer utility's GPQA null points the same way. Once reading draws on domain knowledge, weak-reader scores stop tracking legibility. These are inferences. No study has run humans and LLMs on the same passages with cohesion or order manipulated.

## Human prose effects are small too, so our null may be partly real

The pilot found a 2–9 point obfuscation effect and called it a near-null. Human research on writing quality suggests the human effect on *accuracy* may not be much bigger, while the effect on *perceived* readability is large. In the only controlled study of real science abstracts rewritten at four difficulty levels with content held constant, readability ratings fell from **66.3 to 44.4**. Understanding moved little and non-monotonically: 57.9%, 39.5%, 40.8% and 48.0% correct from easiest to hardest, an odds ratio of 1.08 per level. Writing level explained **marginal R² = 0.053 of understanding versus 0.244 of readability** ([Ryba (formerly Freeling) et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.714321/full)). That study had only 2 questions per abstract, so the accuracy estimates are noisy. In Ozuru et al., a cohesion revision that made biology texts 50% longer moved text-based accuracy from .20 to .22 in one reader group and from .37 to .45 in the other. It had no effect on inference questions, while domain knowledge had a large main effect (F = 31.66) ([Ozuru et al. 2005](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0tw744hb)). Only 6 of 15 enhanced consent-form trials improved understanding ([Flory & Emanuel 2004](https://vivo.weill.cornell.edu/display/pubid15467062)). The largest effects appear when the original text is genuinely broken, as in Britton and Gulgoz's repair of missing inferences, where recall rose markedly; only the abstract was available, so no effect size is given ([Britton & Gulgoz 1991](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/britton-glgz-usingkintschscomputationalmodeltoimproveinstructionaltexteffectsofrepairinginferencecallsonrecallandcognitivestructures-1991)).

Read against this, the pilot's pattern is partly human-like. Ratings dropped steeply while accuracy barely moved, which is the same pattern as Ryba et al.'s. The difference is that our obfuscation was presumably more severe than Ryba et al.'s rewrites, and we have no human number for it. **Without a human baseline we cannot tell whether a 2–9 point drop is too small, about right, or too large.** That is the central unknown. The follow-up results support the inference that the quiz measures information availability. Removing key results dropped scores sharply, as QAEval-style measures would predict. Making insights implicit moved nothing, as a high-knowledge reader would predict. Neither result tests legibility to humans.

## The most promising path is bounded readers calibrated on human data

Nothing in the literature solves the problem. The pieces that do exist point toward one design. **Score documents behaviourally**, by quiz performance and not by ratings. Use **weak readers bounded in memory and re-reading**, not persona prompts. **Compare the same items across text versions** so that question difficulty cancels out. **Calibrate against human data** before trusting any threshold. Each element has some support: behaviour over ratings (KnowledgeGain, Acquaye), weakness and bounds (Kirchner's verifier sizes, Acquaye's Gemma result, LLMSim's bottleneck, the constrained-memory literature), within-item comparison (Sugawara 2022 found readability features do not predict human question difficulty ([arXiv 2203.06342](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06342), snippet-level)), and human calibration (Zelikman's r = 0.93 came only after fine-tuning on human responses). No one has assembled them. The risk is that bounded readers become sensitive to obfuscation in ways that do not match humans, for example by failing on long-range references that humans handle with a quick look back. Only human data can settle that. A second risk is gaming. An optimizing AI author will learn whatever the reader rewards, as the presentation-only attack shows, so the test needs a sneaky-writer red team like the sneaky prover in prover-verifier games.

Cheap human ground truth already exists for the first calibration step. **OneStopQA** asks identical questions about original (Advanced) and teacher-simplified (Elementary) Guardian paragraphs, and the OneStop eye-tracking corpus has answers from 360 readers on OSF ([OneStop](https://lacclab.github.io/OneStop-Eye-Movements); [STARC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14797v1)). Per-level human accuracy was not extracted and should be in the OSF data; the pooled 81.2% figure is snippet-only. **QuALITY** labels questions on 2k–8k-token passages as HARD when most annotators with 45 seconds got them wrong, against 93.5% untimed human accuracy ([QuALITY](https://aclanthology.org/2022.naacl-main.391.pdf)). That tests whether a bounded reader fails where skimming humans fail. **PoTeC** crosses reader expertise with scientific texts (German) ([PoTeC](https://github.com/DiLi-Lab/PoTeC)). None of these are research papers, but each can answer the first question cheaply: does a bounded weak reader's accuracy move with text difficulty the way human accuracy does? A paper-level human study comes later. A rough extrapolation from QuALITY pay rates, not quoted by any source, gives **$12k–30k** for 10 papers × 2 versions × 15 screened graduate readers. Short-survey respondents on Prolific cost about $1.90 each ([Douglas et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36917576)), so a Ryba-style study on paper sections would cost far less.

## Conclusion

The literature changes what our pilot's null means. The pilot asked whether weak LLM readers are hurt by bad prose the way humans are. The evidence suggests that humans' *accuracy* is hurt less than assumed, while their *perceptions* are hurt a lot, and that models already copy the perception side. So the real gap is not that models are immune to bad writing. Their working conditions remove the costs humans pay, namely limited memory, linear reading and missing background knowledge, and nobody knows how large the human accuracy cost is for papers like ours. That makes the next experiments clear. Impose the human costs on the reader, and anchor the effect size to human data. Start with existing datasets, then a small panel.

A legibility gate for AI researchers also has to survive optimization pressure, which rules out ratings as the scored signal. The likely shape of a scalable test is the prover-verifier one: a weak, bounded reader taking a behavioural quiz, an adversarial writer probing it, and periodic human calibration. Our pilot would be the first to try that on research papers.
