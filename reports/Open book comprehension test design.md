# Ask what the paper implies, not states

An open-book quiz measures understanding only when its items have answers the paper does not state anywhere. The reader has to combine the method, the result and the stated mechanism to get each answer, so no retrieved passage can hold it. With the paper in context, an LLM reader's grasp of the explicit text is effectively perfect. Any item answerable by finding and paraphrasing one or a few passages therefore measures retrieval, which is exactly what the pilot found. The best-supported design draws on four sources: greCAPTCHA's free-response "failure mode" and "unstated rationale" families (AUC 0.934 for telling authors from strangers once multiple-choice questions were removed), ROPES-style minimal-pair application items, contrast-set consistency scoring, and two-tier rubrics that score the conclusion and the reasoning separately. A validation battery should screen every item. It should drop items that a no-paper reader or a top-k-retrieval "skim" reader can pass, and items that a careful full-paper reader cannot. Grading should use 2–3 independent binary rubric points scored by a grader from a different model family, calibrated against about 50–100 human-graded answers. Expect to need roughly 30–70 items per reader to detect a 10–15 point legibility effect. Much of the evidence is thin. The theory of what separates understanding from retrieval comes from human reading research. The question-type advice comes mostly from assessment frameworks and benchmark builders. No study we found tests these item types on weaker LLM readers taking an open-book quiz, so the pilot pipeline itself has to supply that validation.

## The pilot failures are what reading research predicts

Kintsch's construction-integration model splits comprehension into two layers. The *textbase* is the propositions the text states. The *situation model* is that content integrated into a working mental model. Recall and questions about explicit content measure the textbase. Inference, problem-solving and transfer tasks measure the situation model ([Long et al. 2006](https://sites.uw.edu/ccdl/files//2013/12/Long_Assessing-text_2006.pdf); [Kintsch 1994](https://www.scribd.com/document/584304292/Kin-Tsch)). The two kinds of measure can move in opposite directions. McNamara & Kintsch found that **a highly coherent text improved recall and multiple-choice scores, while high-knowledge readers did better on open-ended inference questions after reading a low-coherence text** ([McNamara & Kintsch 1996](https://scilit.com/publications/74c26bef2b7c9f5a1f1ef4c44095b868)). Later work did not consistently replicate the interaction ([Utrecht](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/coherence-marking-prior-knowledge-and-comprehension-of-informativ/)). All of this is human evidence.

Human open-book studies show the same pattern. Having the passage available raised initial short-answer scores from .70 to .82 (d = 1.12) ([Agarwal et al. 2008](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2008/2008_Agarwal_etal_ACP.pdf)). In one study in a systematic review, **recall items were the only ones where open-book performance was higher**. Other studies in the review found that time spent consulting notes correlated negatively with score (R = −0.44) ([Durning et al. 2016](https://blogs.cornell.edu/vetess/files/2020/07/Durning-2016-Comparing-Open-Book-and-Closed-Book-Examinations.pdf)). The same review states the rationale for open-book exams: they free the examiner to test "what cannot simply be 'looked up'". It also concedes that most studies reused recall-heavy multiple-choice items and so never tested that rationale. Agarwal et al. also show that a "why" question can be shallow. Their "Why did the Voyager have instruments…?" item was answerable from a single sentence of the passage. QuALITY's builders gave the operating rule: if a reader "can quickly identify the answer… through skimming or ctrl-F-style… search, then the question does not require broader understanding of the passage" ([Pang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08608)).

For the pilot, this means two things. First, the kept questions were almost certainly textbase items. A smart reader answering most of them from 3 keyword-retrieved paragraphs is direct evidence of that. Second, the legibility null result is uninterpretable until the items are fixed. A strong reader can pull facts out of awkward prose, so textbase items are the ones least likely to register a legibility manipulation. The reverse-cohesion finding also warns that "harder to read" does not reliably mean "harder to understand." Our inference is that the legibility test can only be read once items pass a retrieval filter and the evidence they need sits in the rewritten section. It remains possible that legibility simply matters little to LLM readers. A retrieval-proof item set is the only way to tell these explanations apart.

## Seven item types force a situation model; multiple choice does not

The best direct evidence comes from greCAPTCHA. Thirty-one researchers took 8 questions per paper in 15 minutes and could search the paper. Overall, the questions told authors from non-authors with AUC 0.90. **Planted-error multiple-choice questions alone reached only AUC 0.595, versus 0.934 for the free-response families without them** ([greCAPTCHA](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20481v1)). Choices-only studies explain why. LLMs beat majority baselines with both passage and question removed, by eliminating distractors and reconstructing a plausible context ([NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/126714)). On QuALITY, a question-only model scored 43.3% where chance is 25% ([Pang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08608)). Default to short free responses.

| Item type | Template (strong LLM fills the slots) | Why lookup fails | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Failure mode / edge case** | "Describe a realistic condition under which [main result R] would no longer hold, and explain the mechanism from the paper that makes it fail." | The paper does not list the condition, so the reader must know which component carries the result | Human-tested on researchers ([greCAPTCHA](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20481v1)); Bloom "Checking/Critiquing" is advice ([Krathwohl 2002](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)) |

| **Transfer to a new case** | "Lab X applies [method] to [new model, threat model or domain] with property P. | **Cross-section integration** | "Which result in [Section A] would be undermined if [ablation or control in Section B] had come out the other way, and why?" | The evidence sits in distant sections | Benchmark ([ELAIPBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10549); [NovelQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12766v3) multi-hop is hardest) |

| **Scope / "not determinable"** | "Does the paper's evidence support claim Q′ (a plausible over-generalization)? Answer supported, contradicted or not settled, and say what evidence would settle it." | The reader must know the boundary of the claims | Human-tested format "Right/Wrong/Not in the text" ([Sedlmayr & Weissenbacher 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1524561/full)); "I don't know" option ([KnowledgeGain](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31099v1)) |

Two constraints keep these items from demanding more intelligence than understanding the paper needs. First, **every rubric answer must follow from mechanisms, ablations or numbers the paper states**. An item whose answer needs new research is an open problem and cannot be graded. Second, items should hinge on paper-specific details so that field knowledge alone fails. LLMs often fall back on parametric knowledge instead of in-context counterfactual premises ([ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15732v1)). Readers given the full text also score high on well-known texts: GPT-4 reached 60.94% multiple-choice accuracy on NovelQA with no novel at all ([NovelQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12766v3)). The frameworks agree on which mental operations to target: Bloom's Inferring and Explaining, SOLO's relational and extended-abstract levels, and the UbD facets of explanation and application. These frameworks are design heuristics, and **no controlled study shows that items written at higher levels measure understanding better** ([CUHK SOLO](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/policy/assessment/SOLO-description.pdf); [SERC on UbD](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/1008.html)).

## Generate from a claim map, one aspect per question

Whole-paper understanding is a coverage property of the quiz as well as of each item. Savaal's concept-first pipeline was the only generation method we found that was validated by experts, with the papers' own authors acting as raters. It extracts main ideas per section, merges and ranks them, retrieves supporting passages, and then writes questions. On 142-page dissertations it cut "doesn't test understanding" questions from 29.0% to 11.9%. **On conference papers the gain was small: 16.7% down to 10.9%** ([Savaal](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12477v2)). For 10–30 page safety papers, use the concept step mainly to build a *claim map*: the main claims, the method components and results that support each one, and the stated conditions. Then allocate items so that methods, key results and conclusions are each covered, and so that most items link at least two map nodes. Krathwohl's taxonomy table (knowledge type × cognitive process) is the standard way to spot over-represented and empty cells in such a blueprint. That is advice, not tested evidence ([Krathwohl 2002](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)). Counterfactual items should be built from claim-map nodes that state a mechanism. IfQA found that **pre-selecting passages suited to counterfactuals and showing writers 5 examples substantially improved item quality** ([IfQA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14010)). That was human writers on Wikipedia.

Keep the generator prompt short and give it one worked example per item type. In Scaria et al., chain-of-thought plus one example per level gave 82.6% high-quality questions. Adding everything dropped that to 74.7%. Even GPT-4 hit its intended Bloom level only about two-thirds of the time ([Scaria et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04394v1)). So label item types after generation by checking them, not by trusting the prompt. greCAPTCHA participants asked for **one aspect per question, an explicit statement of the expected detail, and rubric items signalled in the question**. They also found the grader too rigid about equivalent answers ([greCAPTCHA](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20481v1)). Have the generator output, with each item, the rubric points and the paper spans that justify each point. Recorded spans make two later checks possible: confirming the item is answerable, and measuring how spread out the needed evidence is. Spans from two or more sections are a usable proxy for whole-paper dependence ([ELAIPBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10549)). Do not use an LLM judge's holistic quality rating as the filter. Savaal's GPT-4o judge rarely rated anything negatively, and DSPy tuning did not fix it. Scaria's Gemini judge disagreed with the experts about which generator was best ([Savaal](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12477v2); [Scaria et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04394v1)). Behavioural checks on readers, described next, are more trustworthy than a judge's opinion.

## A six-check battery turns the pilot's failure into a filter

The pilot's diagnostic, a capable reader given 3 keyword-retrieved paragraphs, is a rejection filter. It is the LLM version of QuALITY's speed validation, in which annotators got 40 seconds of skimming or ctrl-F and items were kept only if most skimmers failed while careful untimed readers succeeded. **Under that protocol 49.9% of retained questions were HARD, and every model also scored lower on them** ([Pang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08608)). ELAIPBench supplies a cheap LLM version: a question is revised if any of three small LLMs answers it correctly with the paper. On the resulting items, **intra-paper retrieval-augmented generation lowered accuracy** and the best model scored about 40% ([ELAIPBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10549)). That is evidence that items written against retrieval do resist chunk retrieval. QuALITY's question-only score of 43.3% shows that the difficulty filter and the leakage filter are separate checks, so both are needed.

| Check | Run | Keep the item if | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. No-paper baseline | Each ladder reader, question only | Score is near floor; otherwise it tests priors | [NovelQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12766v3); [KnowledgeGain](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31099v1) gain = (Post−Pre)/(1−Pre) |
| 2. Skim/retrieval proxy | A reader at the target ladder's strength, given top-k (k≈3) retrieved chunks or only the abstract | Fails, or earns at most 1 of 3 points | [QuALITY](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08608); [ELAIPBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10549) |
| 3. Full-paper answerability | Strongest reader plus the recorded evidence spans; ideally a human spot-check | Passes; spans come from 2 or more sections | [ELAIPBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10549) evidence verification |
| 4. Contrast consistency | Minimal-pair twins | Strong readers get both twins right; weak readers often get only one | [Gardner et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.117/) |
| 5. Ladder discrimination | All rungs, K samples each | Item score rises with reader strength (upper-minus-lower D ≥ ~0.3) and is not at ceiling or floor (p ≈ 0.25–0.75) | [Ebel bands](https://metricgate.com/blogs/item-difficulty-vs-discrimination/); [UF](https://teach.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Module-4c_-Analyzing-Item-Difficulty-and-Discrimination.pdf) |
| 6. Manipulation sensitivity | Clear vs. hard-to-read version | p(clear) − p(hard) > 0 for items whose evidence sits in the rewritten section | PPDI, [Naumann et al. 2014](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2020/18997/pdf/Naumann_et_al_2014_Modeling_instructional_sensitivity_using_LML-DIF_A.pdf) |

Three cautions apply to the battery. First, the skim proxy in check 2 should be a reader about as strong as the target readers, not the strongest model. If a very smart model passes from three paragraphs by out-reasoning the gap, the item demands intelligence rather than understanding. If a target-strength reader passes, the item demands only retrieval. Both should be rejected, but for different reasons. This calibration is our inference, and no source tests it. Second, the Ebel bands come from large human cohorts. **With about 6 readers, a point-biserial has a standard error near 0.45**, so check 5 is for screening only. IRT is out of reach: stable 2PL estimates need hundreds to about 1,000 respondents ([irtsim](https://cloud.r-project.org/web/packages/irtsim/vignettes/irtsim.html); [REID 2024](https://scholarhub.uny.ac.id/reid/vol10/iss1/8)), and tinyBenchmarks calibrated on 395 models ([Polo et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14992)). Third, the sensitivity index in check 6 (PPDI, the pass-rate difference between conditions) is cleaner here than in classrooms because the manipulation is experimental. It still disagreed with DIF-based indices in the original literature ([Naumann et al. 2014](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2020/18997/pdf/Naumann_et_al_2014_Modeling_instructional_sensitivity_using_LML-DIF_A.pdf)). A logistic model with an item × condition interaction separates sensitivity to the manipulation from sensitivity to reader ability. An optional seventh check catches readers who answer from memory of a published paper: edit one key result in the reader's copy and see whether the answers follow the edit. This is an inference drawn from contrast-set and counterfactual-dataset logic.

## Grade with binary, two-tier rubric points from a different-family grader

Keep every grading decision binary and get partial credit by summing points. In ResearchRubrics, **moving rubric items from ternary to binary grading raised human–LLM-judge agreement by about 20 percentage points** ([ResearchRubrics](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07685)). Savaal's human raters show the same effect: agreement was ρ = 0.32 on raw scores and ρ = 0.76 once binarized ([Savaal](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12477v2)). Structure the 2–3 points as a free-response two-tier item. Point 1 is the correct prediction or conclusion. Point 2 is the correct mechanism, tied to a named paper fact. An optional point 3 is the boundary or condition. Treagust's two-tier scoring gives full credit only when both answer and reason are right, and Rasch analyses treat the reason tier as a related but harder trait ([core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/195657903.pdf); [Curtin](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/43349?show=full)). That evidence comes from human science education. The four answer/reason combinations double as a diagnostic, and **"right answer, wrong reason" is the signature of surface retrieval**, so track its rate per condition. Phrase each point as a claim the answer must assert or entail ("states that C′ lowers M *because* …"), not "mentions X". List acceptable paraphrases and named near-misses, and score 0 when the rest of the answer contradicts the credited claim. These rubric-writing rules are advice. Fluent explanations from an LLM can be confabulated, so require the grader to quote the span that earns each point.

LLM graders are good but not human-level. On binary rubric items they reach κ ≈ 0.64–0.76 against humans, compared with a human–human ceiling of about 0.82 ([BLUEX v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22723); [step-wise rubric study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17291)). Depending on the task they can lean lenient or strict, so calibrate per task. Self-preference is causal and rises with self-recognition ([Panickssery et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)), so use a grader from a different family than the readers, or average two graders. Build a calibration set of about 50–100 human-graded answers spread across rungs and both conditions. Hold it to the automated-scoring standard of κ/QWK ≥ 0.70, machine–human agreement no more than 0.10 below human–human, and a standardized mean difference of at most 0.15 ([Williamson et al. via ACT](https://www.act.org/content/dam/act/unsecured/documents/R2304-CRASE-Essay-Scoring-Model-Performance-02-2023.pdf)). The 50–100 figure is our recommendation, not sourced. For this experiment, one check matters more than overall κ: **grader error must not correlate with condition**. A grader that is more lenient toward answers written from the clear version would manufacture a legibility effect. Many rubric points will have extreme pass rates among strong readers, which makes Cohen's κ unstable, so report raw agreement and Gwet's AC1 alongside it. Cap answer length, because judges show verbosity bias, and in recent work a conciseness bias ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685); [Judging the Judges](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23178)).

For sample size, Miller's eval formula gives n = (z_{α/2}+z_β)²(ω² + σ²_A/K_A + σ²_B/K_B)/δ² ([Miller 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)). Here ω² is the between-item variance of the per-item difference between conditions, and σ²/K is the sampling noise left after K samples per item. With assumed values of ω ≈ 0.3, α = 0.05 and 80% power, **detecting a 10-point legibility effect needs about 70 items per reader, and a 15-point effect about 30**. Estimate ω from the pilot before committing. Resampling saturates quickly: most of the variance reduction comes by K ≈ 4–8, so **K = 3–5 samples per item per condition**, with re-grading 2–3 times, is the sensible range. Adding items beats adding samples. Treat the item as the unit of analysis and cluster standard errors by paper. To get the whole-paper score the user wants, take each reader's mean rubric score over the full blueprint for a paper. Fit score ~ condition + (1|item) + (1|reader) + (condition|reader) to pool rungs and test whether the legibility effect changes with reader strength. Report the score as gain over the no-paper baseline. At 8–10 items per paper, several papers are needed to reach these totals.

## Conclusion

The pilot has already shown the central problem: an LLM reader with the paper in context has a perfect textbase, so open-book quiz design is entirely about writing items whose answer is in no passage. That turns the work from item writing into a filtering problem. Generate many more candidates than you need, about 2–3× per paper, from a claim map. Write failure-mode, rationale, minimal-pair and transfer items with mechanism-anchored binary rubrics. Then let the no-paper baseline, the target-strength skim proxy and the full-paper answerability check reject most of them. QuALITY's attrition and ELAIPBench's revise-if-any-weak-model-passes rule suggest heavy rejection is normal. Only items that survive can say anything about legibility. The pilot's null result should be re-tested on them before anyone concludes that legibility does not matter to LLM readers.

The largest gap is that none of this has been validated on the actual population: weaker LLMs reading AI safety papers open-book. greCAPTCHA's AUC compares human authors with strangers, the open-book and textbase findings come from students, and ROPES and contrast sets come from short passages. The project should therefore treat its own data as the validity study. Store the per-item results for every check, the ladder slopes, the PPDI values and the grader–human agreement by condition. The two most useful unknowns to pin down from the next pilot are ω, which sets how many items are needed, and the pass rate of target-strength readers on retrieval-filtered items, which shows whether the battery can avoid tipping into an intelligence test.
